Differentiate common anemia patterns, identify needed workup and generate a perioperative recommendation

Purpose

Preoperative anemia should be evaluated according to severity, laboratory pattern, chronicity, symptoms and anticipated surgical blood loss. The purpose of the evaluation is to identify potentially treatable causes and reduce the risk of perioperative transfusion and complications without unnecessarily delaying low-blood-loss procedures.

For major joint replacement, multilevel spine fusion, revision surgery and other procedures with meaningful expected blood loss, identifying and treating anemia before surgery may be particularly valuable.
The calculator below is clinical decision support, not a definitive diagnosis. Mixed anemia is common. Ferritin can be falsely normal or elevated during inflammation, infection, liver disease and other inflammatory states.

Iron Deficiency Pattern

Iron deficiency is particularly important before operations with expected blood loss because it is often treatable.

Findings Supporting Iron Deficiency

  • Low ferritin
  • Low serum iron
  • Low transferrin saturation
  • Often elevated TIBC
  • Increasing RDW
  • Microcytosis as deficiency progresses
A ferritin below approximately 30 ng/mL strongly supports depleted iron stores in many clinical situations.
Inflammation can elevate ferritin. Iron deficiency may still be present with a higher ferritin when TSAT is reduced and the clinical context supports functional or mixed iron deficiency.

TSAT Calculation

Transferrin Saturation

TSAT (%) = Serum Iron ÷ TIBC × 100

The calculator automatically computes this when serum iron and TIBC are entered.

A reduced TSAT, commonly below approximately 20%, supports insufficient circulating iron availability when interpreted with ferritin and clinical context.

Anemia of Chronic Disease / Inflammation

Inflammatory disease can restrict iron availability even when total body iron stores are not profoundly depleted.

Test Common Pattern
Serum iron Low
TIBC Low or normal
Ferritin Normal or elevated
TSAT May be low
MCV Often normocytic; may become mildly microcytic
Iron deficiency and inflammatory anemia can coexist. Do not force a patient into one category when laboratory findings suggest a mixed pattern.

CKD-Associated Anemia

CKD commonly causes a normocytic hypoproliferative anemia due in part to inadequate erythropoietin activity and altered iron metabolism.

When eGFR is reduced, consider CKD as a contributor—but still assess iron status because iron deficiency frequently coexists.

Review

  • Creatinine/eGFR trend
  • Ferritin
  • TSAT
  • Reticulocyte response
  • Prior hemoglobin values
  • Nephrology history when advanced CKD is present

Vitamin B12 Deficiency

B12 deficiency classically produces macrocytic anemia but may present without marked macrocytosis, especially in mixed anemia.

Possible Findings

  • High MCV
  • Low B12
  • Neuropathy
  • Paresthesias
  • Gait abnormality
  • Glossitis
Neurologic B12 deficiency should be recognized even when anemia is mild.

Folate Deficiency

Folate deficiency may cause macrocytosis and megaloblastic anemia.

Risk Factors

  • Poor nutritional intake
  • Alcohol exposure
  • Malabsorption
  • Selected medications
  • Increased physiologic requirement

Reticulocyte Count

The reticulocyte count helps determine whether bone marrow is responding appropriately to anemia.

Low/inappropriately normal reticulocyte response
Consider reduced production.

Examples:
Iron deficiency
CKD
Chronic inflammatory disease
B12/folate deficiency
Bone-marrow disease

Elevated reticulocyte response
Consider increased RBC loss or destruction.

Examples:
Acute blood loss
Hemolysis
Recovery after effective treatment

Possible Hemolysis

Laboratory Clues

  • Elevated reticulocyte count
  • Elevated LDH
  • Low haptoglobin
  • Elevated indirect bilirubin
  • Peripheral smear abnormalities
Active or unexplained hemolysis warrants further evaluation and may require hematology consultation before elective surgery.

Blood Loss

Blood loss may be acute, chronic or occult.

Potential Sources

  • Heavy menstrual bleeding
  • GI blood loss
  • Recent surgery
  • Trauma
  • Anticoagulant-associated bleeding
  • Hematuria
  • Other clinically significant bleeding
Iron-deficiency anemia in an adult without an obvious explanation should prompt consideration of the source rather than treatment with iron alone.

Mentzer Index — Selected Microcytic Cases

When microcytosis is present and thalassemia trait is being considered, the Mentzer index can provide supportive—not diagnostic—information.

Mentzer Index

MCV ÷ RBC count

Lower values may support thalassemia trait.
Higher values may support iron deficiency.

This index should never replace ferritin/iron studies or hemoglobinopathy evaluation when clinically indicated.

Oral Iron

Oral iron may be appropriate when deficiency is present, surgery is not imminent and the patient can tolerate and absorb oral therapy.

Consider

  • Time before surgery
  • Severity of anemia
  • GI tolerance
  • Prior response
  • Malabsorption history
  • Expected surgical blood loss

IV Iron

IV iron may be useful when iron deficiency is clinically important and oral therapy is inadequate, poorly tolerated, poorly absorbed or unlikely to provide adequate repletion in the available preoperative interval.

For major joint or spine surgery with significant iron-deficiency anemia and a short interval before surgery, IV iron may be considered when clinically appropriate.
IV iron is treatment for iron deficiency—not a substitute for evaluating unexplained blood loss.

When GI Evaluation May Be Appropriate

  • Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia
  • Evidence of GI bleeding
  • Melena or hematochezia
  • Unexplained progressive hemoglobin decline
  • Appropriate age/risk context for occult GI blood loss
  • Other concerning GI symptoms

When Hematology Is Appropriate

Consider hematology for:

Severe unexplained anemia
Progressive anemia despite appropriate treatment
Possible hemolysis
Pancytopenia
Multiple unexplained cytopenias
Possible marrow disorder
Hemoglobinopathy
Complex transfusion history
Anemia pattern that remains unclear after reasonable initial evaluation

When Nephrology Is Appropriate

Stable mild CKD does not require nephrology solely because anemia is present.

Consider nephrology for advanced CKD with clinically important anemia, erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy questions, dialysis patients or progressive renal dysfunction.

Anemia & Expected Surgical Blood Loss

Procedure General Concern
Minor hand / superficial procedure Low expected blood loss; mild stable anemia often has limited procedural impact.
Arthroscopy Usually low blood loss.
TKA / THA Meaningful blood-loss/transfusion consideration.
Single-level fusion Moderate concern depending on technique and patient factors.
Multilevel spine fusion Greater concern; anemia optimization increasingly important.
Revision spine / deformity surgery Potentially substantial blood loss; optimize anemia early.

Do Not Use One Universal Cancellation Hemoglobin

There is no single hemoglobin value that automatically determines suitability for every elective procedure.

Interpret hemoglobin with symptoms, cardiovascular reserve, chronicity, etiology, expected surgical blood loss and the ability to safely manage perioperative bleeding.

Anemia More Likely to Require Further Optimization

Additional optimization should be considered when there is:

Severe symptomatic anemia
Rapidly falling hemoglobin
Active bleeding
Unexplained significant anemia
Iron deficiency that has not been addressed before major blood-loss surgery
Evidence of hemolysis
Significant anemia plus poor cardiovascular reserve
Blood-refusal patient undergoing high-blood-loss surgery
Other hematologic instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

Depending on the patient and procedure:

Mild stable chronic anemia
Known CKD-associated anemia with stable renal function
Mild iron deficiency already under appropriate treatment
Stable anemia before a minimal-blood-loss procedure
Stable B12/folate deficiency under treatment

Blood Refusal

When the patient will not accept allogeneic transfusion, the threshold for aggressive preoperative anemia optimization may be different because rescue options for perioperative blood loss are limited.

For major spine or joint surgery, identify acceptable blood products, blood-conservation techniques and hemoglobin optimization strategy before the day of surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Always classify anemia by MCV before building the differential.
  • Compare hemoglobin with prior results to determine chronicity.
  • Low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency.
  • Low TSAT suggests reduced circulating iron availability.
  • Ferritin may be misleadingly normal or elevated with inflammation.
  • Iron deficiency and anemia of inflammation frequently coexist.
  • CKD commonly causes normocytic anemia but does not exclude iron deficiency.
  • High reticulocytes suggest blood loss, hemolysis or recovery from treatment.
  • Low B12 or folate can cause macrocytic anemia.
  • Evaluate unexplained iron deficiency for the underlying source.
  • IV iron may be useful when rapid repletion is needed or oral iron is unsuitable.
  • Expected surgical blood loss changes the significance of a given hemoglobin.
  • Major multilevel/revision spine surgery deserves earlier anemia optimization.
  • Do not use one universal hemoglobin cancellation threshold for every operation.
  • Blood-refusal patients require particularly deliberate blood-conservation planning.
  • The calculator is an aid; mixed or unclear patterns still require clinical judgment.
