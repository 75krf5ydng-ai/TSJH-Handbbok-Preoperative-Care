Preoperative Anemia Workup & Blood Management

CBC interpretation, iron deficiency, diagnostic workup and optimization before elective spine and joint surgery

Home Evaluation Hematology Renal Cardiac

Purpose

Preoperative anemia is common and may increase transfusion requirements, length of stay and perioperative morbidity. Anemia should be recognized early enough to identify the cause and treat reversible abnormalities before elective surgery whenever feasible.

Do not manage preoperative anemia by hemoglobin value alone. Determine the cause, severity, chronicity, symptoms, expected surgical blood loss and relevant cardiovascular, renal or hematologic comorbidities.

Who Should Be Screened?

A CBC is appropriate for most patients undergoing procedures with expected meaningful blood loss, including major spine surgery and total joint arthroplasty.

Minor procedures with negligible expected blood loss may not require anemia screening unless patient-specific factors create another indication.

Practical Definition of Preoperative Anemia

Traditional laboratory reference ranges vary by sex and laboratory. Contemporary perioperative blood-management recommendations often use hemoglobin below approximately 13 g/dL in either sex as a practical trigger to consider preoperative anemia evaluation before major surgery with expected blood loss.

A hemoglobin below 13 g/dL does not automatically require postponement. It identifies a patient who may benefit from further assessment depending on the planned surgery and clinical context.

Start With the CBC

Parameter How to Use It

Step 1: Determine Whether Anemia Is New or Chronic

Compare the current hemoglobin with prior laboratory values whenever available.

Pattern Concern
Stable chronic mild anemia May be compatible with proceeding after appropriate evaluation.
Progressive decline Raises concern for ongoing blood loss, renal disease, nutritional deficiency, inflammation or marrow disease.
Sudden drop Evaluate for active or recent bleeding, hemolysis, fluid shifts or laboratory error.

Step 2: Use the MCV

MCV Pattern Common Causes
Microcytic Iron deficiency, chronic blood loss, thalassemia and selected chronic inflammatory states.
Normocytic CKD, chronic inflammation, acute blood loss, early iron deficiency, hemolysis or bone-marrow disease.
Macrocytic Vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, medication effect or marrow disorders.

Microcytic Anemia Workup

Iron deficiency is the most common concern when microcytosis is present.

Initial Studies

  • Ferritin
  • Serum iron
  • Total iron-binding capacity or transferrin
  • Transferrin saturation
  • Reticulocyte count when appropriate
Do not rely on serum iron alone. Serum iron varies substantially and should be interpreted together with ferritin and transferrin saturation.

Ferritin

Ferritin estimates iron stores and is often the most useful initial test for absolute iron deficiency.

Typical interpretation:

Low ferritin strongly supports absolute iron deficiency.

Normal or elevated ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency when inflammation or chronic disease is present.
Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and may be falsely reassuring in inflammatory states.

Transferrin Saturation

Transferrin saturation reflects the proportion of circulating transferrin carrying iron.

Reduced transferrin saturation supports iron-restricted erythropoiesis and can be particularly useful when ferritin is normal or elevated because of inflammation.

Laboratory Typical Absolute Iron Deficiency

Anemia of Chronic Inflammation

Laboratory Typical Pattern
Ferritin Normal or elevated
Serum iron Low
TIBC / transferrin Low or normal

MCV Usually normocytic but may become microcytic
Iron deficiency and anemia of chronic inflammation may coexist. Mixed disease is common and laboratory patterns may overlap.

Normocytic Anemia Workup

Normocytic anemia requires review of kidney function, inflammation, blood loss and bone-marrow response.

Consider

  • Creatinine and eGFR
  • Iron studies
  • Reticulocyte count
  • Evidence of recent or ongoing bleeding
  • Chronic inflammatory disease
  • Hemolysis studies when indicated
  • Bone-marrow disease when other blood-cell lines are abnormal

Anemia of Chronic Kidney Disease

CKD commonly causes normocytic anemia through impaired erythropoietin production and altered iron utilization.

Do not assume every anemic CKD patient has renal anemia. Iron deficiency, gastrointestinal bleeding, B12 deficiency and other causes may coexist.

Review

  • Creatinine
  • eGFR
  • Iron studies
  • Previous hemoglobin trend
  • Nephrology treatment history

Macrocytic Anemia

Macrocytosis should prompt review of nutritional status, alcohol use, thyroid disease, liver disease and medications.

Potential Evaluation

  • Vitamin B12
  • Folate
  • TSH when clinically indicated
  • Liver studies
  • Reticulocyte count
  • Peripheral smear when appropriate

Vitamin B12 Deficiency

B12 deficiency may cause macrocytic anemia and neurologic abnormalities.

Risk Factors

  • Prior gastric or bariatric surgery
  • Pernicious anemia
  • Malabsorption
  • Restrictive diet
  • Long-term metformin use
  • Older age
Neurologic manifestations may occur even when anemia is not severe.

Folate Deficiency

Folate deficiency may occur with poor nutrition, alcohol use, malabsorption or certain medications.

When macrocytic anemia is present, B12 deficiency should be considered before attributing the abnormality solely to folate deficiency.

Reticulocyte Count

The reticulocyte response provides information about bone-marrow production.

Finding Interpretation
Appropriately elevated reticulocytes Consider blood loss, recovery from deficiency or hemolysis.
Inappropriately low reticulocytes Consider impaired marrow production, renal disease, nutritional deficiency or chronic inflammation.

Hemolysis

Hemolysis evaluation is not required for every anemia but should be considered when the clinical or laboratory pattern suggests increased red-cell destruction.

Potential Studies

  • Reticulocyte count
  • LDH
  • Haptoglobin
  • Indirect bilirubin
  • Peripheral smear

Look for Blood Loss

Iron deficiency in an adult should prompt consideration of the reason iron has been lost.

Ask About

  • Melena
  • Hematochezia
  • Hematemesis
  • Heavy menstrual bleeding
  • Hematuria
  • Frequent blood donation
  • Recent surgery or trauma
  • Chronic NSAID use
  • Known gastrointestinal disease
New unexplained iron-deficiency anemia should not be treated indefinitely without considering an underlying source of blood loss.

Iron Replacement

Iron replacement is appropriate when iron deficiency is established and there is no contraindication.

Route Advantages Limitations
Oral iron Inexpensive, accessible and appropriate when adequate time is available. Gastrointestinal intolerance, slower response and reduced absorption in inflammatory states.
IV iron Faster iron repletion and useful when oral therapy is ineffective, poorly tolerated or the surgical interval is short. Requires infusion capability and individualized assessment.

How Early Should Anemia Be Identified?

Whenever possible, screen and begin treatment several weeks before major elective surgery rather than discovering anemia immediately before the procedure.

A short interval before surgery does not mean evaluation is useless. Identification of the cause can still influence perioperative planning and selected treatments may still be beneficial.

When Anemia Should Prompt Surgical Delay

There is no universal hemoglobin value that requires cancellation of every elective procedure.

Consider delaying elective surgery when anemia is:
  • Severe and unexplained
  • Rapidly worsening
  • Associated with active blood loss
  • Symptomatic
  • Associated with hemodynamic instability
  • Associated with active cardiac ischemia
  • Likely to create unacceptable risk given expected operative blood loss
  • Part of unexplained pancytopenia or suspected marrow disease

Symptoms That Increase Concern

  • Dyspnea disproportionate to baseline
  • Chest discomfort
  • Exertional intolerance
  • Persistent tachycardia
  • Presyncope or syncope
  • Marked fatigue with functional decline
  • Evidence of ongoing blood loss
Symptoms should be interpreted carefully in patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure or significant valvular disease because reduced oxygen-carrying capacity may be less well tolerated.

Spine Surgery Considerations

Complex spinal fusion procedures may involve substantial blood loss. Preoperative anemia therefore deserves greater attention than it may for a minor decompression or limited outpatient procedure.

Consider the number of operative levels, revision status, expected duration, surgical approach and anticipated blood loss when deciding how aggressively anemia should be optimized.

Total Joint Arthroplasty Considerations

Preoperative anemia increases the likelihood of postoperative anemia and transfusion after hip or knee arthroplasty.

Identifying and treating iron deficiency before elective arthroplasty is an important component of patient blood management when time permits.

Transfusion Is Not the Same as Anemia Treatment

Red-cell transfusion treats reduced oxygen-carrying capacity temporarily. It does not correct the underlying cause of iron deficiency, nutritional deficiency, CKD-associated anemia or chronic blood loss.

In a stable elective patient, identify and treat the cause when possible rather than transfusing solely to normalize the hemoglobin.

Restrictive Transfusion Strategy

Modern transfusion practice generally favors restrictive thresholds in hemodynamically stable adults while incorporating symptoms and clinical context.

Clinical Situation Common Guideline Consideration
Most stable hospitalized adults Consider transfusion when hemoglobin is below approximately 7 g/dL, while accounting for clinical circumstances.
Orthopedic surgery A threshold around 8 g/dL may be selected in stable patients depending on symptoms and overall context.
Preexisting cardiovascular disease A threshold around 8 g/dL may be considered, individualized to the patient.
These transfusion thresholds are not automatic triggers and are not intended for every clinical situation. Active hemorrhage, acute coronary syndrome, hemodynamic instability and other special circumstances require individualized management.

Do Not Transfuse to a “Normal” Hemoglobin

In a stable patient without active hemorrhage, transfusion should not be used simply to restore hemoglobin to the laboratory normal range.

Consider symptoms, hemodynamics, cardiac disease, ongoing bleeding and expected operative requirements.

When Hematology Consultation May Be Needed

Consider hematology evaluation for:
  • Severe unexplained anemia
  • Progressive anemia despite appropriate treatment
  • Pancytopenia
  • Abnormal peripheral smear
  • Suspected hemolytic anemia
  • Possible marrow disorder
  • Complex anemia requiring erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy
  • Known hematologic malignancy

When Gastroenterology or Other Evaluation May Be Needed

New iron-deficiency anemia may require evaluation for gastrointestinal blood loss depending on patient age, symptoms, sex, prior screening and clinical history.

The SPA clinician can identify iron-deficiency anemia and initiate an appropriate referral or PCP notification. The need to complete an extensive diagnostic evaluation before surgery depends on severity, surgical urgency and concern for active disease.

Practical Anemia Algorithm

Step Action
1 Confirm anemia and compare with previous CBC.
2 Review MCV and RDW.
3 Obtain ferritin and transferrin saturation when iron deficiency is possible.
4 Review creatinine/eGFR for renal contribution.
5 For macrocytosis, evaluate B12/folate and other indicated causes.
6 Assess for overt or occult blood loss.
7 Determine surgical blood-loss risk.
8 Treat reversible cause when feasible.
9 Determine whether surgery may proceed or should be delayed.

Concise Physician Documentation Examples

Iron Deficiency Anemia

Preoperative CBC demonstrates anemia with hemoglobin ___ g/dL and MCV ___ fL. Iron studies demonstrate ferritin ___ and transferrin saturation ___%, consistent with iron deficiency. No evidence of acute hemodynamic instability. Recommend iron replacement and evaluation for the underlying source of iron loss. Surgical timing should be determined according to anemia severity, expected blood loss and response to optimization.

Stable Chronic Mild Anemia

Mild chronic anemia is stable compared with prior laboratory values. Patient is without symptoms attributable to anemia and has no evidence of active blood loss. Abnormality reviewed in the context of the planned procedure and anticipated blood loss. No urgent transfusion is indicated based on the hemoglobin value alone.

Anemia Requiring Further Evaluation

Preoperative anemia is new/progressive compared with prior baseline and remains unexplained. Further diagnostic evaluation and optimization are required prior to elective surgery because of the degree of anemia and anticipated procedural blood loss.

Key Takeaways

  • Identify preoperative anemia early whenever possible.
  • Compare current hemoglobin with previous values.
  • Use MCV to guide the initial differential diagnosis.
  • Ferritin and transferrin saturation are central to iron-deficiency evaluation.
  • Normal ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency during inflammation.
  • Do not assume normocytic anemia is automatically caused by CKD.
  • Consider B12 and folate when macrocytosis is present.
  • Search for the source of new iron deficiency rather than treating indefinitely without explanation.
  • Optimize iron deficiency before major elective surgery when feasible.
  • No single hemoglobin value mandates cancellation of every surgery.
  • Transfusion should be individualized and generally follows a restrictive strategy in stable patients.
  • Transfusion does not replace diagnosis and treatment of the underlying anemia.
