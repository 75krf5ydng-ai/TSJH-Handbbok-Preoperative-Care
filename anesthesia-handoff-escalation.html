Anesthesia Handoff, Escalation & Surgical Venue Review

What to send, when to escalate, what questions need anesthesia input and how to avoid day-of-surgery surprises

Purpose

Anesthesia escalation is most useful when the question is specifically related to airway, anesthetic technique, aspiration risk, neuraxial timing, device management, respiratory risk, hemodynamic risk or appropriateness of the planned surgical venue.

The goal is not to send every medically complex patient to anesthesia for a generic “review.” The goal is to identify the exact perioperative decision that requires anesthesia expertise.

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

Consider anesthesia review for:

Prior difficult airway
Prior failed intubation
Major prior anesthesia complication
History of malignant hyperthermia
Severe OSA or PAP intolerance with high respiratory risk
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
24-hour oxygen dependence
Severe COPD or ILD
Pulmonary hypertension
Complex pacemaker/ICD management
Neuraxial anticoagulation timing
GLP-1 aspiration concern
Insulin-pump management during surgery
Severe frailty
Uncertain surgical venue appropriateness
Need for higher level of postoperative care

Airway Escalation

Flag for anesthesia review when there is:

  • Prior difficult intubation
  • Prior awake fiberoptic intubation
  • Known difficult mask ventilation
  • Prior anesthesia record documenting difficult airway
  • Severe limited cervical range of motion
  • Significant cervical instability
  • Large neck mass
  • Severe trismus
  • Prior head/neck radiation
  • Major airway reconstruction
  • Other known airway abnormality
Whenever possible, obtain the prior anesthesia record rather than relying only on the phrase “difficult airway.”

OSA / Respiratory Escalation

Consider anesthesia review when OSA is combined with:

PAP intolerance
Chronic opioid therapy
Benzodiazepine use
Severe obesity
Prior postoperative respiratory depression
Home oxygen
Hypercapnia
Significant COPD
Pulmonary hypertension
For PAP-intolerant OSA, a postoperative plan should include continuous pulse oximetry when indicated and NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen when clinically warranted.

Pulmonary HLOC Review

Higher-risk pulmonary findings include:

FEV1 <50% predicted
BODE ≥5
CAT >10
Arozullah ≥4
24-hour oxygen requirement
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

When these are present, anesthesia review can help determine whether the planned outpatient setting is appropriate.

Pulmonary Hypertension

Consider higher-level-of-care review for:

WHO Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension
RVSP >65 mmHg
Severe tricuspid regurgitation
Moderate-to-severe RV dysfunction
Advanced pulmonary vasodilator therapy

Cardiac Conditions Requiring Anesthesia Awareness

  • HFrEF
  • Significant valvular disease
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Pacemaker/ICD
  • Recent arrhythmia
  • Chronic LBBB
  • Recent PCI
  • Positive BNP surveillance trigger
  • Other significant hemodynamic risk
Cardiology determines cardiovascular disease management; anesthesia determines how that disease affects anesthetic and perioperative management.

EF / Surgical Venue Concern

Under the TSJH pathway, EF <35% is a higher-level-of-care concern.
For EF ≥35%, review current heart-failure symptoms, NYHA class, GDMT, cardiology follow-up, recent admissions and functional status.

Higher-Level-of-Care / Venue Review

A patient can be medically stable but still exceed the resources of an ambulatory or specialty surgical setting.

Consider venue review for:

EF <35%
Severe pulmonary hypertension criteria
24-hour oxygen dependence
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Complex dialysis need
Severe frailty
Anticipated ICU requirement
Major blood loss with limited rescue options
Complex airway risk
Other risk exceeding facility capability

Good Handoff vs Vague Handoff

Avoid:

“Needs anesthesia review.”
“High risk.”
“Has bad lungs.”
“Has pacemaker.”
“On Ozempic.”
Prefer:

“Patient scheduled for C4-7 ACDF on ___. Known OSA, stopped PAP approximately 2 years ago, STOP-BANG 6, BMI ___, chronic opioid therapy and prior postoperative desaturation. Room-air SpO2 today ___. Please review postoperative respiratory monitoring and appropriateness of planned surgical setting.”

Same-Day Anesthesia Surprises to Prevent

  • Patient took anticoagulant too recently
  • Recent ICD shock not disclosed
  • No device interrogation available
  • GLP-1 patient with active vomiting
  • SGLT2 hold not completed
  • Type 1 patient told to stop all insulin
  • Difficult airway record never obtained
  • Severe OSA/PAP intolerance not communicated
  • 24-hour oxygen requirement discovered on arrival
  • Recent hospitalization not reviewed
  • Severe frailty with no disposition plan
  • Patient exceeds facility resources

Key Takeaways

  • Escalate a specific anesthesia question, not merely “high risk.”
  • Prior airway records are more useful than a vague history of difficult intubation.
  • OSA plus opioids, benzodiazepines or PAP intolerance deserves careful respiratory planning.
  • Severe pulmonary disease may require venue review even when the patient is clinically stable.
  • CIED management depends on device type, pacing dependence, EMI and procedure.
  • Neuraxial/spine anticoagulant timing should be specifically confirmed when needed.
  • GLP-1 risk assessment should include GI symptoms and dose escalation.
  • Type 1 diabetes must maintain basal insulin coverage.
  • Severe frailty may affect postoperative observation and disposition.
  • Some patients need a higher level of care rather than simply more testing.
  • Close anesthesia-specific pending items before the day of surgery whenever possible.
