Pacemakers, ICDs, CRT Devices & Perioperative Management

Device identification, interrogation, pacing dependence, electrocautery and EP escalation

Purpose

Patients with permanent pacemakers, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) and cardiac resynchronization therapy devices require preoperative review of device type, indication, pacing dependence, recent interrogation and potential interaction with surgical equipment.

The preoperative goal is not simply to document that a cardiac device exists. Determine whether the device is functioning appropriately and whether the planned procedure creates meaningful electromagnetic interference or other device-management concerns.

Common Device Types

Device Primary Function
Permanent pacemaker Treats clinically important bradycardia or conduction disease by providing cardiac pacing.
ICD Provides pacing capability in many systems and detects/treats life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias.
CRT-P Provides cardiac resynchronization pacing for selected patients with heart failure and electrical dyssynchrony.
CRT-D Provides both cardiac resynchronization therapy and defibrillation capability.

Determine the Indication

The reason for device implantation often provides important information about the patient's underlying cardiovascular disease.

Examples

  • Complete heart block
  • High-grade AV block
  • Symptomatic sinus-node dysfunction
  • Severe bradycardia
  • Primary prevention of sudden cardiac death
  • Secondary prevention after ventricular arrhythmia
  • Heart failure with conduction delay requiring CRT
A patient with a CRT-D generally has a different cardiovascular risk profile than a patient with a pacemaker placed for isolated sinus-node dysfunction. Review the underlying disease as well as the device.

Pacemaker Dependence

Pacemaker dependence means that loss or inhibition of pacing could result in clinically significant bradycardia or loss of adequate cardiac output.

Pacemaker dependence is a critical preoperative variable because electromagnetic interference may inhibit pacing in susceptible situations.

Determine From

  • Recent device interrogation
  • Underlying intrinsic rhythm
  • EP/device clinic documentation
  • History of complete/high-grade heart block
  • Pacing percentage when clinically informative

Recent Device Interrogation

Interrogation provides information about battery longevity, lead function, pacing burden, arrhythmia events and programmed settings.

Device information should be sufficiently recent to establish current function. The exact acceptable interval depends on device type, clinical status, local policy and whether the patient has had symptoms or device alerts.
If the date or result of the most recent interrogation cannot be established, or if malfunction is suspected, obtain device/EP review before elective surgery.

What to Review on Interrogation

Item Why It Matters
Battery status Identifies elective-replacement or end-of-life concerns.
Lead function Confirms sensing and pacing integrity.
Underlying rhythm Helps determine pacing dependence.
Pacing percentage Provides context regarding device reliance.
Recorded arrhythmias May reveal recent atrial or ventricular arrhythmia.
ICD therapies Recent shocks or ATP may indicate cardiovascular instability.
Programmed mode Relevant to anticipated EMI and any reprogramming plan.

Device Card

Patients may carry a device identification card listing device type, manufacturer, model and implant information.

A device card is useful for identification but does not replace interrogation when current device function or perioperative management needs to be established.

Symptoms That Require Additional Review

Obtain further assessment for:

Syncope or near-syncope
Unexplained dizziness
New palpitations
New bradycardia
Unexpected tachycardia
Recent ICD shock
Multiple recent ICD therapies
Possible device alarm
New heart-failure symptoms
Concern for lead or generator malfunction

Recent ICD Shock

A recent ICD shock may reflect ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, atrial arrhythmia with inappropriate detection or device malfunction.

Do not proceed with routine elective surgery without determining why a recent ICD shock occurred and whether the patient's rhythm/device status is currently stable.

Battery Depletion

Device interrogation should identify estimated battery longevity and whether the generator has reached elective replacement indicators.

A device nearing end of battery life, particularly in a pacemaker-dependent patient, requires device-clinic or EP review before elective surgery.

Electromagnetic Interference — EMI

Electrosurgical equipment can produce electromagnetic interference that may be detected by an implanted cardiac device.

Risk depends on the type of electrosurgery, location of the procedure relative to the generator and leads, current path, device type and whether the patient is pacing dependent.

Monopolar Electrocautery

Monopolar electrosurgery generally creates more potential for device interference than bipolar electrosurgery.

When clinically significant EMI is anticipated, an individualized device plan should be established before the procedure.

Potential Effects

  • Inhibition of pacemaker output
  • Inappropriate device sensing
  • Inappropriate ICD detection or therapy
  • Transient device interference

Bipolar Electrocautery

Bipolar electrosurgery generally produces less widespread electrical current and is less likely to interfere with implanted cardiac devices.

Use of bipolar electrosurgery may reduce EMI risk when surgically appropriate, but device planning should still reflect the individual procedure and patient.

Procedure Location Matters

The risk of clinically meaningful EMI is generally greater when electrosurgery is used near the generator or leads.

Situation General Concern
Procedure close to upper chest device Greater potential EMI exposure.
Procedure above the umbilicus Often deserves more deliberate device-management review when monopolar electrosurgery is expected.
Procedure distant from device Potential interference may be lower, but final management depends on device type and electrosurgical setup.

Dispersive Electrode / Grounding Pad

For monopolar electrosurgery, current-path planning can help reduce device exposure.

When possible, the electrosurgical current pathway should be arranged so that it does not pass across or near the implanted cardiac device and its leads. Anesthesia and the surgical team determine the final intraoperative setup.

ICD Tachytherapy

When significant EMI is expected, ICD tachyarrhythmia detection and therapy may need to be temporarily suspended to prevent inappropriate shocks.

If ICD tachytherapy is disabled, continuous appropriate monitoring and immediate external defibrillation capability must be available until therapy is restored.

Pacemaker Function During EMI

In pacing-dependent patients exposed to significant EMI, inhibition of pacing can be clinically important.

Anesthesia/device specialists may recommend an asynchronous pacing strategy in selected pacemaker-dependent patients when substantial EMI is anticipated. Do not assume the same approach is appropriate for every pacemaker patient.

Magnet Use

Magnets can alter the behavior of some implanted cardiac devices, but the response varies by device type, manufacturer and programming.

Do not assume that placing a magnet over every cardiac device produces the same effect.
Device General Magnet Concept
Pacemaker A magnet may produce asynchronous pacing in many systems, but response depends on device programming and manufacturer.
ICD A magnet commonly suspends tachyarrhythmia therapies while generally not changing pacing mode; device-specific confirmation is important.
The device plan should specify whether magnet use, reprogramming or no device change is appropriate rather than leaving the decision undefined for the day of surgery.

Magnet Must Be Accessible

A magnet plan is only useful when the generator can be reliably accessed and the magnet can remain appropriately positioned.

Prone positioning, lateral positioning, surgical drapes, body habitus or the operative field may make continuous magnet placement unreliable. In these situations, formal reprogramming may be more appropriate.

Prone Spine Surgery

Prone positioning deserves special consideration because access to an anterior chest generator may become difficult after positioning and sterile preparation.

For patients with ICDs or pacemaker dependence undergoing prone spine surgery, establish the device plan before positioning. Do not rely on an inaccessible magnet strategy unless the team has confirmed it will be feasible.

Shoulder / Upper-Chest Surgery

Procedures involving the shoulder, clavicle or upper chest may occur close to the generator and leads.

Device proximity, planned electrosurgery and patient dependence should be reviewed with anesthesia/device personnel before surgery.

Lower-Extremity Orthopedic Surgery

Hip, knee, foot and ankle procedures are generally more distant from an upper chest cardiac device.

Distance may reduce EMI risk, but do not omit device review when the patient has an ICD, pacing dependence, recent device concerns or another reason for specialized management.

SCS and Other Implanted Neurostimulators

Patients may have both a cardiac implanted electronic device and a spinal cord stimulator or other neurostimulator.

Multiple implanted electronic devices require coordinated perioperative planning. Identify all devices rather than assuming the cardiac device is the only implant relevant to electrosurgery or programming.

External Defibrillation / Pacing Availability

When ICD therapy is disabled or pacing dependence creates meaningful risk, appropriate external pacing/defibrillation capability should be immediately available according to the anesthesia/device plan.

Postoperative Device Restoration

Any temporary device change made for surgery should be reversed when the period of EMI risk has ended and it is safe to restore the usual settings.

A patient whose ICD therapy was intentionally disabled should not leave the appropriately monitored setting until tachyarrhythmia therapy has been confirmed restored.

When Postoperative Interrogation May Be Needed

Consider After

  • Device reprogramming
  • Unexpected significant EMI exposure
  • Suspected device malfunction
  • Hemodynamic event potentially related to pacing
  • External cardioversion/defibrillation when clinically relevant
  • New device alert
  • Unexpected arrhythmia

Cardiac Device and EKG

A paced rhythm may limit interpretation of native conduction and ST-segment changes.

Do not label a paced EKG abnormal merely because pacing spikes or paced QRS complexes are present. Interpret the tracing in the context of the known device and underlying cardiac disease.

Pacemaker Does Not Eliminate Bradycardia Risk

Patients with pacemakers can still have clinically important problems from device malfunction, programming issues, lead dysfunction or noncapture.

Unexpected bradycardia in a paced patient warrants assessment of the rhythm and device function rather than assuming the pacemaker is working normally.

ICD Does Not Eliminate Arrhythmia Risk

An ICD treats selected ventricular arrhythmias but does not eliminate the underlying arrhythmogenic substrate.

Recent ventricular arrhythmia, repeated ICD therapy or worsening cardiomyopathy may change perioperative risk even when the device itself functions normally.

CRT and Heart Failure

CRT devices are commonly implanted in patients with significant ventricular dysfunction and conduction delay.

When CRT is present, review current EF, heart-failure symptoms, recent decompensation and cardiology follow-up rather than treating the device as an isolated technical issue.

When EP / Device Clinic Input Is Appropriate

Obtain EP/device-clinic guidance when there is:

Uncertain device type
Uncertain interrogation status
Pacemaker dependence with anticipated EMI
ICD with expected significant EMI
Recent ICD shock
Suspected device malfunction
Battery nearing replacement
Lead issue
Complex CRT device
Need for formal device reprogramming
Uncertain magnet response
Inability to establish a safe perioperative device plan

When Cardiology Review Is Also Needed

Device clinic review addresses technical device function, while cardiology may be required when the underlying cardiovascular disease is unstable.

Examples

  • Decompensated heart failure
  • New ischemic symptoms
  • Recent sustained ventricular arrhythmia
  • Recent syncope of uncertain cause
  • Significant valvular disease
  • Worsening cardiomyopathy

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider delay when there is:

Suspected device malfunction
Unexplained recent ICD shock
Battery status inadequate or uncertain in a dependent patient
Unresolved lead malfunction
Unexplained syncope with device concern
Unresolved device-management plan for expected significant EMI
Associated decompensated cardiovascular disease
Other clinically significant instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When device function and perioperative planning are adequate, the following do not automatically require postponement:

Stable chronic pacemaker
Stable ICD without recent therapy
Stable CRT device
Chronic paced rhythm
Routine remote monitoring with acceptable recent device status

Decision Framework

Question Action
What device does the patient have? Identify pacemaker, ICD, CRT-P or CRT-D.
Why was it implanted? Understand the underlying rhythm or cardiomyopathy.
Is the patient pacing dependent? Establish before procedures with meaningful EMI risk.
Is interrogation current and acceptable? Obtain device review if status is uncertain or symptoms changed.
Will significant EMI occur? Determine ICD/pacing management with anesthesia/device specialists.
Will a magnet be reliable? Consider positioning, surgical field and device-specific response.
Was the device reprogrammed? Ensure postoperative restoration before leaving appropriate monitoring.

Key Takeaways

  • Identify device type, indication and manufacturer when possible.
  • Determine pacemaker dependence.
  • Review sufficiently recent interrogation information.
  • Recent ICD shocks require explanation before elective surgery.
  • Monopolar electrocautery creates more EMI concern than bipolar cautery.
  • Procedure location relative to the device matters.
  • ICD tachytherapy may require temporary suspension when significant EMI is expected.
  • Magnet responses differ between devices and manufacturers.
  • Do not assume magnet placement changes pacemaker and ICD function in the same way.
  • Prone positioning may make magnet use unreliable or inaccessible.
  • If ICD therapy is disabled, confirm it is restored postoperatively.
  • Device malfunction, battery depletion or an unresolved EMI plan may require surgical delay.
