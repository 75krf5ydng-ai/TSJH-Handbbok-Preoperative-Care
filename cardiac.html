Preoperative Cardiac Evaluation & Risk Stratification

Practical guidance for elective noncardiac surgery

Home Preoperative Evaluation

Purpose

Preoperative cardiovascular assessment is intended to identify patients with cardiac conditions that may meaningfully increase perioperative risk, determine whether additional evaluation or treatment is indicated, and avoid unnecessary testing in patients who can safely proceed with elective noncardiac surgery.

Cardiac testing should be performed only when the result is expected to change management. Routine stress testing, echocardiography, or cardiology consultation is not indicated solely because a patient is scheduled for surgery.

Step 1: Determine Surgical Risk

Risk Category Estimated Cardiac Risk Examples
Low <1% Minor superficial procedures, cataract surgery, endoscopy, minor hand procedures, and other limited procedures with minimal physiologic stress.
Intermediate Approximately 1–5% Most orthopedic joint replacements, many spine procedures, major urologic surgery, and other procedures associated with moderate physiologic stress.
High >5% Major vascular procedures and selected prolonged or highly invasive operations associated with substantial blood loss or hemodynamic stress.

Step 2: Identify Active or Unstable Cardiac Conditions

Elective surgery should generally be postponed when an active cardiac condition is present until the condition has been evaluated and treated appropriately.

Potential reasons to delay elective surgery include:
  • Acute coronary syndrome or ongoing myocardial ischemia
  • Decompensated heart failure
  • Unstable or clinically significant arrhythmia
  • Symptomatic severe valvular heart disease
  • New unexplained cardiac symptoms requiring evaluation

Symptoms requiring further assessment may include new or worsening chest pain, unexplained dyspnea, orthopnea, syncope, significant palpitations, rapidly progressive edema, or a meaningful decline in functional status.

Step 3: Calculate the Revised Cardiac Risk Index

The Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) is used to estimate risk of major perioperative cardiac complications.

RCRI Risk Factor Point
History of ischemic heart disease 1
History of congestive heart failure 1
History of cerebrovascular disease 1
Diabetes requiring insulin therapy 1
Preoperative serum creatinine >2.0 mg/dL 1
High-risk surgery 1
Documentation format:
RCRI: ___ points

Step 4: Assess Functional Capacity

Functional capacity should preferably be assessed using the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) rather than relying only on an informal estimate of METs.

Poor or unknown functional capacity does not automatically require cardiac testing. The patient's surgical risk, clinical risk factors, symptoms, and whether a test result would change management should all be considered.

Recommended documentation:
DASI score: ___

Step 5: BNP or NT-proBNP Screening

Cardiac biomarker screening may assist with perioperative risk stratification in selected patients undergoing elevated-risk noncardiac surgery.

TSJH / TSJR Screening Approach

Obtain BNP or NT-proBNP when the patient meets the applicable institutional screening criteria, including patients with an elevated RCRI or patients meeting age and cardiovascular-risk criteria.

Biomarker Threshold Action
BNP ≥92 pg/mL Initiate postoperative cardiac surveillance according to institutional protocol.
NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L Initiate postoperative cardiac surveillance according to institutional protocol.
When the biomarker threshold is met, obtain a postoperative EKG and daily troponins for approximately 48–72 hours according to the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol.

BNP SmartPhrase

BNP screening. Obtain BNP because of age greater than 65 years and RCRI score of 1. If BNP is 92 pg/mL or greater, implement the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.

Step 6: Determine Whether Additional Cardiac Testing Is Needed

Additional testing should be selective and should generally be ordered only when results are likely to change perioperative management.

Clinical Situation General Approach
Asymptomatic patient with acceptable functional capacity Additional ischemic testing is generally not required solely for surgical clearance.
Poor or unknown functional capacity with elevated perioperative risk Consider biomarkers and selective additional testing if results would change management.
New or worsening cardiac symptoms Evaluate the symptoms before elective surgery.
Known cardiovascular disease with stable symptoms Review prior testing, current therapy, functional capacity, and recent cardiology evaluation before ordering duplicate studies.

Electrocardiogram

A preoperative EKG may be reasonable in patients with known cardiovascular disease, significant arrhythmia, structural heart disease, important cardiovascular risk factors, or when required by institutional protocol.

Minor nonspecific abnormalities in an asymptomatic patient do not automatically require additional testing. Compare with previous tracings whenever available and evaluate new clinically significant changes.

Left bundle branch block: When ischemic evaluation is indicated in a patient with LBBB, exercise ECG alone is not an appropriate ischemic test. Pharmacologic nuclear stress imaging, such as regadenoson testing, or cardiology evaluation should be considered according to the clinical scenario and institutional guidance.

Heart Failure and Cardiomyopathy

Determine whether heart failure is clinically compensated. Review symptoms, recent hospitalizations, left ventricular function, guideline-directed medical therapy, and current volume status.

Decompensated heart failure is an active cardiac condition and elective surgery should generally be postponed pending stabilization.

TSJH Ischemic Cardiomyopathy / HFrEF Parameters

Patients with an ejection fraction below approximately 35% may require a higher level of care depending on institutional criteria. Patients with an EF of 35% or greater may be considered for surgery at TSJH when clinically stable and when other institutional requirements are satisfied, including appropriate cardiology follow-up, stable symptoms, optimized therapy, and absence of recent heart-failure decompensation.

Valvular Heart Disease

Aortic Stenosis

Moderate-to-severe or symptomatic aortic stenosis warrants careful cardiovascular assessment before elective surgery.

Severe aortic stenosis includes high-gradient disease with findings such as aortic valve area ≤1.0 cm², mean gradient ≥40 mmHg, or peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s. Severe aortic stenosis may be defined by findings such as valve area less than 1.0 cm², mean gradient greater than 40 mmHg, or peak velocity greater than 4.0 m/s. Patients meeting institutional severe-AS exclusion criteria should be considered for a higher level of care.

Mitral Stenosis

Moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis should have appropriate cardiovascular evaluation. Severe disease may include mitral valve area of 1.5 cm² or less, mean gradient of approximately 10 mmHg or greater, or pulmonary artery systolic pressure above approximately 50 mmHg.

Pulmonary Hypertension and Right Heart Disease

Pulmonary hypertension can substantially increase perioperative risk, particularly when accompanied by right ventricular dysfunction or severe tricuspid regurgitation.

Consider higher-level-of-care evaluation when significant pulmonary hypertension is present, including WHO Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension, RVSP greater than approximately 65 mmHg, severe tricuspid regurgitation, moderate-to-severe right ventricular dysfunction, or advanced pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Coronary Artery Disease

Patients with stable coronary artery disease do not require routine preoperative coronary angiography or revascularization solely to permit noncardiac surgery. Indications for coronary intervention should generally be the same as they would be outside the perioperative setting.

Review prior myocardial infarction, PCI or CABG history, current symptoms, antiplatelet therapy, recent cardiac testing, and cardiology recommendations.

Stress Testing

Stress testing should not be ordered routinely. Consider testing only when the patient has elevated clinical and surgical risk, poor or unknown functional capacity, and the result is expected to alter management.

A recent normal stress study in a clinically stable and asymptomatic patient generally does not need to be repeated solely because another elective procedure is planned.

Echocardiography

Routine repeat echocardiography is not indicated in every patient with cardiac disease. Consider echocardiography when there is new or worsening dyspnea, suspected new ventricular dysfunction, clinically important valvular disease, or a meaningful change in cardiac status.

Stable patients with adequate recent echocardiographic information usually do not require duplicate testing solely for preoperative purposes.

Arrhythmias and Cardiac Devices

Evaluate clinically important arrhythmias based on type, rate control, symptoms, anticoagulation status, and hemodynamic stability.

Patients with pacemakers or implantable cardioverter-defibrillators should have device information reviewed when relevant to the planned procedure, particularly when electrocautery or electromagnetic interference is anticipated.

Antiplatelet and Anticoagulant Considerations

Perioperative management requires balancing surgical bleeding risk against thrombotic risk. Medication interruption should account for the indication for therapy, renal function, procedural bleeding risk, neuraxial considerations, recent coronary intervention, and surgeon or specialist recommendations.

Recent coronary stent placement, recent ablation, Watchman placement, recent thromboembolism, or other high-thrombotic-risk conditions may require cardiology, electrophysiology, or anticoagulation-management guidance before medication interruption.

When Cardiology Consultation Is Appropriate

Cardiology consultation may be appropriate when there is an active cardiac condition, unexplained symptoms, significant new EKG abnormality, severe or inadequately characterized valvular disease, unstable heart failure, significant pulmonary hypertension, complex arrhythmia, recent coronary intervention, or uncertainty regarding cardiovascular optimization.

A cardiology visit should answer a specific clinical question. Routine referral solely to obtain the phrase “cardiac clearance” is not necessary when the preoperative clinician can adequately assess and optimize the patient's cardiovascular risk.

Preoperative Documentation

A concise cardiovascular assessment should document the major information needed to support the perioperative decision.

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is the surgery emergent? Proceed according to urgency while managing cardiovascular risk as clinically feasible.
Is there an active cardiac condition? Evaluate and treat before elective surgery when appropriate.
What is the procedural cardiac risk? Categorize as low, intermediate, or high risk.
What is the RCRI? Calculate and document the numeric score.
What is the patient's functional capacity? Use DASI when possible.
Is BNP or NT-proBNP screening indicated? Obtain according to institutional and cardiovascular-risk criteria.
Would additional testing change management? Order only if clinically useful.
Is the patient medically optimized? Proceed, postpone, or obtain further evaluation based on the overall assessment.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not order cardiac testing simply because surgery is planned.
  • Identify active cardiac conditions before elective surgery.
  • Document surgical risk and the numeric RCRI score.
  • Use DASI for structured functional-capacity assessment when possible.
  • Use BNP or NT-proBNP selectively for perioperative risk stratification.
  • Use postoperative cardiac surveillance when institutional biomarker thresholds are met.
  • Review existing cardiac studies before ordering duplicate testing.
  • Refer to cardiology when there is a specific cardiovascular question or unresolved risk.
  • Avoid using the phrase “cleared for surgery.” Document optimization and residual risk instead.
