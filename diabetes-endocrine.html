Preoperative Diabetes & Endocrine Evaluation

Glycemic, thyroid, adrenal and endocrine optimization before elective surgery

Purpose

Endocrine disorders may affect wound healing, infection risk, hemodynamic stability, electrolyte balance, medication response and postoperative recovery. Diabetes is the most common endocrine condition encountered in elective surgical patients.

The goal of preoperative endocrine evaluation is to identify uncontrolled disease, prevent medication-related complications, establish a safe perioperative glucose plan and determine whether additional evaluation is necessary before elective surgery.

Diabetes: Initial Assessment

Preoperative assessment should establish the type of diabetes, current medications, recent glucose control, complications and history of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemic emergencies.

Review Important Information
Diabetes type Type 1, type 2, secondary diabetes or other documented form.
Current medications Insulin, metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and other antihyperglycemic medications.
A1c Review the most recent value when available and clinically relevant.
Glucose pattern Fasting glucose, home readings, CGM data and history of symptomatic hypo- or hyperglycemia.
Complications CKD, neuropathy, gastroparesis, CAD, PAD, retinopathy and autonomic dysfunction.
Prior emergencies DKA, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state or severe hypoglycemia.

Hemoglobin A1c

A1c reflects average glycemic control over the preceding several months and may help identify patients at increased risk of perioperative hyperglycemia, infection and impaired wound healing.

A1c should not be used as the sole determinant of whether surgery can proceed. Interpret it together with current glucose values, procedure type, infection risk, wound-healing considerations and institutional criteria.
Documentation format:

A1c: ___%

Day-of-Surgery Glucose

Perioperative glucose goals should balance avoidance of significant hyperglycemia against the risk of hypoglycemia.

Marked hyperglycemia should prompt evaluation for dehydration, ketosis, infection, medication nonadherence or hyperglycemic emergency before elective surgery proceeds.
Elective surgery should generally be postponed in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state or another acute metabolic decompensation.

Type 1 Diabetes

Patients with type 1 diabetes require basal insulin even when fasting. Complete omission of basal insulin can result in severe hyperglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis.

Do not stop all insulin in a patient with type 1 diabetes. Perioperative insulin dosing should follow the anesthesia and facility medication protocol.

Confirm the patient's usual basal regimen, insulin pump use, CGM use and history of hypoglycemia.

Insulin Therapy

Insulin management should be individualized based on insulin type, dosing schedule, diabetes type, fasting duration, usual glucose pattern and institutional protocol.

Insulin Issue Preoperative Consideration
Basal insulin Often continued at an adjusted dose according to institutional guidance.
Mealtime insulin Generally withheld while the patient is fasting unless specifically directed otherwise.
Correction insulin May be used according to perioperative glucose protocol.
Insulin pump Determine pump type, basal rate, procedure duration and whether the device can safely remain in use.
Continuous glucose monitor CGM information may be useful, but perioperative treatment decisions may still require point-of-care glucose confirmation.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors are associated with perioperative euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis and require special attention before surgery.

SGLT2 inhibitors should be held before elective surgery according to current prescribing information, anesthesia guidance and institutional protocol. Many agents require interruption for approximately 3 days, with some requiring a longer interval.

Examples include empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin and ertugliflozin.

Red Flags for Euglycemic DKA

  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Abdominal pain
  • Tachypnea
  • Unexplained metabolic acidosis
  • Elevated anion gap
  • Positive ketones despite only modest glucose elevation

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists may delay gastric emptying and can create perioperative aspiration concerns in selected patients.

Management should follow the most current anesthesia and institutional guidance because recommendations may differ according to the agent, dosing schedule, treatment phase, gastrointestinal symptoms and aspiration risk.

Important questions include whether the patient is in a dose-escalation phase and whether nausea, vomiting, early satiety, abdominal bloating or symptoms suggesting delayed gastric emptying are present.

Metformin

Metformin management should consider renal function, fasting status, anticipated contrast exposure and the facility's medication protocol.

Review kidney function before surgery and follow anesthesia or institutional instructions regarding the timing of the last dose and postoperative resumption.

Sulfonylureas and Other Oral Agents

Sulfonylureas can cause fasting hypoglycemia and are commonly withheld on the day of surgery.

Other oral diabetes medications should be managed according to their pharmacology and the current perioperative medication protocol.

Diabetic Gastroparesis

Diabetic autonomic neuropathy may impair gastric emptying and increase aspiration risk.

Significant nausea, vomiting, early satiety, retained food, known severe gastroparesis or other evidence of delayed gastric emptying should be communicated to anesthesia before elective surgery.

Hypoglycemia Risk

Patients with prior severe hypoglycemia, impaired awareness, CKD, intensive insulin regimens or prolonged fasting may have increased perioperative hypoglycemia risk.

Ensure medication instructions are clear and that glucose is checked appropriately before surgery.

Hyperglycemia and Infection Risk

Persistent hyperglycemia can impair leukocyte function and wound healing and is associated with increased postoperative infection risk.

The significance of an elevated glucose value depends on degree, duration, symptoms and whether there is evidence of acute metabolic decompensation.

Thyroid Disease

Stable treated hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism generally does not require special preoperative testing beyond appropriate clinical review.

Hypothyroidism

Confirm medication use and assess for evidence of severe untreated disease, including significant bradycardia, hypothermia, altered mental status, heart failure or other signs of major physiologic compromise.

Severe untreated hypothyroidism or suspected myxedema decompensation warrants treatment before elective surgery.

Hyperthyroidism

Assess for tachycardia, tremor, heat intolerance, weight loss, arrhythmia and other manifestations of active thyrotoxicosis.

Elective surgery should generally be postponed in patients with overt, uncontrolled thyrotoxicosis because of the risk of severe cardiovascular instability and thyroid storm.

When Thyroid Testing Is Appropriate

Routine thyroid testing is not required in every surgical patient.

Consider TSH and related testing when thyroid disease is known and control is uncertain, symptoms suggest thyroid dysfunction, treatment has recently changed or there is another specific clinical indication.

Chronic Corticosteroid Therapy

Determine whether the patient is taking chronic systemic corticosteroids and whether hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis suppression is plausible.

Review Clinical Question
Medication Which glucocorticoid is being used?
Dose Is the daily exposure sufficient to suppress the HPA axis?
Duration Has therapy been prolonged?
Recent discontinuation Could suppression persist despite stopping therapy?
Surgical stress Is supplemental perioperative steroid dosing needed?
Perioperative steroid supplementation should be individualized according to chronic steroid exposure, adrenal function and surgical stress.

Adrenal Insufficiency

Known adrenal insufficiency requires a perioperative glucocorticoid plan. Abrupt interruption of required steroid replacement can precipitate adrenal crisis.

Patients with known adrenal insufficiency should have perioperative steroid management clearly documented and communicated to the anesthesia and surgical teams.

Obesity and Metabolic Risk

Obesity may be associated with diabetes, hypertension, OSA, difficult airway management, venous thromboembolism risk and impaired wound healing.

BMI should be considered together with comorbid disease, mobility, functional capacity and institutional surgical criteria rather than as an isolated risk measure.

When Endocrinology Consultation May Be Appropriate

Endocrinology consultation may be helpful when endocrine disease remains unstable or complex despite routine preoperative management.

Examples include:
  • Severely uncontrolled diabetes
  • Recurrent severe hypoglycemia
  • Complex insulin pump management
  • Recent DKA or hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state
  • Suspected adrenal insufficiency
  • Complex chronic steroid management
  • Uncontrolled hyperthyroidism
  • Severe untreated hypothyroidism

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when significant endocrine instability is present, including:
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis
  • Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state
  • Severe symptomatic hypoglycemia
  • Marked uncontrolled hyperglycemia with acute metabolic abnormalities
  • Uncontrolled thyrotoxicosis
  • Severe untreated hypothyroidism with systemic manifestations
  • Suspected adrenal crisis
  • Other acute endocrine decompensation

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is diabetes clinically stable? Review glucose pattern, A1c, medications and acute symptoms.
Is the patient using an SGLT2 inhibitor? Confirm appropriate preoperative interruption according to protocol.
Is the patient using a GLP-1 receptor agonist? Review current anesthesia guidance and assess GI symptoms and aspiration risk.
Is the patient on insulin? Establish basal, prandial and day-of-surgery insulin instructions.
Does the patient have thyroid disease? Determine whether disease is clinically controlled.
Is chronic steroid therapy present? Determine whether perioperative steroid supplementation is needed.
Is endocrine disease unstable? Treat, obtain specialist input or postpone elective surgery as appropriate.

Key Takeaways

  • Assess current glucose control rather than relying on A1c alone.
  • Never completely discontinue required basal insulin in type 1 diabetes.
  • Recognize the perioperative euglycemic DKA risk associated with SGLT2 inhibitors.
  • Review current anesthesia guidance for GLP-1 receptor agonists.
  • Consider gastroparesis and aspiration risk in long-standing diabetes.
  • Postpone elective surgery for DKA or hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state.
  • Identify uncontrolled thyroid disease before elective surgery.
  • Review chronic steroid use and adrenal suppression risk.
  • Use endocrinology consultation when there is a specific unresolved endocrine problem.
