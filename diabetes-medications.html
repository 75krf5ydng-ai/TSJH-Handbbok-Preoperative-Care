Purpose

Perioperative diabetes management aims to reduce hypoglycemia, severe hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, aspiration risk and medication-related complications while avoiding unnecessary cancellation.

Medication instructions should be individualized to diabetes type, medication class, fasting duration, procedure timing, renal function and current glycemic control.

A1c

A1c reflects average glucose exposure over approximately the prior 2–3 months. It is useful for chronic risk assessment but does not replace the current glucose measurement.

A1c should not be used as the sole reason to cancel elective surgery. Interpret it with current glucose, procedure type, wound-healing risk, infection risk and facility/surgeon criteria.

Day-of-Surgery Glucose

Current glucose is more important than A1c for identifying acute metabolic instability on the day of surgery.

Mild to moderate asymptomatic hyperglycemia is common perioperatively and does not automatically require cancellation.
Marked hyperglycemia accompanied by ketosis, anion-gap acidosis, dehydration, vomiting, altered mental status or other evidence of DKA/HHS requires treatment before elective surgery.

Type 1 Diabetes

Patients with type 1 diabetes require basal insulin even while fasting. Complete omission of basal insulin can precipitate diabetic ketoacidosis.

Establish a specific basal insulin or pump plan rather than simply instructing the patient to “hold all diabetes medications.”

Type 2 Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes management depends on whether the patient uses oral agents, injectables, basal insulin, mealtime insulin or combination therapy.

The perioperative goal is to avoid both fasting hypoglycemia and significant hyperglycemia while maintaining adequate basal insulin when clinically needed.

Basal Insulin

Long-acting insulin often requires dose adjustment rather than complete omission.

Examples

  • Glargine
  • Detemir
  • Degludec
  • NPH
Dose reduction depends on the patient's usual regimen, hypoglycemia risk, procedure timing and institutional anesthesia protocol.

Mealtime / Prandial Insulin

Rapid-acting insulin given specifically for meals is generally not administered when the patient is fasting unless it is being used as correction insulin under an established plan.

Do not confuse prandial insulin with basal insulin. Holding all insulin in a patient who requires basal therapy may be unsafe.

Premixed Insulin

Premixed insulin regimens require extra planning because the dose contains both basal/intermediate and prandial components.

Avoid giving routine full premixed doses to a fasting patient without a defined perioperative plan.

Insulin Pumps

Patients using continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion require a documented perioperative strategy.

Continuous Glucose Monitors

CGMs can provide useful trend information, but intraoperative management may still require point-of-care or laboratory glucose confirmation.

Do not rely exclusively on CGM readings when clinical decisions require confirmation, particularly if perfusion, device accuracy or interference is a concern.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

Examples

  • Empagliflozin / Jardiance
  • Dapagliflozin / Farxiga
  • Canagliflozin / Invokana
  • Ertugliflozin / Steglatro
SGLT2 inhibitors increase perioperative risk for euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis and should be held before elective surgery according to current anesthesia and medication guidance.

SGLT2 Hold Timing

Current practice commonly uses a multi-day preoperative interruption rather than holding only on the morning of surgery.

Verify the exact hold interval under the current anesthesia/facility protocol, as recommended timing can differ slightly by agent.
If the patient did not hold an SGLT2 inhibitor as instructed, do not assume a normal glucose excludes risk.

Euglycemic DKA

Euglycemic DKA can occur with normal or only moderately elevated glucose.

Consider euglycemic DKA when SGLT2 exposure is associated with:

Nausea
Vomiting
Abdominal pain
Malaise
Tachypnea
Dehydration
Ketones
Low bicarbonate
Elevated anion gap
Important:

Normal or modestly elevated glucose does not exclude euglycemic DKA.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Examples

  • Semaglutide / Ozempic / Wegovy
  • Tirzepatide / Mounjaro / Zepbound
  • Dulaglutide / Trulicity
  • Liraglutide / Victoza / Saxenda

GLP-1 therapy may delay gastric emptying, particularly during dose escalation or when significant gastrointestinal symptoms are present.

Current anesthesia guidance increasingly emphasizes individualized aspiration risk assessment rather than automatically applying the same hold rule to every patient.

Anesthesia review is especially important when there is:

Metformin

Metformin management should follow the facility's perioperative medication protocol and take into account renal function, fasting status and the planned procedure.

Metformin is not a cause of fasting hypoglycemia when used alone, but renal function and acute illness remain important considerations.

Sulfonylureas

Examples

  • Glipizide
  • Glimepiride
  • Glyburide
Sulfonylureas may cause fasting hypoglycemia and are generally withheld on the day of surgery according to standard perioperative medication protocols.

Meglitinides

Short-acting insulin secretagogues such as repaglinide are meal-related and typically withheld when the patient is fasting.

DPP-4 Inhibitors

Examples

  • Sitagliptin
  • Linagliptin
  • Saxagliptin

These agents have relatively low hypoglycemia risk when used alone, but final perioperative instructions should follow the facility protocol.

Thiazolidinediones

Example

  • Pioglitazone

Review heart-failure history and edema because thiazolidinediones can promote fluid retention.

In a patient with active heart-failure decompensation, address the cardiovascular instability rather than focusing only on the diabetes medication.

Hypoglycemia

Preoperative fasting and medication effects can precipitate hypoglycemia.

Symptoms may include:

Sweating
Tremor
Palpitations
Confusion
Weakness
Dizziness
Seizure
Loss of consciousness

Treat clinically significant hypoglycemia before proceeding with elective anesthesia.

Hyperglycemia

Hyperglycemia should be interpreted in context rather than by one rigid cancellation threshold.

Important questions are whether the patient has DKA/HHS, dehydration, acute infection, severe symptoms or other metabolic instability.
Very high glucose with acute illness or metabolic decompensation warrants further evaluation before elective surgery.

DKA

Diabetic ketoacidosis is incompatible with elective surgery and requires treatment before proceeding.

Common Features

  • Hyperglycemia or euglycemic presentation with SGLT2 use
  • Ketosis
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Elevated anion gap
  • Dehydration
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Abdominal pain

Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State

HHS is a medical emergency characterized by severe hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity and dehydration, often with altered mental status. Elective surgery should not proceed.

Renal Function and Diabetes Medications

Renal dysfunction may alter medication safety and clearance.

Review

  • Creatinine
  • eGFR
  • Recent AKI
  • Metformin use
  • SGLT2 therapy
  • Insulin requirements

Diabetes and Infection

Poor glycemic control may increase infection risk, but active infection itself can also worsen glucose control.

Unexpected marked hyperglycemia should prompt assessment for infection, steroid exposure, missed medication and other acute contributors.

Diabetes and Wound Healing

Chronic hyperglycemia may impair wound healing and immune function.

For implant and major spine/joint surgery, document glycemic control and address acute metabolic instability. Use A1c as part of the risk discussion rather than as the sole determination of surgical suitability.

Chronic Steroids

Patients with diabetes taking chronic corticosteroids may have worsened glucose control and may also require assessment for adrenal suppression.

Review steroid dose, duration and indication. Coordinate any stress-dose strategy with anesthesia or the treating clinician when clinically indicated.

When Endocrinology May Be Appropriate

Consider endocrinology for:

Type 1 diabetes with complex perioperative management
Insulin pump management uncertainty
Recurrent severe hypoglycemia
Recent DKA or HHS
Severely uncontrolled diabetes requiring complex adjustment
Complex steroid-related hyperglycemia
Unclear diabetes type
Other difficult metabolic management

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

Examples

  • SGLT2 inhibitor not held as instructed
  • GLP-1 therapy with significant GI symptoms
  • Known gastroparesis
  • Insulin pump management uncertainty
  • Severe hyperglycemia
  • Recent DKA/HHS
  • Complex type 1 diabetes

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

DKA
Euglycemic DKA
Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state
Severe symptomatic hypoglycemia
Severe dehydration related to hyperglycemia
Unresolved metabolic acidosis
Active severe infection with uncontrolled glucose
Other acute metabolic instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

Depending on clinical context, these do not automatically require cancellation:

Elevated A1c without acute metabolic instability
Mild or moderate asymptomatic hyperglycemia
Stable chronic diabetes on an established regimen
CGM use
Insulin pump use with a defined perioperative plan
GLP-1 use without significant GI/aspiration-risk features

Decision Framework

Question Action
Type 1 or type 2 diabetes? Type 1 requires ongoing basal insulin.
Is the patient fasting? Adjust prandial and oral therapy appropriately.
Is SGLT2 therapy present? Confirm multi-day hold and assess for euglycemic DKA if concerning symptoms exist.
Is GLP-1 therapy present? Assess GI symptoms, dose escalation and aspiration risk.
Does the patient use a pump? Establish pump-continuation or alternative basal insulin plan.
Is glucose elevated? Determine whether acute metabolic decompensation is present.
Is the patient hypoglycemic? Treat before elective anesthesia.
Is DKA/HHS suspected? Postpone elective surgery and treat the metabolic emergency.

Key Takeaways

  • Type 1 diabetes requires basal insulin even while fasting.
  • Do not tell every diabetic patient to hold all insulin.
  • Use current glucose to assess acute status; use A1c for chronic control.
  • Elevated A1c alone does not automatically require cancellation.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors require preoperative interruption because of euglycemic DKA risk.
  • Normal glucose does not exclude SGLT2-associated ketoacidosis.
  • GLP-1 management should include GI symptoms and aspiration-risk assessment.
  • Insulin pump patients need a specific perioperative plan.
  • CGM readings may need confirmation for clinical decisions.
  • Sulfonylureas can cause fasting hypoglycemia.
  • DKA and HHS require treatment before elective surgery.
  • Severe metabolic instability matters more than one isolated glucose or A1c value.
