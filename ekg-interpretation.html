Preoperative EKG Interpretation & Escalation

Rhythm, conduction, QTc, ischemic changes, comparison and when further evaluation is needed

Purpose

The preoperative EKG should be interpreted in the context of symptoms, prior tracings, known cardiovascular disease and the planned surgical procedure. Computer-generated interpretations are useful screening aids but should not replace clinician review.

The key questions are: What rhythm is present? Is the finding new? Does it suggest active cardiac disease? Does it change perioperative management?

Systematic EKG Review

Use the same sequence every time:

1. Rate
2. Rhythm
3. P waves
4. PR interval
5. QRS duration / morphology
6. Axis
7. QT / QTc
8. R-wave progression
9. Q waves
10. ST segments
11. T waves
12. Compare with prior EKG
13. Correlate with symptoms

Basic Measurements

Measurement General Reference
PR interval Approximately 120–200 ms
QRS duration <120 ms is generally considered narrow
QTc Interpret by sex, clinical context, medications and correction formula
Rate 60–100 bpm is typical resting sinus range
Do not make a perioperative decision from a computer-generated interval or diagnosis without reviewing the tracing and clinical context.

Normal Sinus Rhythm

Typical Features

  • Regular rhythm
  • P wave before each QRS
  • Consistent PR interval
  • Expected sinus P-wave morphology
  • Rate generally 60–100 bpm
Normal sinus rhythm without concerning ischemic or conduction findings generally does not require additional cardiac evaluation solely because surgery is planned.

Sinus Bradycardia

Sinus bradycardia may be normal in conditioned patients or may reflect medication effects, conduction disease, hypothyroidism or other conditions.

An asymptomatic stable sinus rate in the 50s may be acceptable, especially when consistent with prior readings and beta-blocker therapy.
Evaluate significant bradycardia associated with syncope, dizziness, hypotension, high-grade conduction disease or other symptoms.

Sinus Tachycardia

Sinus tachycardia is usually a physiologic response rather than a primary arrhythmia diagnosis.

Consider

  • Pain
  • Anxiety
  • Dehydration
  • Fever/infection
  • Anemia
  • Hypoxemia
  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Pulmonary embolism
  • Medication/stimulant effect
Persistent unexplained tachycardia should be evaluated rather than simply documented as an abnormal EKG.

Premature Atrial Contractions

PACs are common and frequently benign.

Occasional PACs in an asymptomatic patient with otherwise stable cardiovascular status usually do not require postponement or specialist evaluation by themselves.
Frequent atrial ectopy with palpitations, tachyarrhythmia or other symptoms may warrant additional evaluation.

Premature Ventricular Contractions

PVCs may occur in healthy patients or in association with structural heart disease, electrolyte abnormalities, ischemia or stimulant exposure.

Occasional isolated PVCs without symptoms or known unstable structural disease often do not require further workup solely before surgery.
Frequent multifocal PVCs, ventricular couplets, nonsustained VT, syncope or other evidence of ventricular instability warrants additional evaluation.

Atrial Fibrillation

Typical Features

  • Irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm
  • No consistent organized P waves
  • Variable R-R intervals
Known chronic atrial fibrillation with controlled ventricular response and an established anticoagulation plan does not automatically require cardiology review before every elective surgery.
New AF, rapid ventricular response, hypotension, heart-failure symptoms or uncertain anticoagulation requires further evaluation.

Atrial Flutter

Atrial flutter may show characteristic flutter waves with fixed or variable AV conduction.

Stable known flutter with adequate rate control and established stroke-prevention management may be handled similarly to stable AF.
New flutter or uncontrolled ventricular rate warrants further assessment.

Supraventricular Tachycardia

Active sustained SVT with significant symptoms, hypotension, ischemia or poor rate control should be treated before routine elective surgery.

A remote history of successfully treated SVT without current symptoms does not automatically require repeat electrophysiologic evaluation.

First-Degree AV Block

First-degree AV block is characterized by PR prolongation greater than 200 ms with conduction of each atrial impulse to the ventricles.

Stable isolated first-degree AV block is commonly benign and usually does not require further preoperative workup by itself.

Mobitz I — Wenckebach

Mobitz I second-degree AV block typically demonstrates progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped QRS complex.

Clinical significance depends on symptoms, location of conduction disease and overall cardiac status.
New conduction abnormality, significant bradycardia or symptoms should prompt further evaluation.

Mobitz II

Mobitz II second-degree AV block is clinically significant conduction disease and requires cardiovascular evaluation. Routine elective surgery should not proceed without an appropriate management plan.

Complete Heart Block

Complete third-degree AV block is a major conduction abnormality. New or unmanaged complete heart block requires urgent cardiology/EP evaluation and is not compatible with routine elective surgery.

Right Bundle Branch Block

RBBB produces a widened QRS with characteristic right-precordial morphology.

A stable chronic isolated RBBB in an asymptomatic patient generally does not require additional cardiac testing solely for elective surgery.
Compare with prior EKG whenever available. Consider further evaluation when RBBB is new and associated with symptoms or other concerning findings.

Left Bundle Branch Block

LBBB alters ventricular depolarization and makes standard ST-segment ischemia interpretation less reliable.

Under the TSJH perioperative pathway, LBBB requires pharmacologic nuclear stress testing such as Lexiscan/regadenoson or cardiology evaluation before elective surgery when adequate prior evaluation is not already available.
Exercise treadmill ECG alone is generally not the preferred ischemia test in LBBB because baseline conduction abnormality limits interpretation.

New LBBB

A newly identified LBBB deserves comparison with prior tracings and clinical assessment. If truly new, evaluate symptoms and obtain appropriate cardiovascular assessment before routine elective surgery.

Bifascicular Block

Bifascicular block commonly refers to RBBB combined with left anterior or left posterior fascicular block.

Stable asymptomatic chronic bifascicular block may not require intervention, but comparison with prior EKG and review for syncope or advanced conduction disease are important.
Bifascicular block with syncope, symptomatic bradycardia or higher-grade AV block warrants cardiology/EP evaluation.

Left Anterior Fascicular Block

Isolated LAFB may produce left-axis deviation without a widened QRS comparable to complete bundle branch block.

Stable isolated LAFB without symptoms or other cardiac abnormalities generally does not require preoperative cardiology evaluation by itself.

Low Voltage

Low QRS voltage may occur with obesity, COPD, pericardial effusion, hypothyroidism, infiltrative disease or technical factors.

Compare with prior tracing and clinical findings. Chronic low voltage in an otherwise stable patient is different from a new finding accompanied by hypotension, dyspnea or suspected pericardial disease.

Axis Deviation

Axis deviation should be interpreted together with conduction pattern, ventricular hypertrophy and prior tracings.

Stable isolated axis deviation without symptoms or additional concerning findings usually does not independently require preoperative cardiac testing.

Left Ventricular Hypertrophy

EKG criteria for LVH may reflect longstanding hypertension or structural heart disease.

LVH on EKG alone does not automatically require echocardiography if the patient is clinically stable and there is no other indication.
LVH plus heart-failure symptoms, significant murmur or suspected structural disease may justify further evaluation.

QTc Prolongation

QTc prolongation increases susceptibility to torsades de pointes, particularly with electrolyte abnormalities and QT-prolonging medications.

Marked QTc prolongation deserves medication and electrolyte review before surgery.

Review

  • Potassium
  • Magnesium
  • Calcium
  • Antiarrhythmics
  • Antipsychotics
  • Selected antidepressants
  • Methadone
  • Antiemetics and other QT-prolonging agents

Practical QTc Approach

Borderline QTc values should be interpreted using the clinical context and correction method rather than treated as an automatic cancellation trigger.
QTc around or above 500 ms generally deserves particular attention to reversible causes, interacting medications and perioperative arrhythmia risk.

Nonspecific ST-T Abnormalities

Nonspecific ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities are common.

Stable chronic nonspecific ST-T changes in an asymptomatic patient do not automatically require stress testing or cardiology referral.
Compare with previous tracings and determine whether symptoms or cardiovascular risk suggest an active ischemic process.

T-Wave Inversions

T-wave inversion may be normal in some leads or may reflect ischemia, ventricular hypertrophy, bundle branch block, pulmonary disease or other causes.

New, deep or dynamic T-wave inversion—especially with ischemic symptoms—requires additional evaluation.

ST Depression

ST depression may reflect ischemia, LVH strain, medication effect or other abnormal repolarization.

New horizontal/downsloping ST depression with compatible symptoms or significant clinical concern warrants ischemic evaluation.

ST Elevation

New ST elevation concerning for acute myocardial injury is an emergency and is not a routine preoperative optimization issue. The elective surgical pathway should stop while urgent cardiac evaluation occurs.

Stable known early repolarization or other chronic patterns should be compared with prior EKG and clinical history.

Pathologic Q Waves

Q waves may indicate prior myocardial infarction but can also reflect lead placement or normal variants depending on location and morphology.

A remote stable infarct pattern does not necessarily represent active ischemia. Review prior CAD history, symptoms and available cardiac testing.
A new infarct pattern or associated ischemic symptoms requires further evaluation.

Poor R-Wave Progression

Poor R-wave progression may occur because of prior anterior infarction, obesity, COPD, lead placement or normal variation.

This finding alone should not automatically be labeled “old anterior infarct” without correlation with prior tracings and clinical history.

Computer-Generated “Cannot Rule Out Infarct”

Automated statements such as “possible anterior infarct,” “cannot rule out inferior infarct,” or “septal infarct, age undetermined” are not definitive diagnoses.
Review QRS morphology, lead placement, prior EKGs, symptoms and known cardiac history before deciding that additional testing is necessary.

Paced Rhythm

Pacemaker-generated ventricular depolarization creates expected wide-QRS and secondary ST-T changes.

A paced rhythm should be interpreted in the context of device function and known underlying disease rather than labeled abnormal solely because the QRS is wide.
Unexpected bradycardia, failure to capture, new device symptoms or recent ICD therapy requires device evaluation.

Pre-Excitation / WPW Pattern

Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern may show a short PR interval, delta wave and widened QRS.

An asymptomatic remote WPW pattern with established evaluation may not require repeat testing before every procedure.
Recent symptomatic tachyarrhythmia, syncope or unstable pre-excitation requires cardiology/EP assessment.

Brugada Pattern

Suspected Brugada pattern or known Brugada syndrome requires anesthesia and cardiology/EP planning because fever and selected medications can increase arrhythmic risk.

EKG and Electrolytes

Electrolyte abnormalities can produce important EKG changes.

Abnormality Possible EKG Effect
Hyperkalemia Peaked T waves, PR prolongation, QRS widening, severe conduction disturbance.
Hypokalemia ST depression, flattened T waves, U waves, ventricular arrhythmia risk.
Hypomagnesemia QT-related ventricular arrhythmia risk.
Hypocalcemia QT prolongation.
Hypercalcemia QT shortening.
Treat clinically important electrolyte abnormalities rather than interpreting their EKG manifestations as isolated cardiac disease.

Always Compare With Prior EKG When Possible

Comparison is one of the most valuable steps in preoperative EKG interpretation. A chronic abnormality often has very different significance from a new one.

Compare

  • Rhythm
  • Rate
  • PR interval
  • QRS duration
  • Bundle branch pattern
  • Q waves
  • ST-T changes
  • QTc

Examples include:

New LBBB

New ischemic ST changes
New significant T-wave abnormalities
New pathologic Q-wave pattern

New ventricular arrhythmia

When Stress Testing Is NOT Automatically Required

Stress testing is not automatically required solely for:

Stable RBBB
Stable first-degree AV block
Occasional PACs/PVCs
Stable nonspecific ST-T changes
Stable controlled AF
Remote Q-wave pattern with adequate prior evaluation and no new symptoms

When Cardiology Consultation Is Appropriate

Consider cardiology for:

New LBBB
New significant ischemic changes
New or uncontrolled AF/flutter
High-grade AV block
Unexplained syncope
Significant ventricular arrhythmia
Recent ICD shock
New heart-failure symptoms
Other concerning EKG change that cannot be adequately resolved in the preoperative clinic

When EP Consultation Is Appropriate

  • High-grade conduction disease
  • Pacemaker dependence concerns
  • Device malfunction concern
  • Recent ICD therapy
  • Complex tachyarrhythmia
  • Symptomatic bradycardia
  • Unexplained syncope with conduction concern

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

  • Significant conduction disease
  • Marked QT prolongation
  • Pacemaker/ICD management issue
  • Known Brugada syndrome
  • Complex antiarrhythmic regimen
  • New rhythm abnormality with uncertain perioperative significance

Immediate Escalation

Stop the routine elective pathway and arrange urgent clinical evaluation for:

EKG changes concerning for acute MI
Unstable ventricular tachyarrhythmia
Complete heart block with instability
Symptomatic severe bradycardia
Arrhythmia with hypotension
EKG abnormality accompanied by active chest pain or acute heart-failure symptoms

Key Takeaways

  • Review the actual tracing rather than relying solely on the computer interpretation.
  • Use a systematic approach every time.
  • Compare with prior EKG whenever possible.
  • Stable chronic abnormalities are different from new abnormalities.
  • Occasional PACs/PVCs usually do not require further workup in an asymptomatic patient.
  • Stable first-degree AV block usually does not require further evaluation.
  • Stable isolated RBBB generally does not require stress testing.
  • LBBB requires special ischemic-assessment consideration under the TSJH pathway.
  • Mobitz II and complete heart block require additional evaluation.
  • Stable chronic AF may proceed when rate and anticoagulation plans are appropriate.
  • Nonspecific ST-T changes do not automatically require stress testing.
  • Automated “possible infarct” statements are not definitive diagnoses.
  • QT prolongation should prompt medication and electrolyte review.
  • New ischemic, conduction or rhythm abnormalities deserve additional assessment.
  • Active unstable cardiac findings require postponement of routine elective surgery.
