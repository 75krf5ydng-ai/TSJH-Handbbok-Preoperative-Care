Preoperative EKG Interpretation & Workup Thresholds

Practical recognition of findings that require action before elective noncardiac surgery

Purpose

The preoperative EKG should be interpreted in the context of symptoms, cardiovascular history, functional capacity, prior tracings and planned surgical risk. The purpose is to identify abnormalities that could represent active or previously unrecognized cardiovascular disease and determine whether they require additional evaluation before elective surgery.

An abnormal EKG does not automatically mean surgery should be postponed. The key questions are whether the finding is new, clinically significant, associated with symptoms and likely to change perioperative management.

Systematic EKG Review

Use a consistent sequence rather than focusing only on the automated interpretation.

Step Review
1 Heart rate
2 Rhythm
3 Cardiac axis
4 PR interval
5 QRS duration and morphology
6 QT / QTc interval
7 Q waves and evidence of prior infarction
8 ST segments
9 T-wave morphology
10 Comparison with prior EKG when available

Normal Sinus Rhythm

Normal sinus rhythm is characterized by a regular rhythm with a sinus P wave preceding each QRS complex and a consistent PR interval.

Normal sinus rhythm in an asymptomatic patient without other significant EKG abnormalities generally requires no additional cardiac testing solely for preoperative purposes.

Sinus Bradycardia

Sinus bradycardia may be physiologic or medication-related and is common in patients taking beta blockers or other rate-controlling medications.

Mild asymptomatic sinus bradycardia with stable blood pressure generally does not require additional preoperative cardiac testing.
Further evaluation is warranted when bradycardia is accompanied by syncope, presyncope, hypotension, exercise intolerance, high-grade conduction disease or another concerning clinical feature.

Sinus Tachycardia

Sinus tachycardia is usually a physiologic response rather than a primary arrhythmia. Determine the underlying cause.

Common Causes

  • Pain or anxiety
  • Dehydration
  • Anemia
  • Fever or infection
  • Hypoxemia
  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Medication or stimulant effect
  • Pulmonary disease
  • Heart failure
Persistent unexplained tachycardia should be evaluated rather than attributed automatically to anxiety.

Atrial Fibrillation

Determine whether atrial fibrillation is known or newly identified, whether ventricular rate is controlled and whether anticoagulation is indicated or already prescribed.

Review

  • Prior diagnosis and duration
  • Ventricular rate
  • Symptoms
  • Hemodynamic stability
  • Anticoagulation status
  • Recent cardiology evaluation
  • Heart failure or structural heart disease
Stable chronic atrial fibrillation with acceptable rate control does not necessarily require postponement of elective surgery.
Newly recognized atrial fibrillation, rapid ventricular response, hypotension, ischemic symptoms or decompensated heart failure requires further evaluation before elective surgery.

Atrial Flutter

Atrial flutter should be approached similarly to atrial fibrillation with attention to ventricular rate, symptoms, anticoagulation requirements and underlying structural heart disease.

New atrial flutter or poorly controlled ventricular response should be evaluated before elective surgery.

Premature Atrial Contractions

Isolated premature atrial contractions are common and frequently benign, particularly in asymptomatic patients.

Occasional PACs without symptoms or other significant abnormalities generally do not require additional preoperative evaluation.

Premature Ventricular Contractions

Isolated PVCs may occur in otherwise healthy patients. Determine whether they are new, frequent, multifocal or associated with symptoms or known structural heart disease.

Occasional asymptomatic PVCs do not automatically require stress testing or cardiology consultation.
Frequent ventricular ectopy, couplets, runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, syncope or suspected structural heart disease warrants further assessment.

First-Degree AV Block

First-degree AV block is characterized by PR prolongation with conduction of every atrial impulse to the ventricles.

Isolated first-degree AV block in an asymptomatic patient generally does not require additional preoperative evaluation.

Second-Degree AV Block

Mobitz I / Wenckebach

Mobitz I may be benign in selected patients, particularly when asymptomatic, but clinical context and medications should be reviewed.

Mobitz II

Mobitz II second-degree AV block is a significant conduction abnormality because of the potential for progression to complete heart block. Elective surgery should generally not proceed without appropriate cardiovascular evaluation.

Complete Heart Block

Complete or third-degree AV block is a major conduction abnormality. Elective surgery should be postponed pending urgent cardiovascular assessment and appropriate management unless the patient already has an appropriately functioning permanent pacing system and the situation has been fully evaluated.

Right Bundle Branch Block

An isolated right bundle branch block may be chronic and clinically benign. Compare with prior EKGs and consider associated symptoms and structural disease.

A stable asymptomatic chronic RBBB alone generally does not require additional ischemic testing.
New RBBB accompanied by chest pain, dyspnea, syncope or other concerning findings requires further evaluation.

Left Bundle Branch Block

Left bundle branch block can make standard EKG assessment for myocardial ischemia more difficult and may reflect underlying structural heart disease.

Under TSJH perioperative guidance, LBBB should prompt pharmacologic nuclear stress testing, such as regadenoson/Lexiscan when ischemic testing is appropriate, or cardiology evaluation before elective surgery.
Exercise treadmill ECG without imaging is not an appropriate ischemic assessment when baseline LBBB prevents reliable interpretation of ST segments.

Bifascicular Block

Bifascicular block usually consists of RBBB with either left anterior or left posterior fascicular block.

Chronic asymptomatic bifascicular block may not independently require postponement, but compare with previous tracings and assess for syncope, presyncope or additional AV conduction disease.
Bifascicular block with unexplained syncope or higher-grade AV block requires cardiovascular evaluation.

Left Ventricular Hypertrophy

EKG criteria for LVH commonly reflect longstanding hypertension or structural heart disease.

Isolated LVH on EKG in an asymptomatic patient does not automatically require echocardiography or stress testing. Evaluate blood pressure control, symptoms and whether structural disease is otherwise suspected.

Q Waves / Prior Myocardial Infarction Pattern

Pathologic Q waves may indicate prior myocardial infarction but should be correlated with history and previous EKGs.

A chronic stable infarct pattern in an asymptomatic patient does not automatically require additional ischemic testing if prior disease has been adequately evaluated and the patient is clinically stable.
A newly recognized infarct pattern, particularly with symptoms or new ST-T abnormalities, should prompt further cardiovascular assessment.

ST-Segment Elevation

New ST-segment elevation concerning for acute myocardial injury or acute coronary syndrome is an emergency finding and is not compatible with proceeding with routine elective surgery.

Benign early repolarization and other nonischemic causes exist, but acute ischemia must be excluded when the pattern is new or clinically suspicious.

ST-Segment Depression

ST depression may represent ischemia, strain, medication effect or other repolarization abnormalities.

New significant ST depression, particularly when associated with chest discomfort, dyspnea or reduced functional capacity, warrants further cardiovascular evaluation.

Nonspecific ST-T Wave Abnormalities

Nonspecific ST-T changes are frequently reported on automated EKG interpretations and are common in otherwise stable patients.

Mild nonspecific ST-T abnormalities in an asymptomatic patient, particularly when unchanged from a prior tracing, generally do not require automatic stress testing or cardiology consultation.
Compare with prior EKGs whenever available. A change from baseline is more clinically meaningful than an isolated automated phrase.

T-Wave Inversion

T-wave inversion may be a normal variant or may reflect ischemia, ventricular strain, conduction abnormality or other cardiac disease.

Chronic stable T-wave inversions without symptoms may not require further testing. New or dynamic T-wave inversion should be interpreted with the patient's symptoms and cardiac risk.

QTc Prolongation

QT prolongation increases susceptibility to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, particularly when combined with electrolyte abnormalities or QT-prolonging medications.

Review

  • Potassium
  • Magnesium
  • Calcium
  • Medication list
  • History of syncope
  • Congenital long-QT syndrome
  • Prior ventricular arrhythmia
Marked QTc prolongation, especially near or above approximately 500 ms, should prompt medication and electrolyte review and may require additional evaluation depending on the clinical context.

Low Voltage

Low-voltage QRS may occur with obesity, COPD, pericardial effusion, hypothyroidism or infiltrative disease.

Low voltage alone is not an indication for postponement. Investigate when it is new or accompanied by symptoms or other findings suggesting structural heart disease.

Axis Deviation

Left or right axis deviation may reflect conduction disease, ventricular hypertrophy or chronic cardiopulmonary disease.

Stable isolated axis deviation in an asymptomatic patient generally does not require additional preoperative workup.

Paced Rhythm

Identify whether the patient has a pacemaker, ICD, CRT device or other implanted cardiac rhythm device.

Review

  • Device type
  • Indication
  • Pacemaker dependence
  • Most recent device interrogation
  • Battery status when available
  • Electrocautery considerations
A paced EKG is not inherently abnormal for the patient. Perioperative planning should focus on device function and potential electromagnetic interference from the planned procedure.

Automated EKG Interpretation

Do not rely on the computer-generated interpretation alone. Automated systems commonly overcall infarction, chamber enlargement, axis abnormalities and nonspecific ST-T changes.

Review the actual tracing, compare with prior EKGs and correlate the result with the patient's clinical history.

Compare With Prior EKG

Prior EKG comparison is one of the most useful tools in preoperative assessment.

Finding Significance
Unchanged chronic abnormality Usually less concerning when the patient remains asymptomatic and clinically stable.
New abnormality Requires clinical correlation and may warrant additional assessment.
Dynamic ST-T changes Raise concern for active ischemia or another evolving cardiac process.
New conduction disease Determine severity and whether cardiology evaluation is needed.

Findings That Usually Do Not Require Additional Workup by Themselves

In an asymptomatic and clinically stable patient, these findings often do not require additional cardiac testing solely because surgery is planned:
  • Normal sinus rhythm
  • Mild sinus bradycardia
  • Occasional PACs
  • Occasional PVCs
  • First-degree AV block
  • Chronic isolated RBBB
  • Stable LVH pattern
  • Stable axis deviation
  • Mild nonspecific ST-T wave abnormalities
  • Chronic stable prior-infarct pattern already evaluated

Findings That Commonly Require Further Evaluation

Additional evaluation should be considered for:
  • New ischemic ST-segment changes
  • New significant T-wave abnormalities with clinical concern
  • New LBBB
  • LBBB requiring ischemic risk assessment under institutional criteria
  • Mobitz II AV block
  • Complete heart block
  • New atrial fibrillation or flutter
  • Rapid ventricular response
  • Frequent or complex ventricular ectopy
  • Nonsustained or sustained ventricular tachycardia
  • Marked QT prolongation
  • EKG abnormality associated with syncope
  • EKG abnormality associated with chest pain or unexplained dyspnea

When Stress Testing Is Appropriate

An abnormal EKG alone does not automatically justify stress testing.

Stress testing should generally be reserved for patients in whom estimated perioperative risk, symptoms, functional capacity and clinical risk factors make ischemic evaluation appropriate and when the test result would change management.

LBBB and Stress-Test Selection

When ischemic evaluation is necessary in a patient with LBBB, use an appropriate imaging strategy rather than a standard exercise ECG alone. Under TSJH guidance, pharmacologic nuclear stress testing such as Lexiscan or cardiology evaluation is appropriate before elective surgery.

When Echocardiography May Be Needed

An EKG abnormality should lead to echocardiography only when there is a clinical question that echocardiography can answer.

Examples

  • New or worsening heart-failure symptoms
  • Suspected significant valvular disease
  • Unexplained dyspnea
  • Suspected new ventricular dysfunction
  • Evidence suggesting clinically important structural heart disease

When Cardiology Consultation Is Appropriate

Consider cardiology evaluation for:
  • Active ischemic symptoms
  • New significant ischemic EKG changes
  • New or unexplained LBBB
  • High-grade AV block
  • Uncontrolled atrial arrhythmia
  • Complex ventricular arrhythmia
  • Syncope with concerning EKG findings
  • Suspected significant structural cardiac disease
  • Uncertainty regarding whether an EKG abnormality changes surgical risk

EKG and BNP Integration

EKG findings should be considered together with RCRI and institutional BNP screening criteria rather than as separate unrelated assessments.

BNP screening SmartPhrase:

BNP screening. Obtain BNP because of age greater than 65 years and RCRI score of 1. If BNP is 92 pg/mL or greater, implement the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.

Postoperative Cardiac Surveillance

Biomarker Institutional Threshold Postoperative Plan
BNP ≥92 pg/mL Postoperative EKG and daily troponins for approximately 48–72 hours.
NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L Postoperative EKG and daily troponins for approximately 48–72 hours.

Concise Physician Interpretation Examples

Example 1: Stable EKG

EKG demonstrates normal sinus rhythm without acute ischemic changes or clinically significant conduction abnormality. No additional cardiac testing is indicated based on the EKG alone in the absence of concerning symptoms or other high-risk clinical findings.

Example 2: Nonspecific T-Wave Abnormality

EKG demonstrates sinus rhythm with nonspecific T-wave abnormality. No acute ST-segment ischemic pattern identified. Compare with prior tracing when available. In the absence of active cardiac symptoms or significant interval change, this finding alone does not mandate additional ischemic testing.

Example 3: LBBB

EKG demonstrates left bundle branch block. Under TSJH perioperative guidance, patient requires appropriate ischemic risk assessment with pharmacologic nuclear stress testing when indicated or cardiology evaluation prior to elective surgery.

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is the patient symptomatic? Evaluate chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations or heart-failure symptoms before relying on the EKG alone.
Is the finding new? Compare with prior tracings whenever available.
Is there evidence of acute ischemia? Do not proceed with routine elective surgery until evaluated.
Is high-grade conduction disease present? Obtain cardiovascular evaluation.
Is an arrhythmia present? Determine chronicity, rate control, symptoms and anticoagulation needs.
Is LBBB present? Apply institutional LBBB pathway and determine whether cardiology or pharmacologic nuclear stress evaluation is needed.
Would further testing change management? Do not order stress testing or echocardiography reflexively.

Key Takeaways

  • Interpret the actual tracing rather than relying solely on the computer report.
  • Always compare with prior EKGs when available.
  • Stable nonspecific ST-T changes usually do not mandate additional testing.
  • Isolated chronic RBBB is usually less concerning than new conduction disease.
  • LBBB requires special attention under TSJH perioperative guidance.
  • Mobitz II and complete heart block require cardiovascular evaluation.
  • New atrial fibrillation requires assessment of rate, symptoms and anticoagulation.
  • Marked QT prolongation should trigger medication and electrolyte review.
  • New ischemic changes or EKG abnormalities with cardiac symptoms require further evaluation.
  • Order additional cardiac testing only when the result is expected to change management.
