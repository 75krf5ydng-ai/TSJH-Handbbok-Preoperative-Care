Cognition, falls, polypharmacy, functional decline, caregiver support and postoperative disposition planning

Purpose

Older adults may have increased perioperative vulnerability related to frailty, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, sensory loss, falls, reduced mobility and limited physiologic reserve.

Age alone should not determine surgical suitability. The more useful questions are how the patient functions, how frail they are, whether cognition is stable, what medications increase risk and what support will be available after surgery.

Frailty Is Not the Same as Age

A functionally independent 82-year-old may have better physiologic reserve than a highly dependent 68-year-old. Frailty should be assessed directly rather than assumed from chronological age.

Recent Functional Decline

Recent decline in mobility, self-care or cognition deserves evaluation rather than being attributed automatically to “getting older.”

Possible Causes

  • Acute illness
  • Anemia
  • Heart failure
  • Infection
  • Medication adverse effects
  • Neurologic disease
  • Deconditioning
  • Poor nutrition
  • Depression or other psychosocial factors

Polypharmacy

Polypharmacy increases the risk of medication interactions, falls, delirium, orthostasis, constipation and postoperative sedation.

The goal is not to stop every medication. Identify medications that create meaningful perioperative risk and determine whether they should be continued, adjusted or temporarily withheld.

Anticholinergic Burden

Anticholinergic medications can worsen confusion, urinary retention, constipation, dry mouth and falls in vulnerable older adults.

Potential Contributors

  • Diphenhydramine
  • Hydroxyzine
  • Oxybutynin
  • Tricyclic antidepressants
  • Cyclobenzaprine
  • Selected antipsychotic medications
When a muscle relaxant is clinically necessary, methocarbamol may be preferable to cyclobenzaprine in selected older or delirium-prone patients because of lower anticholinergic burden.

Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines may increase sedation, falls and delirium risk.

Do not abruptly discontinue chronic benzodiazepines in a dependent patient, because withdrawal can cause agitation, seizures and other serious complications.
Avoid adding unnecessary perioperative benzodiazepine exposure in high-risk older adults when safer alternatives are available.

Vision Impairment

Poor vision may increase falls, disorientation and postoperative delirium.

Encourage patients who normally wear glasses to have them available after surgery when safe and feasible.

Hearing Impairment

Hearing loss can contribute to communication errors and apparent confusion.

Ensure hearing aids are available when appropriate and communicate face-to-face in a quiet environment when possible.

Nutrition

Review

  • Recent weight loss
  • Poor appetite
  • Difficulty chewing
  • Dysphagia
  • Protein intake
  • Food insecurity
Unintentional weight loss and poor intake may indicate reduced physiologic reserve even when BMI is normal or elevated.

Sarcopenia

Loss of muscle strength and mass can occur with or without low body weight.

Difficulty rising from a chair, marked weakness, slow gait and progressive mobility loss may indicate reduced reserve and increased rehabilitation needs.

DASI in Older Adults

DASI: ___

Use DASI to assess self-reported functional capacity, but determine why the patient cannot perform an activity.

Severe arthritis or spine disease may reduce the DASI score because of pain, not cardiopulmonary limitation.

Urinary Symptoms

Baseline urinary function matters because postoperative urinary retention is common in older adults.

Review

  • BPH
  • Prior urinary retention
  • Bladder medications
  • Incontinence
  • Recurrent UTI

Constipation

Baseline constipation often worsens after surgery because of opioids, reduced mobility and dehydration.

For high-risk patients, establish a bowel regimen early rather than waiting for significant postoperative constipation or ileus.

Sleep and OSA

OSA is common in older adults and may increase sensitivity to postoperative opioids and sedatives.

Moderate Frailty

A CFS score of 6 indicates moderate frailty and should prompt proactive planning for mobility, fall prevention, delirium prevention and discharge support.
Moderate frailty does not automatically make surgery inappropriate, but it may make a routine rapid outpatient pathway unrealistic.

Decision-Making Capacity

Cognitive impairment does not automatically mean a patient lacks medical decision-making capacity.

Capacity is decision-specific and includes the ability to understand relevant information, appreciate consequences, reason about options and communicate a choice.
When capacity is genuinely uncertain, involve the appropriate clinician and legal surrogate process rather than assuming incapacity based on age or dementia diagnosis alone.

Advance Directives

Review advance directives when appropriate, particularly in older adults with significant comorbidity or frailty.

Depression and Social Isolation

Depression and social isolation may affect rehabilitation participation, nutrition, medication adherence and postoperative recovery.

When clinically apparent, identify significant psychosocial barriers and ensure the postoperative plan is realistic.

When Geriatrics / PCP Input May Be Helpful

Formal geriatric consultation is not required for every older surgical patient.

Consider Additional Support For

  • Complex frailty
  • Progressive cognitive decline
  • Multiple falls
  • Major polypharmacy concerns
  • Unclear functional baseline
  • Complex caregiver/disposition barriers

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

Examples

  • Severe frailty
  • Prior severe postoperative delirium
  • Marked cognitive vulnerability
  • Complex polypharmacy
  • High aspiration risk
  • Severe OSA with sedative sensitivity
  • High-risk outpatient disposition concern

When to Consider Delaying Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when there is:

Acute delirium
Unexplained rapid functional decline
Active untreated infection
Severe dehydration or malnutrition
Unsafe unresolved medication issue
Acute cardiopulmonary instability
No safe postoperative disposition for a patient requiring substantial support
Other acute geriatric instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When an appropriate perioperative plan exists, the following do not automatically require postponement:

Advanced age alone
Stable mild cognitive impairment
Stable dementia
CFS 4–5
Remote fall history
Use of cane or walker
Hearing or vision impairment
Living alone when reliable postoperative support has been arranged

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is the patient frail? Document CFS and plan mobility/disposition needs.
Is cognition stable? Differentiate chronic impairment from acute delirium.
Has the patient fallen recently? Assess gait, medications, orthostasis and home safety.
Is polypharmacy creating risk? Reduce unnecessary sedative/anticholinergic burden.
Are hearing/vision aids needed? Plan sensory support to reduce confusion and falls.
Is caregiver support adequate? Confirm before routine outpatient discharge planning.
Can the patient mobilize safely after surgery? Plan PT, walker use and possible rehab needs.
Is there acute decline? Evaluate the cause before proceeding with routine elective surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Chronological age alone should not determine surgical suitability.
  • Use CFS to assess frailty directly.
  • CFS 6 indicates moderate frailty and should influence postoperative planning.
  • Document baseline mobility and cognitive status.
  • Use AWOL-S when delirium risk assessment is clinically appropriate.
  • Acute confusion is not the same as chronic dementia.
  • Falls should trigger review of medications, orthostasis, gait and home safety.
  • Reduce unnecessary anticholinergic and sedative burden.
  • Do not abruptly stop chronic benzodiazepines in a dependent patient.
  • Sensory aids can reduce confusion and improve communication.
  • Confirm actual caregiver support and assistive devices before outpatient surgery.
  • Disposition planning should begin preoperatively.
  • Acute delirium or unexplained rapid functional decline may require postponement.
  • Stable cognitive impairment or walker use alone does not automatically require delay.
