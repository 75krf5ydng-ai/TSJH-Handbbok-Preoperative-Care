Purpose

Perioperative diabetes management should prevent hypoglycemia, severe hyperglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, euglycemic DKA, dehydration and aspiration risk while maintaining enough basal insulin to prevent metabolic decompensation.

The medication plan depends on diabetes type, medication class, fasting duration, renal function, procedure timing, insulin dependence and anesthesia risk.

Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 diabetes requires basal insulin even when the patient is fasting. Complete omission of basal insulin may precipitate ketosis or DKA.
The basal dose is often reduced rather than stopped. The exact adjustment should follow the anesthesia/facility diabetes protocol.

Type 2 Diabetes

Management varies according to oral medications, GLP-1 therapy, SGLT2 therapy, basal insulin use, fasting duration and current glucose control.

Do not use one blanket instruction for all patients with type 2 diabetes.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Examples

  • Semaglutide / Ozempic / Wegovy
  • Tirzepatide / Mounjaro / Zepbound
  • Dulaglutide / Trulicity
  • Liraglutide / Victoza / Saxenda

GLP-1 therapy may delay gastric emptying and can increase aspiration concern in selected patients.

GLP-1 — Lower-Risk Scenario

A patient on a stable dose without significant GI symptoms or known delayed gastric emptying may be able to proceed under the current anesthesia pathway without automatically requiring a prolonged medication hold.

GLP-1 — Higher-Risk Scenario

Higher aspiration concern may be present with:

Recent dose escalation
Significant nausea
Active vomiting
Marked bloating
Severe early satiety
Known gastroparesis
Other evidence of delayed gastric emptying
These patients should be reviewed with anesthesia before elective sedation or general anesthesia.

GLP-1 Day-of-Surgery Red Flags

Active vomiting, significant abdominal distention, severe nausea or other strong evidence of delayed gastric emptying should not be ignored simply because the patient followed fasting instructions.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

Examples

  • Empagliflozin / Jardiance
  • Dapagliflozin / Farxiga
  • Canagliflozin / Invokana
  • Ertugliflozin / Steglatro
SGLT2 inhibitors require a multi-day hold before elective surgery because of the risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

Why SGLT2 Inhibitors Matter

SGLT2-associated ketoacidosis can occur with only mild or moderate glucose elevation.

A normal or only mildly elevated glucose does not exclude euglycemic DKA.

Euglycemic DKA Red Flags

Recent SGLT2 inhibitor use plus:

Nausea
Vomiting
Abdominal pain
Malaise
Tachypnea
Unexplained anion-gap metabolic acidosis
Ketones
Unexpected dehydration
Otherwise unexplained metabolic illness

SGLT2 Medication Hold

Use the current facility/anesthesia medication-specific interval. Most SGLT2 agents require several days off therapy before elective surgery.
If the required hold was not completed, do not simply ignore the missed hold. Assess fasting duration, procedure, symptoms and anesthesia recommendations.

Restarting SGLT2 Therapy

Restart only after the patient is clinically stable, eating adequately and no longer at meaningful risk for perioperative ketosis/dehydration.

Metformin

Metformin alone usually has a low risk of hypoglycemia.

Follow the current facility day-of-surgery medication pathway and consider renal function, AKI, dehydration and acute illness.

Sulfonylureas

Examples

  • Glipizide
  • Glimepiride
  • Glyburide
Sulfonylureas may cause fasting hypoglycemia and are generally held on the day of surgery according to standard perioperative protocols.

DPP-4 Inhibitors

Examples

  • Sitagliptin
  • Linagliptin
  • Saxagliptin
These have relatively low hypoglycemia risk when used without insulin or a sulfonylurea. Follow facility-specific instructions.

Basal Insulin

Examples

  • Glargine
  • Detemir
  • Degludec
  • NPH
Basal insulin is generally reduced rather than completely omitted, especially in patients who are insulin-dependent.
Type 1 diabetes requires ongoing basal insulin.

Prandial Insulin

Rapid-acting insulin includes lispro, aspart and glulisine.

Prandial insulin is typically held when the patient is fasting, unless being used as correction insulin according to an established protocol.

Premixed Insulin

Premixed insulin preparations are more difficult to manage during fasting because they contain both basal/intermediate and prandial components.

Patients using premixed insulin should receive a specific day-of-surgery plan rather than a generic “take half” or “hold everything” instruction.

Insulin Pump

Do not remove an insulin pump from an insulin-dependent patient without an alternative basal insulin strategy.

Review

  • Pump type
  • Basal rate
  • Recent glucose control
  • Infusion site
  • Procedure duration
  • Whether the device can remain accessible
  • Anesthesia plan

Continuous Glucose Monitor

CGMs may remain useful perioperatively, but point-of-care glucose confirmation may still be needed for treatment decisions because perfusion, medications and rapid glucose changes can affect sensor reliability.

Day-of-Surgery Glucose

The acceptable glucose range should follow the current institutional perioperative glycemic pathway.

The more important issue is whether the patient has acute metabolic instability, rather than treating a single moderately elevated glucose as an automatic cancellation.

Hyperglycemia

Mild to moderate asymptomatic hyperglycemia is common before surgery.

Assess trend, A1c, medication adherence, acute illness and whether the patient is insulin-dependent.
Severe hyperglycemia associated with dehydration, ketones, acidosis or systemic illness requires further evaluation before elective surgery.

Hypoglycemia

Symptomatic or clinically significant hypoglycemia should be treated before anesthesia. Identify the cause and ensure glucose stability before proceeding.

DKA

Active diabetic ketoacidosis is incompatible with routine elective surgery. Treat metabolic decompensation before reconsidering the elective procedure.

HHS

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state requires acute treatment and stabilization. Elective surgery should not proceed during active HHS.

A1c

A1c reflects average glycemic exposure over approximately the previous 2–3 months.

An elevated A1c identifies increased chronic glycemic risk but does not by itself define acute metabolic instability.
Apply the surgeon/facility A1c threshold when one is established for elective joint, spine or implant procedures.

A1c Does Not Tell the Whole Story

A patient may have an elevated A1c but stable current glucose, or a relatively acceptable A1c with significant recent hyperglycemia.

Review both chronic control and current metabolic status.

Renal Disease and Diabetes Medications

CKD may affect insulin requirements and medication clearance.

Declining renal function can increase hypoglycemia risk in insulin-treated patients and may change the suitability of several diabetes medications.

Gastroparesis

Diabetic gastroparesis independently increases delayed gastric emptying and aspiration concern.

GLP-1 therapy plus known gastroparesis or significant GI symptoms should prompt particular anesthesia attention.

Procedure-Specific Considerations

Procedure Key Diabetes Concerns
Minor short procedure Fasting glucose, medication timing and hypoglycemia prevention.
Total joint arthroplasty Chronic glycemic control, infection risk, postoperative glucose, SGLT2 and GLP-1 management.
Spine fusion Implant/wound risk, prolonged fasting, postoperative steroids/glucose, medication holds.
Long revision surgery Extended fasting, insulin management, intraoperative glucose monitoring and higher metabolic stress.

When Endocrinology Is Appropriate

Consider endocrine input for:

Brittle type 1 diabetes
Recurrent DKA
Recurrent severe hypoglycemia
Complex insulin-pump management
Severe uncontrolled hyperglycemia
Unclear perioperative insulin strategy
Complex steroid-induced diabetes
Other difficult metabolic management

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

  • GLP-1 with significant GI symptoms
  • Known gastroparesis
  • Insulin pump that may remain during surgery
  • Long procedure with insulin dependence
  • Missed SGLT2 hold
  • Recent DKA
  • Complex metabolic history

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

DKA
Euglycemic DKA
HHS
Severe symptomatic hypoglycemia
Severe uncontrolled hyperglycemia with acute illness/dehydration
Unresolved significant GLP-1 aspiration concern
Acute metabolic instability
Other serious diabetic decompensation

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

Depending on current facility/surgeon thresholds and clinical stability:

Moderately elevated A1c
Mild asymptomatic fasting hyperglycemia
Stable type 2 diabetes
Stable insulin-treated diabetes with an established plan
GLP-1 therapy without significant GI symptoms
CGM use
Insulin pump use with an appropriate anesthesia plan

Key Takeaways

  • Type 1 diabetes requires basal insulin during fasting.
  • Do not tell every diabetic patient to hold all insulin.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors require multi-day interruption because of euglycemic DKA risk.
  • Normal glucose does not exclude SGLT2-associated euglycemic DKA.
  • GLP-1 management should include symptoms, dose escalation and gastroparesis risk.
  • Significant GI symptoms require anesthesia review.
  • Sulfonylureas are generally held on the day of surgery because of fasting hypoglycemia risk.
  • Insulin pumps require an explicit perioperative plan.
  • CGM values may need point-of-care confirmation.
  • Elevated A1c is not the same as acute metabolic instability.
  • DKA and HHS require treatment before elective surgery.
  • Renal dysfunction may substantially change insulin and medication risk.
  • Major implant surgery deserves particular attention to chronic glycemic control and infection risk.
