Purpose

Hematologic abnormalities can affect bleeding risk, transfusion exposure, thromboembolic risk, wound healing and postoperative recovery. The preoperative assessment should identify clinically significant anemia, platelet disorders, coagulopathy and known hematologic disease before major elective surgery.

The objective is not to normalize every hematologic value. Determine whether the abnormality is stable or progressive, whether it has a known cause, whether the planned procedure has meaningful blood-loss or neuraxial consequences and whether hematology input will change management.

Anemia

Anemia should be interpreted according to severity, symptoms, chronicity and the planned operation.

Mild stable anemia may be compatible with elective surgery, while progressive or significant unexplained anemia deserves further evaluation—particularly before major spine, revision arthroplasty or other procedures with meaningful expected blood loss.

Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant. A normal or elevated ferritin does not always exclude iron deficiency in inflammatory states.

Iron Deficiency Requires a Cause

Do not stop at the diagnosis of iron deficiency. Determine whether the patient has chronic menstrual loss, GI blood loss, malabsorption, poor intake, prior bariatric surgery or another explanation.

Oral vs IV Iron

The appropriate replacement strategy depends on anemia severity, iron deficit, time before surgery, oral tolerance and expected blood loss.

When surgery is soon and significant iron-deficiency anemia is present, IV iron may produce more timely repletion than oral therapy in selected patients.
Iron treatment should not delay investigation of unexplained blood loss when the underlying source may be clinically important.

Vitamin B12 and Folate Deficiency

Macrocytosis or appropriate clinical risk may prompt B12 and folate testing.

Neurologic symptoms associated with B12 deficiency are clinically important and should not be overlooked solely because the anemia is mild.

Anemia of CKD

CKD commonly produces normocytic anemia, but iron deficiency may coexist.

Review renal function, iron status, erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy and prior hemoglobin trends when significant CKD-associated anemia is present.

Hemolysis

Suspected hemolytic anemia requires targeted evaluation.

Potential Studies

  • Reticulocyte count
  • LDH
  • Haptoglobin
  • Indirect bilirubin
  • Peripheral smear
  • Direct antiglobulin testing when appropriate
Active unexplained hemolysis may require hematology evaluation before elective surgery.

Transfusion Is Not Based on One Number Alone

Transfusion decisions should account for symptoms, active bleeding, hemodynamics, cardiovascular disease, rate of hemoglobin decline and the clinical setting rather than using one universal hemoglobin threshold.
Preoperative transfusion solely to normalize a mildly reduced hemoglobin is not routinely appropriate.

History of Transfusion Reaction

Document the type and severity of prior transfusion reaction whenever known.

Previous transfusion does not automatically prohibit future transfusion, but known antibodies or serious reactions may require additional blood-bank planning.

Thrombocytopenia

The importance of thrombocytopenia depends on severity, cause and planned procedure.

Spine surgery, SCS, intrathecal procedures and neuraxial anesthesia have high bleeding consequences. Platelet adequacy for these procedures should follow procedure-specific anesthesia/surgical criteria.
Do not apply one platelet threshold to every operation.

Possible Causes of Thrombocytopenia

  • Medication effect
  • Liver disease / portal hypertension
  • Immune thrombocytopenia
  • Bone-marrow disease
  • Sepsis
  • Malignancy
  • Chemotherapy
  • Nutritional deficiency
  • Other hematologic disease

Immune Thrombocytopenia

Patients with known ITP should have current platelet trends and treatment status reviewed.

If the platelet count is below the threshold considered acceptable for the planned procedure, coordinate with hematology before elective surgery.

Thrombocytosis

Elevated platelets are often reactive but may occasionally reflect myeloproliferative disease.

Reactive Causes

  • Iron deficiency
  • Inflammation
  • Infection
  • Recent surgery
  • Splenectomy
Marked persistent unexplained thrombocytosis or known essential thrombocythemia requires individualized thrombotic and bleeding-risk review.

Leukopenia / Neutropenia

Review the absolute neutrophil count and cause of cytopenia.

Severe neutropenia may increase perioperative infection risk and may require hematology or oncology guidance before elective implant surgery.

Pancytopenia

Pancytopenia should not be treated as three unrelated mild abnormalities. Consider marrow disease, medication toxicity, liver/splenic disease, nutritional deficiency or other systemic pathology.

Unexplained pancytopenia generally deserves hematology evaluation before major elective surgery.

PT / INR Abnormality

An elevated INR may reflect warfarin therapy, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency or other coagulation abnormalities.

Unexpected clinically significant INR elevation requires explanation before high-bleeding-risk surgery.

aPTT Abnormality

A prolonged aPTT may result from heparin, factor deficiencies, inhibitors or a lupus anticoagulant.

A lupus anticoagulant may prolong the aPTT without implying increased bleeding risk. Clinical history and the underlying diagnosis matter.
Unexpected significant prolongation should be investigated before procedures where bleeding consequences are substantial.

von Willebrand Disease

Patients with known von Willebrand disease require a procedure-specific hemostatic plan.

Do not rely solely on normal routine coagulation studies in a patient with known von Willebrand disease. Hematology guidance may include DDAVP, factor replacement or other measures depending on disease type and procedure.

Hemophilia and Factor Deficiencies

Known hemophilia or clinically significant clotting-factor deficiency requires hematology-directed perioperative factor replacement and should not be managed with routine preoperative pathways alone.

Thrombophilia

Known thrombophilia should be documented specifically.

Examples

  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Antithrombin deficiency
  • Protein C deficiency
  • Protein S deficiency
  • Factor V Leiden
  • Prothrombin gene mutation
Thrombophilia does not automatically require bridging. The clinical history of actual thrombosis is essential.

Antiphospholipid Syndrome

APS may confer substantial arterial and venous thrombotic risk.

Complex APS, particularly with recurrent thrombosis, arterial events or chronic warfarin therapy, often warrants hematology/anticoagulation specialist input.

Inherited Thrombophilia Without Prior VTE

A positive inherited-thrombophilia diagnosis without a history of thrombosis does not automatically mean therapeutic anticoagulation or perioperative bridging is indicated.

Use the patient's actual thrombotic history and current indication for therapy.

Sickle Cell Disease

Patients with sickle cell disease may have risks related to anemia, vaso-occlusive crisis, hypoxemia, dehydration and organ dysfunction.

Major elective surgery in sickle cell disease generally requires coordinated hematology and anesthesia planning.

Review

  • Baseline hemoglobin
  • Prior acute chest syndrome
  • Recent crisis
  • Transfusion history
  • Hydroxyurea therapy
  • Cardiopulmonary disease
  • Renal disease

Sickle Cell Trait

Sickle cell trait is not equivalent to sickle cell disease and should not be managed as though the patient has chronic sickle-cell anemia.

Polycythemia / Erythrocytosis

Marked erythrocytosis may increase thrombotic risk depending on the underlying cause.

Known polycythemia vera or significant unexplained erythrocytosis may require hematology input before elective surgery.

Myeloproliferative Disorders

Essential thrombocythemia, polycythemia vera and related disorders may create both thrombotic and bleeding risks.

Review cytoreductive therapy, aspirin use, blood-count targets and the treating hematologist's perioperative recommendations.

Active Hematologic Malignancy

Leukemia, lymphoma, myeloma and other hematologic malignancies may affect anemia, platelets, neutrophils, infection risk and thrombosis.

Patients receiving active chemotherapy or with significant cytopenias should have oncology/hematology input when those factors may affect elective surgical timing.

Blood Refusal / Jehovah's Witness Planning

Some patients decline allogeneic blood transfusion for religious or personal reasons. The exact products and procedures acceptable to the patient vary.

Do not assume that every patient who declines blood accepts or refuses the same blood fractions, cell-salvage techniques or hemostatic products. Obtain the patient's specific informed choices.

Blood Conservation

Potential Strategies

  • Identify and treat preoperative anemia
  • Correct iron deficiency when feasible
  • Minimize unnecessary phlebotomy
  • Optimize anticoagulant/antiplatelet management
  • Consider surgical blood-conservation techniques
  • Consider cell salvage when appropriate and acceptable
  • Use procedure-specific hemostatic strategies
  • Plan for postoperative blood conservation
Blood-conservation planning is especially important before major spine surgery, revision arthroplasty and other procedures with substantial expected blood loss.

Tranexamic Acid

Tranexamic acid is used in many orthopedic and other surgical pathways to reduce blood loss.

TXA use should follow the surgeon/anesthesia protocol and account for relevant contraindications or thrombotic history.

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Severe symptomatic anemia
Progressive unexplained anemia with major expected blood loss
Active clinically significant bleeding
Severe thrombocytopenia below procedural safety requirements
Uncontrolled bleeding disorder without a hemostatic plan
Severe unexplained coagulopathy
Acute sickle-cell crisis
Severe neutropenia with infection risk for implant surgery
Unresolved blood-refusal plan before high-blood-loss surgery
Other acute hematologic instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When clinically stable and acceptable for the planned procedure, the following do not automatically require postponement:

Mild chronic anemia
Stable iron deficiency already being treated
Mild reactive thrombocytosis
Stable chronic thrombocytopenia above procedure-specific requirements
Remote VTE history with established anticoagulation plan
Known inherited thrombophilia without active thrombosis
Sickle cell trait

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is anemia present? Classify by MCV and determine likely cause.
Is anemia stable? Compare prior hemoglobin values and assess symptoms.
What is expected surgical blood loss? Use procedure-specific risk to determine urgency of optimization.
Is platelet count acceptable for this procedure? Use procedure-specific anesthesia/surgical criteria.
Are PT/INR or aPTT unexpectedly abnormal? Identify medication, liver disease, inhibitor or factor-related cause.
Is there a known bleeding disorder? Establish a hematology-directed hemostatic plan.
Is thrombophilia present? Review actual thrombosis history and anticoagulation indication.
Does the patient refuse blood? Document exact preferences and establish conservation strategy before major surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Interpret anemia according to severity, cause, trend and expected surgical blood loss.
  • Use MCV to guide the initial anemia differential.
  • Ferritin can be misleading when inflammation is present.
  • Iron deficiency should prompt consideration of the underlying source.
  • Do not transfuse solely to normalize a mildly reduced hemoglobin.
  • Platelet requirements depend on the planned procedure and bleeding consequence.
  • Spine, SCS, intrathecal and neuraxial procedures require particular attention to hemostasis.
  • INR in liver disease does not completely predict bleeding risk.
  • A clinical bleeding history matters even when PT/aPTT are normal.
  • Known von Willebrand disease or factor deficiency requires a specific hemostatic plan.
  • Thrombophilia does not automatically mean bridging is necessary.
  • Sickle cell disease and sickle cell trait are not equivalent.
  • Document exact blood-product preferences when transfusion is declined.
  • High-blood-loss surgery requires early blood-conservation planning.
