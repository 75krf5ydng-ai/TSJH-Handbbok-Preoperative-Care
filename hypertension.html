Preoperative Hypertension & Blood Pressure Management

Repeat measurement, medication review, orthostasis and when elective surgery should be delayed

Home Evaluation Cardiac Medications Labs Communication

Purpose

Elevated blood pressure is common during preoperative evaluation and may reflect chronic hypertension, pain, anxiety, medication nonadherence, incorrect measurement technique or acute illness.

A single elevated office blood pressure does not automatically require cancellation of elective surgery. Confirm the measurement, assess symptoms, review chronic control and identify whether there is evidence of acute hypertensive target-organ injury.

First Step: Confirm the Blood Pressure

Unexpectedly high or low blood pressure should be repeated before making a perioperative decision.

Correct Measurement Technique

Confirm

  • Patient has been seated quietly when feasible
  • Back is supported
  • Feet are supported rather than dangling
  • Arm is supported at approximately heart level
  • Correct cuff size is used
  • Cuff is placed on bare skin when possible
  • Recent activity is considered
  • Pain and anxiety are assessed
An undersized blood-pressure cuff can falsely elevate the measured blood pressure, particularly in patients with larger arm circumference.

Ask Whether the Patient Is Symptomatic

Urgent assessment is appropriate when severe hypertension is accompanied by symptoms or findings suggesting acute target-organ injury, including:

Chest pain or pressure
Acute shortness of breath
Pulmonary edema
New focal neurologic symptoms
Acute confusion
Severe concerning headache with neurologic findings
Visual disturbance concerning for hypertensive emergency
Acute kidney injury
Other evidence of acute cardiovascular or neurologic injury

Hypertensive Emergency

Hypertensive emergency is severe blood-pressure elevation associated with acute target-organ injury.

Suspected hypertensive emergency requires urgent medical evaluation and is not compatible with routine elective surgery.

Severe Asymptomatic Hypertension

Markedly elevated blood pressure without acute target-organ injury should be distinguished from hypertensive emergency.

Repeat the blood pressure after appropriate rest, verify medication adherence, review previous measurements and assess contributing factors before determining whether surgical delay is necessary.

Avoid rapid aggressive blood-pressure reduction solely to achieve a number in an otherwise stable asymptomatic patient, unless there is a specific clinical indication.

Practical Preoperative Blood Pressure Framework

Finding General Approach
Mild-to-moderate elevation Repeat measurement, review chronic control and usually proceed if otherwise clinically stable.
Marked elevation that improves with rest/recheck Document repeat value and assess medication adherence/pain/anxiety.
Persistent severe asymptomatic elevation Individualize according to procedure, baseline BP, comorbidities and anesthesia or facility guidance.
Severe elevation with target-organ symptoms Urgent evaluation; do not proceed with routine elective surgery.
Significant hypotension Determine cause before elective surgery.

Common 180/110 mmHg Reference Point

A systolic blood pressure around 180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure around 110 mmHg is commonly used as a point at which elective surgical timing deserves additional consideration.

Do not treat 180/110 mmHg as a rigid universal cancellation rule. Repeat the measurement and consider chronic baseline, symptoms, target-organ injury, procedure urgency, surgical risk and anesthesia/facility policy.
Persistent blood pressure at or above this range should generally trigger provider review and often anesthesia discussion before routine elective surgery.

Do Not Cancel for One Isolated Reading

When the patient is asymptomatic and a repeat properly obtained pressure is substantially improved, the initial elevated value may reflect anxiety, pain or measurement conditions rather than uncontrolled severe hypertension.

Document both the initial and repeat measurements rather than deleting or ignoring the original value.

Review the Patient's Baseline

Ask

  • What are usual home blood pressures?
  • What were recent PCP or cardiology values?
  • Is hypertension usually well controlled?
  • Was today's medication taken?
  • Has the regimen recently changed?
  • Has the patient missed doses?
A value that represents a major departure from a patient's usual blood pressure may deserve more attention than a similar value in a patient with longstanding poorly controlled hypertension.

White-Coat Effect

Some patients have substantially higher office blood pressures than home measurements.

When white-coat hypertension is suspected, review credible home or longitudinal office measurements if available. Do not assume white-coat effect without supporting history.

Pain and Blood Pressure

Severe pain may transiently increase heart rate and blood pressure.

For patients presenting with severe spine or joint pain, repeat the blood pressure after rest and symptom stabilization when appropriate before labeling the hypertension uncontrolled.

Anxiety and Blood Pressure

Preoperative anxiety may temporarily increase blood pressure.

A quiet setting, repeat measurement and reassurance may provide a more accurate assessment than reacting immediately to the first elevated reading.

Abrupt interruption of some chronic antihypertensive medications, particularly beta blockers and clonidine, may cause rebound hypertension or tachycardia.

Beta Blockers

Chronic beta-blocker therapy should generally be continued perioperatively unless a clinical contraindication exists.
Avoid abrupt withdrawal because rebound tachycardia, hypertension and ischemic stress may occur.

Clonidine

Patients taking chronic clonidine may develop rebound hypertension if therapy is suddenly interrupted.

Do not abruptly discontinue chronic clonidine solely because surgery is planned. Follow anesthesia/facility instructions for perioperative administration.

ACE Inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitor and ARB management depends on the medication indication and institutional anesthesia pathway.

When prescribed primarily for hypertension, temporary withholding before surgery may reduce intraoperative hypotension in selected patients. Management may differ when the drug is part of heart-failure guideline-directed therapy.

Follow current anesthesia and institutional instructions rather than applying one rule to every patient.

Calcium Channel Blockers

Calcium channel blockers are commonly continued unless bradycardia, hypotension or another clinical concern warrants adjustment.

Diuretics

Diuretic management should consider the indication, potassium, renal function and volume status.

A patient taking a diuretic only for hypertension may be managed differently from a patient who requires it to maintain euvolemia in heart failure.

Resistant Hypertension

Resistant hypertension generally refers to blood pressure remaining above goal despite treatment with multiple appropriately selected medications, typically including a diuretic.

Consider

  • Medication adherence
  • Accurate BP technique
  • NSAID use
  • Stimulants
  • Untreated OSA
  • CKD
  • Primary aldosteronism
  • Other secondary causes
Preoperative clinic may identify resistant hypertension, but extensive longitudinal secondary-hypertension evaluation can usually be coordinated with PCP/cardiology unless immediate perioperative safety is affected.

NSAIDs and Blood Pressure

NSAIDs may worsen blood pressure control, fluid retention and renal function in susceptible patients.

Review NSAID use when hypertension, edema, CKD or heart failure appears to have worsened.

Stimulants and Decongestants

Prescription stimulants, sympathomimetic decongestants and other agents may contribute to tachycardia or hypertension.

Medication reconciliation should include over-the-counter cold medications and weight-loss/stimulant products when unexplained blood-pressure elevation is present.

OSA and Hypertension

Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is associated with hypertension and may contribute to resistant blood-pressure elevation.

STOP-BANG: ___
In patients with OSA, assess PAP use or intolerance and incorporate respiratory risk into the perioperative plan rather than focusing only on the blood pressure.

Blood Pressure and CKD

Hypertension and renal disease frequently coexist.

Review

  • Creatinine trend
  • eGFR
  • Potassium
  • Volume status
  • ACE/ARB use
  • Diuretic therapy
Severe hypertension with acute renal deterioration requires evaluation for target-organ injury rather than routine outpatient optimization alone.

Blood Pressure and Heart Failure

An elevated blood pressure in heart failure should be interpreted with volume status, dyspnea, edema and current GDMT.

Hypertension with acute pulmonary edema or decompensated heart failure is an active cardiovascular problem and elective surgery should be postponed.

Hypotension

Unexpected low blood pressure can be as important as hypertension.

Potential Causes

  • Dehydration
  • Overdiuresis
  • Bleeding
  • Infection
  • Medication effect
  • Arrhythmia
  • Heart failure
  • Adrenal insufficiency
Persistent unexplained hypotension or hemodynamic instability should be evaluated before elective surgery.

Orthostatic Hypotension

Orthostatic hypotension should be interpreted with symptoms, medications, hydration and autonomic conditions.

Review diuretics, vasodilators, alpha blockers, dehydration, anemia, Parkinson disease and other autonomic disorders when orthostasis is present.

When PCP Follow-Up Is Appropriate

Newly identified chronic hypertension may require longitudinal management even when it does not prevent the planned operation.

Distinguish between “needs long-term hypertension follow-up” and “must be controlled before surgery.” These are not always the same decision.

PCP Follow-Up May Be Appropriate For

  • Repeated outpatient BP above chronic treatment goal
  • Need for long-term medication adjustment
  • Suspected white-coat hypertension requiring confirmation
  • Possible secondary hypertension
  • Home BP monitoring plan

When Cardiology Evaluation May Be Appropriate

Consider cardiology when hypertension is accompanied by:

Active ischemic symptoms
Heart-failure decompensation
Significant arrhythmia
Unexplained syncope
Complex cardiomyopathy
Severe valvular disease
Other significant cardiovascular instability

When to Contact Anesthesia

Anesthesia review is reasonable when blood pressure remains markedly elevated despite repeat measurement or when there is uncertainty about the acceptable range for the planned procedure.

Examples

  • Persistent BP near/above approximately 180/110 mmHg
  • Large deviation from usual baseline
  • Complex cardiovascular disease
  • High-risk or prolonged surgery
  • Medication-management uncertainty
  • Significant orthostatic hypotension

When to Consider Delaying Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when there is:

Hypertensive emergency
Acute target-organ injury
Severe hypertension with active cardiac symptoms
Severe hypertension with new neurologic symptoms
Acute pulmonary edema / decompensated HF
Persistent severe BP elevation outside anesthesia/facility parameters
Significant unexplained hypotension
Other hemodynamic instability requiring further evaluation

Findings That Often Do Not Require Cancellation by Themselves

Depending on clinical context, the following may not independently require postponement:

Mild chronic hypertension
Moderate asymptomatic BP elevation
Single high office reading that improves on repeat
White-coat elevation supported by stable home readings
Chronic hypertension requiring routine PCP follow-up without acute instability

Decision Framework

Question Action
Was the BP measured correctly? Repeat using appropriate technique and cuff size.
Is the patient symptomatic? Evaluate immediately for target-organ injury when concerning symptoms are present.
Is the value near the patient's usual baseline? Use longitudinal data to guide interpretation.
Were medications missed? Identify adherence issue and avoid abrupt withdrawal of chronic therapy.
Did BP improve after rest/recheck? Document repeat value and reassess need for further intervention.
Is BP persistently severe? Review anesthesia/facility criteria and determine whether delay is appropriate.
Is there acute target-organ injury? Urgent evaluation; do not proceed with routine elective surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not make a surgical decision from one isolated blood-pressure reading.
  • Repeat unexpectedly abnormal blood pressure using correct technique.
  • Use the correct cuff size.
  • Assess for acute target-organ injury before labeling severe hypertension as asymptomatic.
  • Approximately 180/110 mmHg is a common review threshold, not an absolute universal cancellation rule.
  • Consider the patient's usual home and office blood pressures.
  • Pain, anxiety and missed medications can transiently raise blood pressure.
  • Avoid abrupt withdrawal of chronic beta blockers or clonidine.
  • ACE inhibitor/ARB management depends on indication and anesthesia protocol.
  • New chronic hypertension may need PCP follow-up without necessarily delaying surgery.
  • Hypertensive emergency, decompensated heart failure or other acute target-organ injury requires postponement.
  • Unexpected hypotension and orthostasis also deserve preoperative evaluation.
