Preoperative Infection Screening & Optimization

Active infection, skin integrity, MRSA screening and implant-related considerations

Purpose

Preoperative infection assessment is especially important before procedures involving prosthetic joints, spinal instrumentation or other implanted hardware. The objective is to identify clinically meaningful infection without delaying surgery for insignificant laboratory or colonization findings.

The key distinction is between active infection that may increase perioperative risk and colonization or minor abnormalities that can be managed according to procedure-specific protocols.

Skin and Operative-Site Assessment

Skin integrity is particularly important when an implant or hardware is planned. Inspect or inquire about lesions near the operative extremity, spine or intended incision.

Active cellulitis, purulent drainage, abscess or an infected wound near the planned operative site generally warrants surgical review and treatment before elective implantation.

Important Findings

  • Cellulitis
  • Abscess
  • Purulent drainage
  • Open ulcer
  • Pressure injury
  • Diabetic foot ulcer
  • Infected abrasion
  • Significant rash at the operative site
  • Recent wound dehiscence

Minor Skin Findings

Not every superficial lesion requires cancellation.

A small healing abrasion or noninfected lesion distant from the operative field may be acceptable after evaluation. Document location, appearance and absence of infection when clinically relevant.
When hardware implantation is planned, uncertainty about whether a skin lesion is infected should be discussed with the surgeon before the procedure date.

Remote Skin Infection

Infection distant from the operative site still deserves assessment because bacteremia or systemic infection may create additional risk.

Active significant cellulitis, abscess or another untreated bacterial infection should generally be resolved before elective implant surgery.

MRSA / MSSA Screening

Some orthopedic and spine programs screen for nasal colonization with methicillin-resistant or methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus before implant surgery.

MRSA/MSSA screening and decolonization should follow the facility and surgeon's approved protocol. Colonization is different from active infection.

Positive MRSA/MSSA Screening

A positive colonization screen does not necessarily mean the patient has an active infection.

When the patient is otherwise clinically well, follow the approved decolonization and perioperative antibiotic pathway rather than automatically cancelling surgery solely because of colonization.

Common Protocol Elements May Include

  • Intranasal decolonization agent
  • Chlorhexidine bathing or wipes
  • Procedure-specific perioperative antibiotic selection
  • Documentation that the protocol was completed

Chlorhexidine Skin Preparation

Patients may be instructed to use chlorhexidine-based bathing or wipes before selected procedures.

Patient instructions should specify when and how the product is used and any areas where it should not be applied. Follow the facility's approved patient preparation protocol.

Respiratory Infection

The impact of a respiratory infection depends on severity, symptoms and underlying pulmonary disease.

Finding General Approach
Mild resolving upper-respiratory symptoms Individualize according to anesthesia, symptoms and procedure.
Active significant wheezing Optimize before elective surgery.
Pneumonia Treat and reassess after clinical recovery.
New oxygen requirement Further evaluation before elective surgery.
Fever with respiratory symptoms Evaluate for active infection before proceeding.

Urinary Infection

An abnormal urinalysis or asymptomatic bacteriuria is not equivalent to symptomatic urinary infection.
Symptomatic UTI, pyelonephritis, fever from a urinary source or urosepsis should be treated and surgical timing reassessed.

See the dedicated Urinalysis & Urine Culture chapter for the full decision pathway.

Dental Infection

Routine dental evaluation is not required before every orthopedic or spine operation. However, active odontogenic infection should not be ignored.

Dental abscess, facial swelling, purulent drainage, fever or another active significant dental infection should be treated before elective implant surgery when feasible.
Routine dental cleaning, chronic stable dental disease or the absence of recent dental evaluation should not automatically be treated as active infection. Apply surgeon/facility requirements when they exist.

Diabetic Foot and Chronic Ulcers

Chronic wounds require assessment for infection, healing status and proximity to the operative field.

An actively infected diabetic foot ulcer, draining ulcer or osteomyelitis requires further evaluation and is generally incompatible with routine elective implant surgery until appropriately treated.
A chronic noninfected wound may still require surgeon review depending on wound location, healing status, vascular supply and planned procedure.

Recent Antibiotic Therapy

Do not assume that a patient is optimized simply because antibiotics were prescribed.

Determine

  • What infection was being treated?
  • What antibiotic was used?
  • When did treatment begin and end?
  • Are symptoms resolved?
  • Was culture data available?
  • Is additional follow-up needed?

Leukocytosis

An elevated white blood cell count alone does not prove active infection.

Interpret leukocytosis with symptoms, fever, medications, smoking, inflammatory conditions and prior trends.
Marked unexplained leukocytosis accompanied by fever, hemodynamic instability or symptoms of infection warrants further evaluation before elective surgery.

Inflammatory Markers

ESR and CRP should not be ordered routinely for every preoperative patient. They are most useful when there is a specific clinical question.

Potential Indications

  • Suspected prosthetic joint infection
  • Concern for discitis or osteomyelitis
  • Unexplained inflammatory syndrome
  • Specialist-directed evaluation
An isolated elevated ESR or CRP is nonspecific and should not be interpreted as proof of infection.

Previous Prosthetic Joint Infection

Patients with a history of prosthetic joint infection require careful review of the previous treatment course and current status.

Document the involved joint, organism when known, surgical treatment, antimicrobial course, current suppressive therapy and whether infectious-disease or orthopedic follow-up remains active.
If recurrent or unresolved prosthetic infection is suspected, obtain the appropriate orthopedic/infectious-disease evaluation before elective surgery.

History of Spine Hardware Infection

A previous spinal surgical-site or hardware infection should be distinguished from an active infection.

Review

  • Date of infection
  • Organism
  • Hardware retained or removed
  • Antibiotic duration
  • Current suppressive therapy
  • Current drainage, redness or wound symptoms
  • Specialist follow-up

Immunosuppression

Immunosuppressed patients may have atypical manifestations of infection and higher infection-related risk.

Examples

  • Active chemotherapy
  • Biologic immune-modulating therapy
  • Chronic systemic corticosteroids
  • Transplant immunosuppression
  • Selected rheumatologic therapies
Determine whether medication interruption is necessary and who is directing the plan. Do not discontinue essential immunosuppressive therapy without reviewing the indication and specialist recommendations.

Rheumatologic Biologic Therapy

Biologic agents and other disease-modifying medications require individualized perioperative management.

Medication management should follow the current rheumatology, surgeon and institutional pathway. The timing of the last dose may depend on the specific agent and dosing cycle.

When Infectious Disease Consultation May Be Appropriate

Consider infectious-disease evaluation for:

Recurrent or unresolved prosthetic joint infection
Spinal hardware infection
Chronic osteomyelitis
Complex resistant organism history
Long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy
Unexplained recurrent infection
Complex immunocompromised patient with infection concern
Unclear perioperative antimicrobial plan

Reasons to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Active systemic infection
Sepsis or bacteremia
Active pneumonia
Symptomatic pyelonephritis
Active significant cellulitis
Draining abscess
Infected wound near operative site
Active osteomyelitis
Uncontrolled prosthetic joint or hardware infection
Significant acute dental infection
Other untreated infection likely to materially increase operative risk

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Cancellation

When the patient is otherwise clinically stable, the following findings do not automatically establish active infection or require cancellation by themselves:

MRSA/MSSA colonization
Asymptomatic bacteriuria
Mild isolated leukocytosis
Elevated ESR/CRP without clinical correlation
Small noninfected healing abrasion
Remote history of prior infection that has resolved

Decision Framework

Question Action
Does the patient have infection symptoms? Evaluate source and severity.
Is infection systemic? Treat and postpone routine elective surgery.
Is an infected lesion near the operative site? Obtain surgeon review and treat before implantation when appropriate.
Is the finding colonization only? Follow decolonization/prophylaxis protocol rather than labeling as active infection.
Is an abnormal UA present without symptoms? Apply the asymptomatic bacteriuria pathway.
Has recent treatment been completed? Confirm clinical improvement rather than relying solely on antibiotic completion.
Is infection complexity beyond routine SPA management? Coordinate with surgeon, PCP or infectious disease as appropriate.

Key Takeaways

  • Screen for active infection rather than treating every abnormal test as infection.
  • Skin integrity matters particularly before hardware or prosthetic implantation.
  • MRSA/MSSA colonization is different from active infection.
  • Follow facility decolonization protocols when screening is positive.
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria generally does not equal symptomatic UTI.
  • Active pneumonia, systemic infection and significant infected wounds usually warrant postponement.
  • Evaluate significant dental infection before elective implant surgery.
  • Leukocytosis and inflammatory markers are nonspecific without clinical correlation.
  • Clarify the indication and response whenever recent antibiotics are reported.
  • Use focused infectious-disease consultation for complex or unresolved infection.
