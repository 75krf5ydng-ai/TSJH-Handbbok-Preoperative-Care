CBC, BMP/CMP, glucose, A1c, coagulation, liver tests and practical perioperative follow-up

Purpose

Preoperative laboratory testing should identify abnormalities that may affect anesthesia, surgical blood loss, medication management, wound healing, cardiovascular stability or postoperative recovery.

The objective is not to normalize every value before surgery. Determine whether an abnormality is new or chronic, clinically significant, reversible and likely to change perioperative management.

General Laboratory Review

Question Why It Matters
Is the result truly abnormal? Confirm laboratory reference range and consider specimen error.
Is it new or chronic? Compare with prior values whenever available.
Is the patient symptomatic? Symptoms often determine urgency more than a mild isolated abnormality.
Could medication explain it? Diuretics, ACE/ARB therapy, anticoagulants, steroids and other drugs may alter labs.
Could dehydration or acute illness explain it? Volume status may affect BUN, creatinine, sodium, hemoglobin and glucose.
Would repeating the test change management? Repeat testing should have a clear purpose.

Critical Results

Critical laboratory values should be managed according to the laboratory's critical-result policy and the patient's clinical status. Do not rely on a handbook threshold when the laboratory has issued a critical alert or the patient is symptomatic.

Severe electrolyte abnormalities, profound anemia, severe thrombocytopenia, significant coagulopathy, marked hyperglycemia with metabolic decompensation or acute renal deterioration may require urgent assessment and postponement of elective surgery.

Hemoglobin

The significance of anemia depends on the degree of reduction, symptoms, chronicity, cardiac disease and anticipated blood loss.

A mildly low stable hemoglobin does not automatically require surgical delay. Major spine surgery, revision procedures and arthroplasty deserve greater attention because expected blood loss may be substantial.
New, progressive, symptomatic or otherwise unexplained significant anemia should be evaluated before elective surgery.

MCV Interpretation

Pattern Possible Causes
Low MCV Iron deficiency, chronic blood loss, thalassemia or selected chronic inflammatory states.
Normal MCV CKD, chronic inflammation, acute blood loss, early iron deficiency or mixed anemia.
High MCV B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, medication effect or marrow disease.

Platelet Count

Platelet adequacy depends on procedure type, bleeding consequence and whether neuraxial anesthesia or neuraxial access is planned.

Significant thrombocytopenia requires individualized assessment before spine, SCS, intrathecal, neuraxial or other procedures where bleeding into a confined space may cause neurologic injury.
Do not use one universal platelet threshold for every procedure. Follow anesthesia, surgeon, hematology and facility criteria when bleeding consequence is high.

Thrombocytosis

Mild thrombocytosis is frequently reactive and may accompany iron deficiency, inflammation, infection or recent surgery.

Marked, persistent or unexplained thrombocytosis should prompt review for an underlying hematologic or inflammatory cause.

White Blood Cell Count

Leukocytosis should not automatically be labeled infection.

Possible Causes

  • Infection
  • Steroid therapy
  • Smoking
  • Inflammation
  • Physiologic stress
  • Medication effect
  • Hematologic disease
Mild stable leukocytosis in an asymptomatic patient may not require additional preoperative workup.
Marked leukocytosis with fever, hemodynamic instability or symptoms of active infection requires further evaluation.

Leukopenia / Neutropenia

Review the absolute neutrophil count, medication history, chemotherapy exposure, chronicity and infection symptoms.

Severe or unexplained neutropenia may increase infection risk and may require hematology or treating-specialist input before elective surgery.

Sodium

Sodium abnormalities should be interpreted according to severity, symptoms, duration and underlying cause.

Mild chronic asymptomatic sodium abnormalities may be compatible with elective surgery after appropriate review.
Severe, acute or symptomatic hyponatremia or hypernatremia requires correction and evaluation before routine elective surgery.

Assess

  • Neurologic symptoms
  • Volume status
  • Diuretic use
  • Heart failure
  • Kidney disease
  • Endocrine disease
  • Recent GI losses

Potassium

Potassium abnormalities may increase arrhythmia risk and frequently reflect renal disease, medication effects, volume status or specimen artifact.

Significant hyperkalemia requires confirmation and evaluation before elective surgery, particularly when EKG changes, kidney failure or progressive elevation is present.
Unexpected potassium elevation in an otherwise well patient should be assessed for hemolysis or specimen error before assuming true hyperkalemia.

Common Causes of Hyperkalemia

  • CKD / AKI
  • ACE inhibitor / ARB therapy
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics
  • Potassium supplements
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Tissue injury
  • Specimen hemolysis

Hypokalemia

Common Causes

  • Loop or thiazide diuretics
  • Vomiting
  • Diarrhea
  • Poor intake
  • Insulin shifts
  • Magnesium deficiency
Significant hypokalemia, particularly with symptoms, arrhythmia, prolonged QT risk or digoxin use, should be corrected and reassessed before elective surgery.

Magnesium

Magnesium is not included in every routine panel but may be important in patients with arrhythmias, prolonged QT, diuretic use, alcoholism or refractory hypokalemia.

Hypomagnesemia can contribute to ventricular arrhythmias and make potassium replacement more difficult.

BUN and Creatinine

Review both absolute values and trends. A single creatinine value should be interpreted together with eGFR, volume status and prior baseline.

A new rise in creatinine may represent AKI even when the value remains within or near the laboratory reference range.

Possible Causes

  • Dehydration
  • Medication effect
  • Recent contrast
  • Obstruction
  • Infection
  • Intrinsic renal disease
  • Heart failure

RCRI and Creatinine

RCRI renal criterion:

Preoperative creatinine >2.0 mg/dL = 1 RCRI point
The RCRI threshold is a cardiac-risk criterion and should not be interpreted as the only threshold for clinically important kidney disease.

eGFR

eGFR assists with assessment of chronic renal function and may influence medication dosing and perioperative anticoagulant clearance.

Renal function is particularly important when managing dabigatran, selected other anticoagulants, nephrotoxic medications and contrast exposure.

CO2 / Bicarbonate

A low bicarbonate may reflect metabolic acidosis, renal dysfunction, diarrhea, DKA or another metabolic process.

Significant unexplained metabolic acidosis warrants further evaluation before elective surgery.

A chronically elevated bicarbonate may occur in selected patients with chronic hypercapnic respiratory disease and should be interpreted with the pulmonary history.

Anion Gap

Typical calculation:

Anion gap = Sodium − (Chloride + Bicarbonate)
An elevated anion gap combined with SGLT2 inhibitor use, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or ketones should raise concern for euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis even when glucose is not markedly elevated.

Glucose

Current glucose reflects acute glycemic status and should be interpreted separately from A1c.

Mild asymptomatic hyperglycemia is common and does not automatically require surgical cancellation.
Marked hyperglycemia with dehydration, ketosis, anion-gap metabolic acidosis or hyperosmolar symptoms requires evaluation before elective surgery.

Hemoglobin A1c

A1c reflects average glycemic exposure over approximately the prior 2–3 months.

A1c can help identify poor chronic glycemic control but should not be used as the sole determinant of whether elective surgery can proceed.

Interpret With

  • Current glucose
  • Procedure type
  • Wound-healing risk
  • Infection risk
  • Diabetes complications
  • Facility/surgeon criteria

Calcium

Mild calcium abnormalities are often related to albumin or chronic conditions. Significant abnormalities should be confirmed and interpreted clinically.

Severe symptomatic hypocalcemia or hypercalcemia may produce neurologic or cardiac effects and requires evaluation before elective surgery.

Albumin

Low albumin may reflect inflammation, liver dysfunction, kidney loss, fluid status or poor nutritional reserve.

Do not diagnose malnutrition solely from a low albumin value.
Low albumin may still identify a higher-risk patient and should prompt review of the underlying clinical condition, especially before major elective surgery.

Liver Enzymes

Test General Interpretation
AST / ALT Hepatocellular injury pattern when significantly elevated.
Alkaline phosphatase May reflect cholestatic, biliary or bone-related processes.
Bilirubin May indicate hepatobiliary disease, hemolysis or other pathology.
Albumin Affected by synthesis, inflammation, renal loss and nutrition-related factors.
A mild stable isolated transaminase elevation does not automatically require surgical cancellation.

Liver Disease Red Flags

Further assessment is appropriate for:

Acute hepatitis symptoms
New jaundice
Markedly abnormal bilirubin
Significant synthetic dysfunction
Unexplained coagulopathy
Ascites or decompensation
Hepatic encephalopathy
Active alcohol-related liver deterioration

PT / INR

PT/INR should be interpreted in the context of warfarin use, liver function, vitamin K status and bleeding history.

Routine INR testing is generally not necessary in every patient who is not taking warfarin and has no liver disease, bleeding history or other indication.
An unexpected prolonged INR should not be ignored before a high-bleeding-risk procedure.

aPTT

A prolonged aPTT may reflect heparin exposure, factor deficiency, inhibitor, lupus anticoagulant or other coagulation abnormality.

Unexpected significant aPTT prolongation warrants confirmation and further assessment before procedures where bleeding consequences are substantial.

Urinalysis

A positive leukocyte esterase, pyuria or bacteriuria does not automatically diagnose symptomatic UTI.
For elective nonurologic spine and joint surgery, assess symptoms before ordering antibiotics or delaying surgery.

See the dedicated urinalysis chapter for the complete asymptomatic bacteriuria pathway.

When to Repeat an Abnormal Test

Situation Repeat Testing
Unexpected isolated abnormality Repeat when specimen error or transient abnormality is possible.
After treatment Repeat when confirmation of improvement is needed before surgery.
Progressive disease Use updated values sufficiently close to surgery to reflect current status.
Stable chronic abnormality Repeat only when required by protocol or when clinical status changed.
Critical abnormality Manage urgently according to clinical and laboratory policy.

Common Lab Findings That May Not Require Delay by Themselves

Depending on clinical context, these may not independently require postponement:

Mild stable chronic anemia
Mild isolated leukocytosis without symptoms
Stable CKD laboratory abnormalities
Mild chronic sodium abnormality without symptoms
Mild nonspecific transaminase elevation
Asymptomatic bacteriuria
Mild stable hyperglycemia without metabolic decompensation

Laboratory Findings More Likely to Require Further Evaluation

Examples include:

Severe or progressive anemia
Active blood loss
Severe thrombocytopenia
Clinically important unexplained coagulopathy
Severe hyperkalemia
Severe hypokalemia
Acute or symptomatic sodium disorder
Significant unexplained metabolic acidosis
DKA or hyperosmolar state
Progressive AKI
Severe symptomatic calcium disorder
Acute hepatic decompensation
Other critical laboratory abnormality

Key Takeaways

  • Interpret laboratory values in context rather than by reference range alone.
  • Compare abnormal results with prior values whenever possible.
  • Identify whether anemia is stable, progressive or associated with active blood loss.
  • Do not diagnose infection from leukocytosis or urinalysis alone.
  • Confirm unexpected potassium abnormalities when specimen error is possible.
  • Review renal trends rather than relying only on one creatinine value.
  • Use A1c for chronic glycemic assessment and glucose for current status.
  • Do not diagnose malnutrition from albumin alone.
  • Unexpected coagulation abnormalities require explanation before high-bleeding-risk procedures.
  • Repeat testing only when the result will help confirm stability or change management.
  • Severe acute metabolic, renal, hematologic or hepatic abnormalities may require postponement.
