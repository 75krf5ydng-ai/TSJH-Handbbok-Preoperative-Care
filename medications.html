Perioperative Medication Management & Hold Instructions

Practical medication review for elective spine, joint and noncardiac surgery

Purpose

Medication reconciliation is a core component of preoperative optimization. The clinician should identify medications that must be continued, adjusted, interrupted, substituted or specifically coordinated with the surgeon, anesthesia team or prescribing specialist.

Medication instructions should be individualized according to indication, renal function, bleeding risk, thrombotic risk, anesthesia plan, surgical procedure and institutional policy.

Medication Reconciliation Checklist

Confirm prescription medications, over-the-counter medications, supplements, injections, inhalers, topical medications and medications used only as needed.

Anticoagulants: Quick Reference

Anticoagulant interruption must balance procedural bleeding risk against the risk of stroke, systemic embolism or recurrent venous thromboembolism.

Medication TSJH Quick-Reference Hold Important Considerations
Apixaban / Eliquis 72 hours Consider renal function, bleeding risk and neuraxial requirements.
Rivaroxaban / Xarelto 72 hours Consider renal function, indication and procedure.
Edoxaban / Savaysa 72 hours Individualize when renal impairment or high bleeding risk is present.
Dabigatran / Pradaxa 3–5 days Renal function significantly affects clearance and required hold time.
Warfarin / Coumadin 5 days Confirm INR when indicated and determine whether bridging is necessary.
Therapeutic enoxaparin / Lovenox 24 hours Longer consideration may be necessary with renal impairment or neuraxial procedures.
These are institutional quick-reference intervals, not universal rules. Neuraxial anesthesia, renal impairment, recent thrombosis and unusually high bleeding-risk procedures may require a different plan.

Bridging Anticoagulation

Bridging should not be performed routinely. It may increase perioperative bleeding without providing benefit in lower-risk patients.

Potential High-Thrombotic-Risk Situations

  • Selected mechanical heart valves
  • Recent stroke or systemic embolism
  • Recent DVT or pulmonary embolism
  • Selected severe thrombophilic disorders
  • Other conditions designated high risk by the treating specialist
Use an established anticoagulation-management algorithm and obtain specialist guidance when risk is uncertain. Document who provided the bridging plan.

Medication TSJH Quick-Reference Hold
Clopidogrel / Plavix 7 days
Ticagrelor / Brilinta 7 days
Prasugrel / Effient 7 days
Do not interrupt antiplatelet therapy after recent coronary intervention without reviewing the PCI/stent date, indication and cardiology recommendations.

Aspirin

Aspirin management depends on the indication and the bleeding risk of the planned procedure.

Situation General Institutional Approach
Dual or triple antithrombotic therapy Aspirin is generally continued unless the procedure creates a specific bleeding concern requiring interruption.
Total joint procedure Aspirin may be held approximately 5 days when interruption is directed.
Spine procedure Aspirin may be held approximately 7 days when interruption is directed.
SCS / intrathecal procedure Follow procedure-specific bleeding and neuraxial guidance.
Determine whether aspirin is being used for primary prevention, secondary cardiovascular prevention, prior PCI, cerebrovascular disease or another indication before recommending interruption.

Recent Watchman or Cardiac Ablation

Patients who have recently undergone left atrial appendage occlusion or cardiac ablation may have a required period of uninterrupted anticoagulation.

Do not interrupt oral anticoagulation soon after Watchman implantation or ablation without reviewing the procedural date and cardiology or electrophysiology recommendations. Institutional guidance may require approximately 2–4 weeks of uninterrupted anticoagulation before elective interruption is considered.

Beta Blockers

Chronic beta-blocker therapy should generally be continued perioperatively unless clinically contraindicated.

Avoid abrupt withdrawal of chronic beta-blocker therapy because rebound tachycardia and hypertension may occur.

ACE Inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers require individualized perioperative planning based on their indication and the patient's hemodynamic status.

When used primarily for hypertension, holding the medication before surgery may reduce intraoperative hypotension in selected patients. When used as part of heart-failure guideline-directed therapy, the decision may differ.

Follow the current anesthesia and institutional day-of-surgery medication protocol rather than applying one rule to every patient.

Calcium Channel Blockers

Calcium channel blockers are commonly continued perioperatively unless hypotension, bradycardia or another clinical reason warrants modification.

Diuretics

Review the indication for diuretic therapy and assess volume status, potassium, sodium and renal function.

Patients taking diuretics solely for hypertension may be managed differently from those requiring them for symptomatic heart failure or significant volume overload. Follow anesthesia instructions for the day of surgery.

Statins

Chronic statin therapy should generally be continued throughout the perioperative period.

Antiarrhythmic Medications

Chronic antiarrhythmic therapy is commonly continued, but the specific drug, rhythm indication, heart rate, QT interval, electrolytes and anesthesia interactions should be reviewed.

Do not discontinue an antiarrhythmic medication solely because surgery is planned without determining the indication and potential consequence of interruption.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors require preoperative interruption because of the risk of perioperative euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

Examples Consideration
Empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin Commonly withheld for approximately 3 days before scheduled surgery.
Ertugliflozin May require a longer preoperative interruption.
Confirm the exact agent and current institutional hold interval. Assess for ketosis or metabolic acidosis if the medication was not appropriately held and the patient becomes symptomatic.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists require individualized assessment because delayed gastric emptying may increase aspiration risk.

Assess

  • Medication and dosing frequency
  • Recent dose escalation
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Abdominal bloating
  • Early satiety
  • Known gastroparesis
  • Other aspiration-risk factors
Follow current anesthesia and institutional guidance rather than relying on an outdated automatic hold interval for all GLP-1 users.

Metformin

Metformin management should consider renal function, fasting status and anticipated contrast exposure.

Follow current institutional instructions regarding the final preoperative dose and postoperative resumption.

Sulfonylureas

Sulfonylureas can cause fasting hypoglycemia and are generally withheld on the morning of surgery.

Examples include glipizide, glyburide and glimepiride.

Insulin

Insulin dosing should be individualized by insulin type, usual dose, diabetes type, fasting duration and institutional protocol.

Patients with type 1 diabetes require basal insulin. Complete omission of basal insulin can result in diabetic ketoacidosis.
Type General Approach
Basal insulin Commonly continued at an adjusted dose according to protocol.
Prandial insulin Generally withheld while fasting.
Insulin pump Requires a documented perioperative plan.

Thyroid Medications

Levothyroxine and other chronic thyroid replacement medications are generally continued.

Antithyroid medications should not be interrupted without clinical reason in patients being treated for hyperthyroidism.

Chronic Corticosteroids

Chronic systemic corticosteroids should not be abruptly discontinued. Determine whether the patient's dose and duration create a risk of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis suppression.

Patients with known adrenal insufficiency or significant chronic steroid exposure may require perioperative glucocorticoid supplementation based on the degree of surgical stress.

Inhalers and Pulmonary Medications

Maintenance inhalers should generally be continued through the perioperative period.

Medication General Approach
Long-acting bronchodilators Continue.
Inhaled corticosteroids Continue.
Rescue inhaler Ensure availability.
Combination inhalers Generally continue.

Antiseizure Medications

Antiseizure medications should generally be continued to avoid breakthrough seizures caused by abrupt interruption.

When oral medication cannot be given, determine whether an alternative route or equivalent therapy is needed.

Parkinson Medications

Dopaminergic therapy should be kept as close to the patient's normal dosing schedule as possible.

Prolonged interruption may worsen rigidity, swallowing, mobility and autonomic dysfunction.

Psychiatric Medications

Most chronic antidepressant and psychiatric medications are continued, but each medication should be reviewed for withdrawal risk, sedation, serotonergic interactions, QT effects and hemodynamic effects.

Avoid abruptly discontinuing chronic psychiatric therapy without a specific reason or an appropriate tapering plan.

Benzodiazepines

Chronic benzodiazepine users may experience withdrawal if medication is abruptly stopped. At the same time, benzodiazepines may increase sedation, respiratory depression and postoperative delirium risk.

Distinguish chronic physiologic dependence from intermittent or unnecessary use. Avoid additional benzodiazepine exposure in delirium-prone patients when feasible.

Opioids

Chronic opioid use should be documented before surgery because opioid tolerance can complicate postoperative pain management.

Review

  • Medication and daily dose
  • Prescriber
  • Duration of therapy
  • History of opioid tolerance
  • Concurrent sedatives
  • OSA or pulmonary disease
Coordinate complex chronic opioid regimens with anesthesia, the surgeon or pain management when appropriate.

Muscle Relaxants

Muscle relaxants can contribute to sedation, dizziness and falls, particularly in older adults.

Cyclobenzaprine has clinically relevant anticholinergic effects. Methocarbamol may be preferred in selected older or delirium-prone patients when a postoperative muscle relaxant is required.

NSAIDs

NSAID management depends on the specific medication, bleeding risk, kidney function, surgical procedure and surgeon preference.

NSAIDs may increase bleeding risk and can worsen renal perfusion in susceptible patients. Review CKD, dehydration and concurrent anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.

Herbal Products and Supplements

Ask specifically about supplements because patients may not consider them medications.

Some herbal products can influence bleeding, sedation, blood pressure or glucose control.

Follow anesthesia and institutional guidance regarding preoperative discontinuation of nonessential herbal products and supplements.

Weight-Loss Medications

Weight-loss regimens may include GLP-1 receptor agonists, stimulant agents or combination medications that affect appetite, gastric emptying, blood pressure or heart rate.

Identify the exact medication rather than documenting only “weight-loss medication.” Apply guidance according to the drug's pharmacology.

Medication Red Flags Requiring Clarification

Clarify the perioperative plan before elective surgery when any of the following are present:
  • Recent coronary stent with antiplatelet therapy
  • Recent Watchman implantation or cardiac ablation
  • Mechanical heart valve
  • Recent DVT or PE
  • Recent stroke or TIA with complex antithrombotic therapy
  • Unclear anticoagulant hold instructions
  • Potential need for anticoagulation bridging
  • SGLT2 inhibitor not appropriately held
  • GLP-1 therapy with significant gastrointestinal symptoms
  • Insulin pump without a perioperative plan
  • Chronic steroids with possible adrenal suppression
  • Complex chronic opioid therapy

Decision Framework

Question Action
Why is the medication prescribed? Determine the consequence of interruption before advising the patient to stop it.
Does it increase surgical bleeding? Balance bleeding risk against thrombotic risk.
Does renal function affect clearance? Adjust the interruption interval or dosing when appropriate.
Could stopping it cause withdrawal or decompensation? Continue or establish an alternative plan when necessary.
Could it increase aspiration risk? Assess gastric-emptying medications and current GI symptoms.
Does the medication affect glucose? Establish fasting and day-of-surgery diabetes instructions.
Is the plan unclear? Clarify with anesthesia, the surgeon or the prescribing specialist before surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Never recommend medication interruption without knowing the indication.
  • Document the exact anticoagulant and antiplatelet hold plan.
  • Do not bridge anticoagulation routinely.
  • Review recent PCI, Watchman, ablation, stroke and thromboembolism before interrupting antithrombotic therapy.
  • Recognize SGLT2-associated perioperative euglycemic DKA risk.
  • Use current anesthesia guidance for GLP-1 receptor agonists.
  • Do not completely stop required basal insulin in type 1 diabetes.
  • Continue essential beta-blocker, antiseizure and Parkinson therapy unless specifically contraindicated.
  • Identify chronic steroids, opioids and benzodiazepines before surgery.
  • Resolve unclear medication plans before the day of surgery whenever possible.
