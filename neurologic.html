Preoperative Neurologic, Stroke/TIA, Delirium & Cognitive Risk Evaluation

Neurologic stability, cerebrovascular risk, frailty and postoperative cognitive protection

Purpose

Neurologic conditions can affect perioperative stroke risk, aspiration risk, medication management, mobility, rehabilitation potential and the risk of postoperative delirium or cognitive decline.

The preoperative goal is to determine whether neurologic disease is stable, identify recent cerebrovascular events, assess baseline cognition and frailty, review antithrombotic therapy, and establish a postoperative plan that reduces avoidable neurologic complications.

Prior Stroke or TIA

A history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack increases perioperative cerebrovascular risk and contributes one point to the Revised Cardiac Risk Index.

RCRI cerebrovascular criterion:

History of stroke or TIA = 1 RCRI point

Document the date of the neurologic event, residual deficits, current antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, and whether symptoms have remained stable.

Timing of Elective Surgery After Stroke

Perioperative stroke risk is highest soon after a cerebrovascular event and decreases over time. Timing should be individualized according to the urgency of surgery, neurologic recovery, recurrent stroke risk and the consequences of delaying the procedure.

Recent stroke requires careful review before elective noncardiac surgery. When surgery can safely be delayed, a substantial interval after the event is generally preferred. Institutional and anesthesia guidance should be followed, with specialist input when timing is uncertain.
For TSJH planning, anesthesia may prefer approximately one year after a cerebrovascular event when feasible, with a minimum interval commonly considered in the range of 8–9 months after individualized discussion.

New Neurologic Symptoms

New focal neurologic findings should not be attributed to a chronic condition without evaluation. Elective surgery should generally be postponed for symptoms suggesting acute stroke, TIA or another unstable neurologic process.

Red Flags

  • New unilateral weakness or numbness
  • New facial droop
  • New speech or language disturbance
  • Acute visual loss or diplopia
  • New severe unexplained headache
  • Acute gait or coordination change
  • New confusion or altered mental status
  • Recent unexplained syncope with neurologic concern

Antiplatelet Therapy After Stroke or TIA

Patients with prior stroke or TIA may be taking aspirin, clopidogrel or another antithrombotic medication for secondary prevention.

Document the exact indication for therapy and who is directing the perioperative interruption and resumption plan. The risk of bleeding must be balanced against the risk of recurrent cerebrovascular events.

For patients with a history of TIA who have been specifically directed to bridge with enoxaparin, document the exact regimen and specialist or surgeon recommendation. Postoperative resumption of clopidogrel should follow the surgeon's plan when surgical bleeding risk is relevant.

Carotid Disease

Known carotid stenosis should be reviewed together with neurologic symptoms, prior imaging, prior intervention and current preventive therapy.

Routine carotid imaging is not indicated solely because elective surgery is planned. Additional evaluation should be driven by symptoms, known disease progression or another specific clinical indication.

Seizure Disorders

Patients with epilepsy or other seizure disorders should generally continue anticonvulsant therapy through the perioperative period unless a specific contraindication exists.

Review

  • Last seizure
  • Typical frequency
  • Recent breakthrough seizures
  • Medication adherence
  • Drug levels when clinically indicated
  • Electrolyte abnormalities that may lower seizure threshold
  • Alcohol or sedative withdrawal risk
Frequent or recently worsening seizures should be evaluated before elective surgery when control is not established.

Parkinson Disease

Parkinson disease requires attention to medication timing because missed dopaminergic therapy can significantly worsen rigidity, mobility and swallowing.

Establish a perioperative medication plan that minimizes interruption of scheduled Parkinson therapy whenever feasible.

Assess for dysphagia, aspiration risk, orthostatic hypotension, autonomic dysfunction, hallucinations and baseline cognitive impairment.

Neuromuscular Disease

Neuromuscular disorders may affect respiratory strength, airway protection, mobility and response to anesthetic or neuromuscular-blocking medications.

Significant respiratory muscle weakness, bulbar dysfunction or rapidly progressive neuromuscular symptoms may require specialty evaluation and a higher-acuity perioperative setting.

Baseline Cognitive Assessment

Baseline cognitive impairment increases the risk of postoperative delirium, prolonged hospitalization, functional decline and discharge to a higher level of care.

Determine whether the patient normally manages medications, finances, transportation and daily activities independently or requires assistance.

When cognitive impairment is suspected, document the patient's baseline and identify an appropriate family member or caregiver who can assist with perioperative communication and discharge planning.

AWOL-S Delirium Risk Screening

AWOL-S or another validated delirium-risk tool may be used in older or higher-risk surgical patients to identify those who may benefit from targeted delirium-prevention strategies.

Documentation format:

AWOL-S score: ___

Screening results should be interpreted with the patient's age, baseline cognition, frailty, medication burden, sensory impairment and surgical complexity.

Clinical Frailty Scale

Frailty provides information that is not captured by age alone and can predict postoperative complications, prolonged recovery and increased discharge needs.

Documentation format:

CFS: ___
CFS General Description
1–3 Very fit to managing well
4 Living with very mild frailty / vulnerable
5 Mild frailty
6 Moderate frailty
7–9 Severe to terminal frailty spectrum
A CFS score of 6 may be documented as: “Moderately frail on CFS scale score 6.”

Postoperative Delirium

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and cognition. It is common in older, frail and cognitively impaired surgical patients and should not be assumed to represent baseline dementia.

Important Risk Factors

  • Older age
  • Baseline cognitive impairment or dementia
  • Frailty
  • Prior delirium
  • Visual or hearing impairment
  • Polypharmacy
  • Anticholinergic medications
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Opioid exposure
  • Sleep disruption
  • Dehydration
  • Infection
  • Electrolyte abnormalities
  • Urinary retention
  • Constipation
  • Hypoxemia

Delirium Prevention

Strategy Purpose
Frequent orientation Reinforces time, place, procedure and familiar context.
Glasses and hearing aids Reduces sensory deprivation.
Early mobilization Reduces immobility and supports normal sleep-wake cycles.
Adequate hydration Reduces dehydration-related confusion and hypotension.
Sleep protection Minimizes unnecessary nighttime disruption.
Medication review Limits avoidable deliriogenic medications.
Pain control Treats pain while minimizing excessive sedative burden.
Bowel and bladder management Prevents retention, constipation and discomfort as delirium triggers.

Medication Considerations in Older or Cognitively Vulnerable Patients

Anticholinergic and sedating medications can contribute to confusion, urinary retention, constipation, falls and delirium.

When clinically appropriate, consider lower-anticholinergic alternatives. For example, methocarbamol may be preferred over cyclobenzaprine in some older or delirium-prone patients when a muscle relaxant is necessary.

Avoid unnecessary benzodiazepines and minimize sedative burden whenever possible.

Falls and Mobility

Review baseline ambulation, assistive-device use, recent falls, lower extremity weakness and home support.

Mobility and fall risk affect postoperative disposition and should be considered during preoperative planning rather than waiting until discharge.

Syncope and Near-Syncope

Syncope requires assessment of both neurologic and cardiovascular causes. A neurologic workup is most relevant when features suggest seizure, focal neurologic dysfunction or another central nervous system process.

Unexplained recent syncope should be evaluated before elective surgery when a significant cardiac or neurologic cause has not been excluded.

When Neurology Consultation May Be Appropriate

Consider neurology consultation for:
  • Recent stroke or TIA with unresolved perioperative questions
  • New focal neurologic deficits
  • Uncontrolled or recently worsening seizures
  • Progressive neuromuscular weakness
  • Unexplained recurrent syncope with neurologic features
  • Rapidly progressive cognitive decline
  • Complex neurologic medication-management concerns

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when neurologic instability is present, including:
  • Acute stroke or TIA symptoms
  • Recent cerebrovascular event without adequate recovery or timing review
  • Uncontrolled recurrent seizures
  • New unexplained focal neurologic deficit
  • Acute unexplained altered mental status
  • Rapidly progressive neuromuscular weakness
  • Other unstable neurologic disease requiring further evaluation

Decision Framework

Question Action
Has the patient had a prior stroke or TIA? Document event date, residual deficit, current therapy and surgical timing.
Are new neurologic symptoms present? Evaluate before elective surgery.
Is seizure disease controlled? Continue therapy and address recent breakthrough seizures.
Is cognitive impairment present? Establish baseline and implement delirium-prevention planning.
Is the patient frail? Document CFS and anticipate mobility, rehabilitation and disposition needs.
Is antithrombotic therapy being interrupted? Document the indication, hold plan and postoperative resumption plan.
Is specialist evaluation necessary? Refer when neurologic instability or complexity exceeds routine preoperative management.

Key Takeaways

  • Document the date and residual effects of prior stroke or TIA.
  • Recognize that prior cerebrovascular disease contributes one RCRI point.
  • Recent stroke requires individualized timing before elective surgery.
  • Do not ignore new focal neurologic symptoms.
  • Continue essential antiseizure therapy whenever possible.
  • Assess baseline cognition and postoperative delirium risk.
  • Use AWOL-S and CFS when appropriate.
  • Reduce avoidable anticholinergic and sedating medication exposure.
  • Plan mobility, fall prevention and discharge needs before surgery.
