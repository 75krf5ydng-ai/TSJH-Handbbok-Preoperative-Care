Preoperative Nutrition, Obesity, Frailty & Prehabilitation

Assessment of nutritional reserve, body composition, functional vulnerability and recovery readiness

Purpose

Nutrition, obesity, frailty and functional reserve can influence wound healing, infection risk, mobility, postoperative complications, rehabilitation needs and disposition after elective surgery.

The goal is to identify modifiable risk before surgery, distinguish obesity from malnutrition, recognize frailty, and establish a practical prehabilitation and postoperative support plan.

Initial Nutrition Assessment

Review Important Findings
Weight history Current weight, recent unintended loss, prior baseline and major weight change.
Oral intake Reduced appetite, poor intake, restrictive diet, chewing/swallowing difficulty.
GI symptoms Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, early satiety, gastroparesis or malabsorption.
Medical conditions Cancer, CKD, chronic inflammation, diabetes, liver disease or other catabolic illness.
Functional status Weakness, reduced mobility, deconditioning and difficulty with activities of daily living.
Social factors Food insecurity, inability to prepare meals, limited support or transportation barriers.

Malnutrition Can Occur at Any BMI

Do not assume that a patient with overweight or obesity is nutritionally replete. Patients with obesity may still have inadequate protein intake, micronutrient deficiency, muscle loss or clinically significant malnutrition.

Nutrition assessment should consider intake, weight trajectory, muscle mass, disease burden and functional status rather than BMI alone.

Unintentional Weight Loss

Recent unintentional weight loss may indicate reduced nutritional reserve, underlying disease or catabolic stress.

Ask whether the weight loss was intentional. Planned weight loss is different from unexplained loss associated with poor intake, weakness or illness.

Red Flags

  • Rapid unexplained weight loss
  • Reduced oral intake
  • Persistent nausea or vomiting
  • Dysphagia
  • Chronic diarrhea
  • Loss of muscle strength
  • Progressive functional decline

Protein Intake and Wound Healing

Adequate protein supports muscle maintenance, immune function, tissue repair and postoperative recovery.

Patients with poor intake, frailty, advanced age or recent weight loss may benefit from targeted nutritional optimization or dietitian referral before major elective surgery.

Albumin and Prealbumin

Albumin and prealbumin can be influenced by inflammation, hydration, liver function, kidney disease and acute illness.

Do not diagnose malnutrition from albumin or prealbumin alone. These tests are not specific nutritional markers and should be interpreted in the full clinical context.

Low albumin may still identify a higher-risk patient and should prompt review of the underlying cause.

Micronutrient Considerations

Micronutrient testing should be targeted rather than ordered routinely in every patient.

Consider When Clinically Indicated

  • Iron studies for anemia or suspected deficiency
  • Vitamin B12 for macrocytosis or risk factors
  • Folate for appropriate macrocytic anemia workup
  • Vitamin D when deficiency is clinically suspected or relevant to treatment
  • Other testing for malabsorption or bariatric surgery history

Prior Bariatric Surgery

Patients with previous bariatric surgery may be at increased risk for selected nutritional deficiencies depending on the procedure and long-term follow-up.

Review

  • Type of bariatric procedure
  • Current supplements
  • Iron status
  • Vitamin B12
  • Folate
  • Calcium/vitamin D history when relevant
  • Current GI symptoms
  • Recent weight trajectory
A history of bariatric surgery does not automatically require delay, but unexplained anemia, malnutrition or significant GI symptoms should be addressed.

Obesity

Obesity may increase anesthetic, respiratory, thromboembolic, mobility and wound-related risk, but BMI should not be interpreted in isolation.

Potential Risk Preoperative Consideration
OSA Screen with STOP-BANG and document PAP use or intolerance.
Hypoventilation Assess daytime hypoxemia, hypercapnia and oxygen requirement.
Difficult airway Review anesthesia history and physical airway concerns.
VTE Consider procedure, mobility and surgeon-directed prophylaxis plan.
Mobility limitation Plan assistive devices, PT and disposition.
Wound complications Review glycemic control, skin integrity and nutritional status.

Obesity Hypoventilation Syndrome

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome should be considered when severe obesity is associated with daytime hypercapnia, hypoxemia or severe sleep-disordered breathing.

Known or suspected chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure may require pulmonary evaluation and a higher-acuity perioperative setting.

BMI Is Not a Complete Risk Assessment

Two patients with the same BMI can have very different perioperative risk. Consider functional capacity, OSA, pulmonary status, diabetes, frailty, mobility and surgical complexity.

Frailty

Frailty reflects reduced physiologic reserve and vulnerability to stress. It is distinct from age and from the presence of chronic disease alone.

Frailty Red Flags

  • Dependence in activities of daily living
  • Repeated falls
  • Recent functional decline
  • Slow gait
  • Marked weakness
  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Low activity level
  • Cognitive vulnerability
  • Need for significant caregiver assistance

Frailty and Surgical Decision-Making

Frailty does not automatically mean surgery should be cancelled. It should trigger a more deliberate discussion of physiologic reserve, expected recovery, rehabilitation needs, postoperative monitoring and discharge disposition.

Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia refers to loss of skeletal muscle mass and function and may coexist with obesity.

A patient can have a high BMI and still have clinically important muscle loss. Weakness, difficulty rising from a chair, reduced gait speed and functional decline should not be dismissed because body weight is elevated.

Prehabilitation

Prehabilitation aims to improve physiologic and functional reserve before surgery.

Potential Components

  • Walking or aerobic activity as tolerated
  • Strengthening exercises
  • Respiratory exercises
  • Nutrition optimization
  • Smoking cessation
  • Diabetes optimization
  • Anemia treatment
  • Sleep optimization
  • Patient education
  • Home-safety and mobility planning

Exercise Before Surgery

The exercise plan should be realistic for the patient's orthopedic or spine condition.

Patients awaiting joint or spine surgery may not tolerate standard walking programs. Consider low-impact or condition-appropriate activity rather than assuming inability to walk means prehabilitation is impossible.

Possible Alternatives

  • Seated strengthening
  • Upper-body conditioning
  • Stationary cycling if tolerated
  • Aquatic exercise when appropriate
  • Home PT exercises
  • Respiratory muscle/lung expansion exercises

Smoking Cessation

Smoking increases pulmonary complications and may impair wound and bone healing.

Encourage cessation as early as possible. Patients should still be advised to stop even when the available preoperative interval is shorter than ideal.

Alcohol Risk

Heavy alcohol use may increase risk for withdrawal, infection, bleeding, cardiomyopathy, liver dysfunction and nutritional deficiency.

Patients at risk for alcohol withdrawal require a specific perioperative plan. Do not advise abrupt unsupervised cessation in a patient with likely physiologic dependence without appropriate medical assessment.

Diabetes and Wound Healing

Persistent hyperglycemia may impair immune function and wound healing.

Review current glucose control, A1c when appropriate, diabetes medications and evidence of acute metabolic instability. A1c should not be the sole determinant of surgical suitability.

Anemia and Recovery

Anemia may reduce exercise tolerance and increase transfusion risk during higher-blood-loss procedures.

For major spine or joint surgery, evaluate and treat reversible anemia when feasible. See the dedicated anemia workup chapter for the diagnostic pathway.

Skin Integrity and Wound Risk

Assess skin condition in patients with diabetes, obesity, immobility, edema or chronic wounds.

Active infected ulcers, cellulitis, draining wounds or wounds near the planned operative field require further evaluation before elective implant surgery.

Pressure Injury Risk

Frailty, immobility, neuropathy and prolonged surgery increase pressure-injury risk.

Preoperative Considerations

  • Baseline skin condition
  • Neuropathy
  • Limited mobility
  • History of pressure injury
  • Expected prolonged prone or lateral positioning
  • Nutritional reserve

Fall Risk

Patients with gait instability, neuropathy, frailty or recent falls require attention to postoperative fall prevention.

Review assistive-device use, home environment, sedating medications and postoperative mobility expectations.

Cognitive Vulnerability

Frailty and cognitive impairment frequently overlap and can increase the risk of postoperative delirium.

AWOL-S: ___
CFS: ___
When risk is elevated, plan orientation, sleep protection, mobility, hydration, sensory aids and minimization of avoidable deliriogenic medications.

Medication Burden

Polypharmacy may increase dizziness, falls, constipation, confusion and postoperative medication complications.

High-Yield Medication Review

  • Opioids
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Anticholinergic medications
  • Muscle relaxants
  • Antihypertensives
  • Diuretics
  • Diabetes medications
  • Anticoagulants
In selected older or delirium-prone patients, avoid unnecessary anticholinergic burden. Methocarbamol may be preferable to cyclobenzaprine when a muscle relaxant is clinically necessary and otherwise appropriate.

When Dietitian Referral May Be Helpful

Consider dietitian evaluation for:

Significant unintentional weight loss
Poor oral intake
Suspected protein-calorie malnutrition
Complex bariatric surgery history with deficiency concerns
Persistent GI symptoms limiting intake
Severe frailty with nutritional concerns
Complex renal or metabolic nutrition needs

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when nutritional or functional instability is severe, including:

Severe untreated malnutrition with major operative risk
Rapid unexplained weight loss requiring evaluation
Severe dehydration
Persistent vomiting preventing adequate intake
Major unresolved metabolic deficiency
Severe functional decline from acute illness
Active infected wound or ulcer relevant to surgery
Other clinically significant instability likely to impair safe recovery

Decision Framework

Question Action
Has the patient lost weight unintentionally? Determine severity, timeframe and cause.
Is oral intake adequate? Identify reversible barriers and consider nutrition referral.
Does obesity coexist with OSA or respiratory risk? Apply OSA and pulmonary pathways.
Is the patient frail? Document CFS and plan recovery/disposition needs.
Is functional decline due to the surgical condition or systemic illness? Distinguish mechanical limitation from medical deconditioning.
Can prehabilitation improve readiness? Use realistic strength, mobility, nutrition and pulmonary interventions.
Is there severe nutritional or functional instability? Optimize before elective surgery when clinically necessary.

Key Takeaways

  • Malnutrition can occur at any BMI.
  • Unintentional weight loss deserves evaluation before major elective surgery.
  • Albumin should not be used as the sole diagnostic test for malnutrition.
  • Obesity risk should be interpreted together with OSA, pulmonary status, diabetes and mobility.
  • Frailty is different from age and should be assessed directly.
  • Use CFS to document frailty when appropriate.
  • Sarcopenia can coexist with obesity.
  • Prehabilitation may include nutrition, strengthening, pulmonary exercises, smoking cessation and education.
  • Orthopedic limitations may require modified rather than standard exercise plans.
  • Plan home support, assistive devices and disposition before surgery.
  • Active infected wounds or severe nutritional instability may require surgical delay.
