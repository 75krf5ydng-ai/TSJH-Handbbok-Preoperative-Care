Purpose

Chronic pain therapy can affect perioperative analgesic requirements, respiratory risk, withdrawal risk, postoperative mobility and discharge planning. Patients taking chronic opioids, buprenorphine, methadone, benzodiazepines or other sedating medications require a clearly documented perioperative plan.

The goal is not to eliminate chronic pain medication before surgery. Identify opioid tolerance, avoid withdrawal, anticipate increased postoperative analgesic needs and minimize unnecessary respiratory or sedative risk.

Opioid Tolerance

Patients taking opioids chronically may require greater postoperative opioid doses than opioid-naive patients to achieve equivalent analgesia.

Opioid tolerance does not mean escalating opioids without limits. Use multimodal analgesia and monitor sedation and respiratory status closely.

Do Not Abruptly Stop Chronic Opioids

Abrupt interruption of chronic opioid therapy may cause withdrawal, sympathetic activation, uncontrolled pain and difficulty with postoperative management.

Establish a perioperative continuation or substitution strategy when a usual oral regimen cannot be administered.

Opioid Withdrawal

Potential Symptoms

  • Anxiety
  • Restlessness
  • Sweating
  • Tachycardia
  • Hypertension
  • Abdominal cramping
  • Diarrhea
  • Nausea
  • Myalgias
  • Rhinorrhea
Withdrawal can mimic postoperative pain, anxiety or autonomic instability. Knowing the patient's baseline medication exposure helps prevent diagnostic confusion.

Multimodal Analgesia

Multimodal analgesia combines medications and techniques with different mechanisms to improve pain control while limiting excessive opioid exposure.

Potential Components

  • Acetaminophen
  • NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor when appropriate
  • Regional anesthesia
  • Local anesthetic techniques
  • Selected gabapentinoid use
  • Muscle relaxant when appropriate
  • Ice / positioning
  • Early mobility
  • Procedure-specific nonopioid strategies
Multimodal does not mean “more medications.” Avoid stacking sedating agents without considering cumulative respiratory and delirium risk.

Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen is frequently used as part of multimodal analgesia.

Review total daily acetaminophen exposure, particularly when combination opioid products such as hydrocodone/acetaminophen or oxycodone/acetaminophen are used.

NSAIDs

NSAIDs can reduce opioid requirements but may not be appropriate for every patient or procedure.

Review

  • CKD / AKI
  • GI bleeding history
  • Anticoagulation
  • Heart failure
  • Bone fusion considerations
  • Surgeon restrictions
For spine fusion or other bone-healing procedures, NSAID use should follow the surgeon's protocol.

Gabapentin and Pregabalin

Gabapentinoids may be useful in selected neuropathic pain pathways but can cause sedation, dizziness and respiratory depression when combined with opioids.

Use additional caution in older adults, OSA, renal impairment and patients receiving multiple CNS depressants.
Gabapentin and pregabalin require renal dose consideration.

Muscle Relaxants

Muscle relaxants may improve postoperative spasm-related pain but can increase sedation and fall risk.

Cyclobenzaprine contributes anticholinergic burden. In selected older or delirium-prone patients, methocarbamol may be preferable when a muscle relaxant is clinically necessary and otherwise appropriate.
Avoid combining multiple sedating muscle relaxants or escalating doses without considering opioid and benzodiazepine exposure.

Buprenorphine

Buprenorphine may be prescribed for opioid use disorder or chronic pain.

Do not automatically discontinue buprenorphine before surgery. Abrupt discontinuation can increase withdrawal and relapse risk in patients treated for opioid use disorder.
Current perioperative practice often favors continuing buprenorphine with an individualized analgesic strategy. Complex cases should involve anesthesia, acute pain or the prescribing clinician.

Why Buprenorphine Requires Planning

Buprenorphine has high affinity for the mu-opioid receptor, but adequate perioperative analgesia can still be achieved with appropriate multimodal strategies and supplemental opioids when clinically needed.

Avoid labeling postoperative pain as “untreatable” solely because the patient is taking buprenorphine.

Methadone

Methadone may be prescribed for opioid use disorder or chronic pain.

Chronic methadone therapy should generally be continued to prevent withdrawal, with perioperative coordination when oral dosing is interrupted.
Methadone can prolong the QT interval and has complex pharmacokinetics. Review QTc, interacting medications and overdose risk in susceptible patients.

Benzodiazepines

Examples

  • Alprazolam
  • Lorazepam
  • Clonazepam
  • Diazepam
  • Temazepam

Chronic benzodiazepine use may increase postoperative sedation, falls, respiratory depression and delirium.

Do Not Abruptly Stop Chronic Benzodiazepines

Abrupt benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause severe anxiety, autonomic instability, delirium and seizures.
Differentiate chronic physiologic dependence from occasional PRN use when creating the perioperative medication plan.

Benzodiazepines + Opioids

Concurrent opioids and benzodiazepines increase sedation and respiratory depression risk, particularly in patients with OSA, COPD, frailty or advanced age.

Use the lowest effective sedative burden and consider enhanced postoperative respiratory monitoring.

PAP-Intolerant OSA

For known OSA with PAP intolerance, use postoperative continuous pulse oximetry when indicated and have NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen available when clinically warranted.

Chronic opioid users frequently have baseline constipation that can worsen after surgery.

Opioid-Induced Hyperalgesia

Some patients receiving prolonged or high-dose opioids may experience increased pain sensitivity.

Escalating opioid dose indefinitely may worsen rather than improve some pain syndromes. Consider anesthesia or pain-management consultation in complex cases.

Chronic Pain Expectations

The realistic postoperative goal is improved function and acceptable pain control—not necessarily a pain score of zero.

Preoperative expectation-setting may reduce distress and improve participation in mobility and rehabilitation.

Pain Contracts

Some patients have controlled-substance agreements with an outpatient pain management clinician.

Clarify who will prescribe postoperative opioids and when prescribing responsibility returns to the chronic pain clinician.
Avoid duplicate opioid prescriptions from multiple clinicians when a controlled- substance plan can be coordinated beforehand.

Alcohol Withdrawal

Patients with physiologic alcohol dependence may develop severe withdrawal, seizures or delirium tremens when alcohol is abruptly stopped.

High-risk patients require an appropriate perioperative monitoring and withdrawal-prevention plan rather than routine outpatient management.

Cannabis

Cannabis use may affect anesthetic requirements, cardiovascular response, airway reactivity and postoperative nausea or pain perception.

Document frequency, route and most recent use when clinically relevant and follow anesthesia instructions for preoperative abstinence.
Acute intoxication or altered mental status should prompt reassessment of elective surgical timing.

Stimulants and Other Substances

Illicit or nonprescribed stimulant use may create cardiovascular, thermoregulatory and behavioral risk.

Potential Concerns

  • Tachycardia
  • Severe hypertension
  • Arrhythmia
  • Hyperthermia
  • Agitation
  • Acute coronary syndrome
Suspected acute stimulant intoxication or cardiovascular instability is incompatible with routine elective surgery.

Do Not Use Stigma-Based Documentation

Document observable clinical facts, medication/substance history and risk. Avoid labels that do not contribute to medical decision-making.

Opioid Use Disorder

Patients receiving medication treatment for opioid use disorder should have their maintenance therapy and relapse-prevention needs incorporated into the surgical plan.

Perioperative treatment should support continuation of evidence-based OUD therapy and avoid unnecessary disruption that may increase relapse or overdose risk.

Naloxone

Patients with high opioid exposure or other overdose risk factors may already have naloxone available.

Naloxone education and access may be appropriate as part of safe outpatient opioid prescribing according to institutional and prescribing policies.

Respiratory Monitoring

Consider Enhanced Monitoring For

  • High-dose chronic opioids
  • Opioid + benzodiazepine therapy
  • OSA
  • PAP intolerance
  • COPD
  • Frailty
  • Prior opioid-related respiratory depression
  • Multiple sedating medications
Continuous pulse oximetry may be appropriate in patients with combined respiratory and sedative risk.

Renal and Hepatic Considerations

Kidney and liver disease may alter clearance of opioids and adjunct medications.

Review renal function for gabapentin/pregabalin and selected opioids, and consider hepatic function when acetaminophen or hepatically metabolized medications are used.

Older Adults

Older adults may have increased sensitivity to opioids, benzodiazepines and muscle relaxants.

Use particular caution with multiple CNS depressants in patients with frailty, cognitive impairment or high fall risk.

Spinal Cord Stimulators

Patients with SCS systems may need device management for surgery in addition to their analgesic plan.

Identify the manufacturer and whether the device should be turned off before surgery according to anesthesia, surgeon and device-specific guidance.

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Acute opioid intoxication
Acute alcohol or stimulant intoxication
Severe uncontrolled withdrawal
Altered mental status from substance exposure
Unstable cardiovascular effects of stimulant use
Unresolved intrathecal pump safety issue
Unresolved perioperative plan in a highly opioid-tolerant patient when major pain is expected
Other acute substance-related instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

With an appropriate perioperative plan, the following do not automatically require postponement:

Stable chronic opioid therapy
Buprenorphine maintenance
Methadone maintenance
Stable chronic benzodiazepine therapy
Pain-management contract
Remote substance-use history
Cannabis use without acute intoxication
Stable implanted pain pump with an established management plan

Chronic opioid therapy reviewed. Patient takes ___ at ___ for chronic ___ pain and is opioid tolerant. Recommend multimodal analgesia with close monitoring for sedation, constipation and respiratory depression.

Buprenorphine

Patient takes buprenorphine ___ for ___. Medication should not be routinely discontinued without a defined perioperative plan. Anesthesia/pain-management strategy reviewed/requested to maintain treatment continuity while providing adequate postoperative analgesia.

Chronic Benzodiazepine Use

Chronic ___ use reviewed. Avoid abrupt discontinuation because of withdrawal and seizure risk. Minimize additional sedative exposure postoperatively, particularly in the setting of concurrent opioid therapy / OSA.

OSA With Sedative Risk

Known OSA with PAP intolerance and concurrent opioid/sedative exposure. Recommend continuous postoperative pulse oximetry, minimization of excessive opioid/sedative burden and NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen when clinically warranted.

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is the patient opioid tolerant? Document baseline exposure and anticipate increased analgesic needs.
Is buprenorphine present? Avoid automatic discontinuation; establish an individualized plan.
Is methadone present? Continue maintenance therapy and review QT/interactions when relevant.
Is chronic benzodiazepine therapy present? Avoid abrupt withdrawal and limit additional sedative burden.
Does the patient have OSA? Plan enhanced respiratory monitoring when combined with opioids/sedatives.
Is there an implanted pain pump? Coordinate with the managing pain specialist.
Is substance use acute or chronic? Acute intoxication/withdrawal may require surgical delay.
Who will prescribe after surgery? Establish responsibility before discharge.

Key Takeaways

  • Document the patient's exact chronic opioid regimen.
  • Do not abruptly stop chronic opioids.
  • Use multimodal analgesia rather than opioid escalation alone.
  • Buprenorphine should not be automatically stopped before surgery.
  • Methadone maintenance generally should be continued.
  • Do not abruptly stop chronic benzodiazepines in a dependent patient.
  • Opioid plus benzodiazepine therapy increases respiratory risk.
  • OSA significantly increases concern when opioids and sedatives are used.
  • Gabapentinoids and muscle relaxants can add sedation and fall risk.
  • Reduce unnecessary anticholinergic burden in older adults.
  • Confirm who will prescribe postoperative opioids when a pain contract exists.
  • Intrathecal pumps require specialist coordination.
  • Acute intoxication or severe withdrawal may require postponement.
  • Stable medication-assisted treatment for opioid use disorder is not a reason to cancel surgery.
