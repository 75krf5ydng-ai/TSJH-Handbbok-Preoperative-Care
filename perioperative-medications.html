Perioperative Medication Management & Hold Guide

Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes medications, GLP-1, SGLT2, cardiovascular drugs, biologics, steroids, pain medications and more

Purpose

Perioperative medication management should balance bleeding, thrombosis, withdrawal, hemodynamic instability, aspiration, metabolic complications, infection risk and control of chronic disease.

This is a clinical quick-reference guide. Final medication instructions should follow the current surgeon, anesthesia, institutional and specialist pathway. Renal function, drug indication, procedural bleeding consequence and neuraxial requirements may change the plan.

Anticoagulants — TSJH Quick Reference

Medication Usual Quick Reference Important Considerations
Apixaban / Eliquis Hold based on procedural bleeding risk: commonly 1 day for low–moderate bleeding risk, 2 days for high bleeding risk surgery, and 3 days (72 hours) for neuraxial/spine or deep plexus procedures. Consider renal function, bleeding consequence, anesthesia technique and thromboembolic risk. Routine heparin bridging is generally not recommended for DOAC interruption.
Rivaroxaban / Xarelto Hold based on procedural bleeding risk: commonly 1 day for low–moderate bleeding risk, 2 days for high bleeding risk surgery, and 3 days (72 hours) for neuraxial/spine or deep plexus procedures. Consider renal function, bleeding consequence, anesthesia technique and thromboembolic risk. Routine heparin bridging is generally not recommended for DOAC interruption.
Edoxaban / Savaysa TSJH quick reference: hold approximately 72 hours. Current guidance: commonly hold 1 day for low–moderate bleeding risk, 2 days for higher-risk surgery, and 3 days (72 hours) for neuraxial/spine or deep plexus procedures. Consider renal function, procedural bleeding consequence, anesthesia technique and thromboembolic risk. Routine heparin bridging is generally not recommended for DOAC interruption.
Dabigatran / Pradaxa TSJH quick reference: hold approximately 3–5 days. Current guidance: interruption depends on renal function and procedural bleeding risk; commonly 2–4 days before higher-risk surgery and 4 days before neuraxial procedures. Renal clearance is especially important. Longer interruption may be required with reduced CrCl. Consider bleeding consequence, anesthesia technique and thromboembolic risk. Routine heparin bridging is generally not recommended for DOAC interruption.
Warfarin Hold approximately 5 days Check INR and determine whether bridging is actually necessary.
Enoxaparin / Lovenox Hold approximately 24 hours Therapeutic vs prophylactic dosing, renal function and neuraxial timing.
Do not bridge anticoagulation routinely. Bridging is reserved for selected patients with sufficiently high thromboembolic risk and should follow an approved anticoagulation algorithm or specialist plan.

Antiplatelet Therapy

Medication Usual Quick Reference Important Considerations
Clopidogrel / Plavix Hold approximately 7 days when appropriate Review indication, recent PCI/stent and cerebrovascular history.
Ticagrelor / Brilinta Hold approximately 7 days when appropriate Recent PCI may make interruption unsafe.
Prasugrel / Effient Hold approximately 7 days when appropriate Confirm coronary intervention history.
Aspirin — joints Commonly hold approximately 5 days Follow surgeon pathway and cardiovascular indication.
Aspirin — spine Commonly hold approximately 7 days High bleeding-consequence procedure.
For dual or triple antithrombotic therapy, aspirin may need to remain continued depending on cardiovascular indication, except when procedure-specific bleeding risk requires an alternative plan.

Recent PCI / Coronary Stent

Do not stop antiplatelet therapy after recent PCI simply because surgery is scheduled. Review the PCI date, indication, stent history and current cardiology recommendations before interrupting therapy.

Watchman / Recent Ablation

Recent Watchman placement or atrial ablation may require uninterrupted anticoagulation during a defined early treatment interval. Review the current cardiology/EP plan before elective interruption.

Beta Blockers

Examples include metoprolol, carvedilol, atenolol and propranolol.

Chronic beta-blocker therapy should generally be continued perioperatively unless significant bradycardia, hypotension or another contraindication is present.
Avoid abrupt withdrawal of chronic beta-blocker therapy.

ACE Inhibitors / ARBs

Examples include lisinopril, losartan, valsartan and olmesartan.

Day-of-surgery management depends on facility/anesthesia guidance and may differ when these agents are prescribed for uncomplicated hypertension versus heart-failure GDMT.

Review blood pressure, potassium, renal function, volume status and indication.

ARNI

Sacubitril/valsartan is a component of guideline-directed therapy for HFrEF.

Review anesthesia and heart-failure guidance rather than treating ARNI simply as another antihypertensive medication.

Calcium Channel Blockers

Amlodipine is commonly continued. Diltiazem and verapamil are also often continued but require review of heart rate, hypotension and conduction disease.

Diuretics

Examples include furosemide, torsemide, hydrochlorothiazide and spironolactone.

Management should be individualized according to indication, potassium, renal function and volume status.
Avoid excessive fasting-related volume depletion, but do not reflexively withhold necessary diuresis in a patient with active congestion.

Statins

Continue chronic statin therapy perioperatively unless a specific contraindication exists.

Antiarrhythmics

Examples include amiodarone, sotalol, flecainide and dofetilide.

Do not independently discontinue chronic antiarrhythmic therapy. Review QTc, renal function and interacting medications when relevant.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

Examples

  • Empagliflozin / Jardiance
  • Dapagliflozin / Farxiga
  • Canagliflozin / Invokana
  • Ertugliflozin / Steglatro
SGLT2 inhibitors require multi-day interruption before elective surgery because of euglycemic DKA risk. Follow the current medication-specific anesthesia/ facility interval.
This applies whether the medication is prescribed for diabetes, heart failure or chronic kidney disease.

SGLT2 Red Flag

Recent SGLT2 use plus nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, tachypnea, malaise or otherwise unexplained metabolic acidosis should prompt evaluation for euglycemic DKA even when serum glucose is not markedly elevated.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Examples

  • Semaglutide / Ozempic / Wegovy
  • Tirzepatide / Mounjaro / Zepbound
  • Dulaglutide / Trulicity
  • Liraglutide / Victoza / Saxenda
Current management should use individualized aspiration-risk assessment rather than assuming that every GLP-1 medication requires the same fixed hold.

Review

  • Daily vs weekly formulation
  • Last dose
  • Recent dose escalation
  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Bloating
  • Early satiety
  • Known gastroparesis
Significant GI symptoms or concern for delayed gastric emptying requires anesthesia review before elective sedation or general anesthesia.

Metformin

Follow current facility instructions for day-of-surgery management and review renal function, AKI and acute illness.

Sulfonylureas

Glipizide, glimepiride and glyburide may cause fasting hypoglycemia.

These are generally withheld on the day of surgery according to standard perioperative diabetes protocols.

DPP-4 Inhibitors

Examples include sitagliptin, linagliptin and saxagliptin.

These have relatively low hypoglycemia risk when used alone. Follow current facility diabetes instructions.

Insulin

Do not instruct every diabetic patient to “hold all insulin.”
Type 1 diabetes requires basal insulin even during fasting. Basal insulin is usually dose-adjusted rather than completely omitted. Prandial insulin is typically held while fasting unless being used as correction insulin under a defined plan.

Insulin Pump

If an insulin pump is removed in a patient dependent on continuous basal insulin, an alternative basal insulin plan must be established.
Coordinate pump continuation, basal rate and intraoperative glucose management with anesthesia and the diabetes pathway.

Thyroid Medications

Levothyroxine is generally continued perioperatively.
Methimazole and PTU are generally continued for treated hyperthyroidism unless the treating clinician provides a different plan.

Chronic Glucocorticoids

Do not abruptly discontinue chronic systemic glucocorticoids.
Continue baseline therapy and assess HPA-axis suppression and whether supplemental perioperative steroid coverage is needed.

Conventional DMARDs

Medication General Approach
Methotrexate Often continued in stable patients; review renal function, CBC and liver tests.
Hydroxychloroquine Often continued.
Sulfasalazine Often continued under current pathways.
Leflunomide May be continued under selected guidance; individualize.

Biologic DMARDs

Do not use one fixed hold interval for every biologic.
Many biologics are managed according to the dosing cycle. Document exact drug, frequency, last dose, next dose and rheumatology/surgeon plan.

JAK Inhibitors

Examples include tofacitinib, upadacitinib and baricitinib.

JAK inhibitors commonly require temporary perioperative interruption. Confirm the current drug-specific recommendation.

Antiseizure Medications

Continue chronic antiseizure medications whenever possible to prevent missed doses and breakthrough seizures.

Parkinson Medications

Avoid unnecessary interruption of dopaminergic therapy because prolonged missed doses can cause severe rigidity, immobility and other complications.

SSRIs / SNRIs

These are commonly continued perioperatively.

Consider withdrawal risk, bleeding tendency and serotonergic drug interactions when making changes.

Benzodiazepines

Do not abruptly stop chronic benzodiazepines in a dependent patient. Withdrawal can cause severe anxiety, delirium, autonomic instability and seizures.
Minimize additional sedative burden when combined with opioids, OSA, COPD or frailty.

Chronic Opioids

Do not abruptly discontinue chronic opioid therapy without a plan.
Document baseline opioid exposure, anticipate tolerance and use multimodal postoperative analgesia with respiratory and bowel monitoring.

Buprenorphine

Do not automatically discontinue buprenorphine before surgery.
Continuation with an individualized multimodal analgesic strategy is often appropriate. Major painful procedures may warrant anesthesia or pain-management review.

Methadone

Maintenance methadone is generally continued to prevent withdrawal.
Review QTc and drug interactions when clinically relevant.

Gabapentin / Pregabalin

Review renal dosing, dizziness, sedation, fall risk and respiratory effects, particularly when combined with opioids or benzodiazepines.

Muscle Relaxants

Cyclobenzaprine and methocarbamol can increase sedation.

Cyclobenzaprine has greater anticholinergic burden. Methocarbamol may be preferable in selected older or delirium-prone patients when a muscle relaxant is clinically necessary.

NSAIDs

Examples include ibuprofen, naproxen, meloxicam, diclofenac and celecoxib.

Hold timing varies by medication, surgeon and procedure. Review renal function, GI bleeding risk, concurrent anticoagulation and spine-fusion restrictions.

Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen is commonly continued and may be used as part of multimodal analgesia.
Avoid duplicate total daily acetaminophen exposure from combination opioid products.

Herbal Supplements

Examples include ginkgo, ginseng, garlic, St. John's wort, valerian and kava.

Herbal supplements may affect bleeding, sedation, cardiovascular function or drug metabolism. Follow the facility/anesthesia preoperative supplement policy.

Procedure-Specific Considerations

Procedure Medication Issues
Total joint arthroplasty Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes drugs, GLP-1/SGLT2 medications, biologics and postoperative VTE prophylaxis.
Spine surgery Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, biologics, chronic pain medications and high bleeding consequence.
SCS / intrathecal procedures Neuraxial anticoagulant/antiplatelet timing is particularly important.
Major revision spine surgery Antithrombotics, anemia, chronic opioids, diabetes medications, biologics and cardiopulmonary GDMT.
Minor hand / superficial procedure Avoid unnecessarily interrupting important chronic medications when bleeding and physiologic stress are minimal.

Key Takeaways

  • Always identify the medication indication before deciding to hold it.
  • Renal function may materially change anticoagulant interruption timing.
  • Dabigatran is particularly dependent on renal clearance.
  • Use the MDCalc perioperative anticoagulation algorithm as an additional decision-support tool.
  • Do not bridge anticoagulation automatically.
  • Recent PCI can make antiplatelet interruption unsafe.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors require multi-day interruption because of euglycemic DKA risk.
  • GLP-1 management should include GI symptoms and aspiration-risk assessment.
  • Type 1 diabetes requires basal insulin during fasting.
  • Continue chronic beta blockers and statins unless contraindicated.
  • Do not abruptly stop steroids, benzodiazepines, opioids, antiseizure medications or Parkinson medications.
  • Biologics and JAK inhibitors require drug-specific perioperative planning.
  • Spine, SCS and intrathecal procedures carry particularly important bleeding consequences.
  • Document exact last dose whenever medication interruption matters.
