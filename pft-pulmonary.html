Pulmonary Function Tests & Preoperative Pulmonary Optimization

COPD, asthma, restrictive disease, respiratory risk and higher-level-of-care assessment

Home Evaluation Pulmonary Cardiac Medications

Purpose

Pulmonary evaluation identifies patients at increased risk for postoperative respiratory complications and determines whether chronic lung disease is sufficiently optimized for the planned procedure and surgical setting.

Pulmonary function tests should answer a specific clinical question. Routine spirometry is not required before every elective nonthoracic operation.

When PFTs May Be Helpful

  • Unexplained dyspnea
  • Suspected but undiagnosed obstructive lung disease
  • Known COPD with uncertain severity
  • Known restrictive lung disease with uncertain severity
  • Significant change in respiratory symptoms
  • Assessment requested by pulmonology or anesthesia
  • When results may alter the surgical setting or perioperative plan
Stable COPD or asthma with well-characterized disease does not automatically require repeat PFTs solely because elective surgery is planned.

Core PFT Measurements

Measurement What It Represents
FEV1 Forced expiratory volume exhaled during the first second.
FVC Total forced vital capacity exhaled after maximal inspiration.
FEV1/FVC Primary spirometric measure used to identify airflow obstruction.
TLC Total lung capacity; useful for confirming restrictive physiology.
RV Residual volume remaining after maximal expiration.
DLCO Diffusing capacity reflecting pulmonary gas-transfer efficiency.

Recognizing Obstructive Physiology

Obstructive disease is characterized by disproportionate reduction in expiratory airflow relative to forced vital capacity.

Typical pattern:

Reduced FEV1/FVC ratio
± Reduced FEV1
± Air trapping or increased RV/TLC

COPD and asthma are common causes, although clinical history remains essential for distinguishing them.

Recognizing Restrictive Physiology

Possible spirometric pattern:

Reduced FVC
Preserved or increased FEV1/FVC
Spirometry alone can suggest restriction but does not confirm it. A reduced total lung capacity is required to establish true restrictive physiology.

Potential Causes

  • Interstitial lung disease
  • Obesity
  • Neuromuscular weakness
  • Chest-wall abnormalities
  • Pleural disease

Mixed Obstructive and Restrictive Pattern

A reduced FEV1/FVC ratio combined with reduced total lung capacity may indicate mixed obstructive and restrictive disease.

Mixed abnormalities should be interpreted with symptoms, imaging, oxygenation, smoking history and pulmonary diagnoses rather than relying on spirometry alone.

Bronchodilator Response

Spirometry performed before and after bronchodilator administration can identify significant improvement in airflow.

A clinically significant bronchodilator response supports reversible airflow limitation but does not by itself distinguish all cases of asthma from COPD.

DLCO Interpretation

DLCO reflects gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary interface.

Finding Possible Explanation
Reduced DLCO with obstruction May support emphysema or pulmonary vascular disease.
Normal/high DLCO with obstruction May be more consistent with asthma or chronic bronchitis.
Reduced DLCO with restriction May suggest interstitial or parenchymal lung disease.
Reduced DLCO with otherwise normal spirometry Consider pulmonary vascular disease, early interstitial disease, emphysema or anemia.

FEV1 and Preoperative Risk

FEV1 provides useful information about severity of airflow limitation but should not be used as the only determinant of operative suitability.

Under TSJH pulmonary criteria, FEV1 below 50% predicted represents a significant pulmonary risk marker and may exclude a patient from routine outpatient surgical pathways or require higher-level-of-care evaluation.

Interpret FEV1 together with oxygen requirements, exacerbation history, symptoms, exercise tolerance and other pulmonary risk measures.

COPD Preoperative Assessment

Review

  • Baseline dyspnea
  • Current cough and sputum
  • Wheezing
  • Recent exacerbations
  • Hospitalizations
  • Home oxygen use
  • Smoking status
  • Maintenance inhalers
  • Rescue inhaler frequency
  • Prior respiratory failure
  • Available PFTs

COPD Optimization

Stable COPD should be treated according to usual disease-management principles rather than with unnecessary preoperative testing.

Optimization May Include

  • Continue maintenance inhalers
  • Ensure rescue bronchodilator availability
  • Treat active exacerbation
  • Address respiratory infection when present
  • Encourage smoking cessation
  • Assess oxygen requirements
  • Use postoperative pulmonary-expansion strategies

Active COPD Exacerbation

Elective surgery should generally be postponed when the patient has an active COPD exacerbation characterized by clinically important worsening dyspnea, wheezing, sputum production, oxygen requirement or respiratory instability.

The patient should return toward baseline before proceeding with routine elective surgery.

Asthma Assessment

Determine current asthma control rather than relying only on the diagnosis listed in the medical history.

Assess

  • Daytime symptoms
  • Nocturnal symptoms
  • Rescue inhaler frequency
  • Recent steroid use
  • Recent emergency visits
  • Recent hospitalization
  • Current wheezing
  • History of intubation for asthma

Poorly Controlled Asthma

Active bronchospasm or poorly controlled asthma should be optimized before elective surgery because airway manipulation may precipitate significant perioperative bronchospasm.
Patients with active wheezing on preoperative examination require reassessment of disease control and treatment before routine elective surgery.

Respiratory Infection

Acute respiratory infection can increase airway reactivity, secretions and postoperative pulmonary complications.

Fever, significant productive cough, lower respiratory infection, pneumonia, acute hypoxemia or clinically important respiratory deterioration generally warrants postponement of elective surgery until adequately treated and clinically improved.

Home Oxygen

Determine whether supplemental oxygen is used intermittently, nocturnally or continuously.

Under TSJH outpatient pulmonary criteria, patients requiring oxygen 24 hours per day are not appropriate for routine outpatient surgical pathways and should be evaluated for a higher level of care.

Chronic Hypercapnic Respiratory Failure

Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure represents significant pulmonary disease and is an exclusion from routine TSJH outpatient surgical pathways. Higher-level-of-care evaluation is appropriate.

Interstitial Pulmonary Fibrosis

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and significant fibrotic interstitial lung disease carry substantial perioperative pulmonary risk and are not appropriate for routine outpatient pathways under TSJH pulmonary criteria.

Review oxygenation, pulmonary function, disease progression, pulmonary hypertension and specialist recommendations.

CAT Score

The COPD Assessment Test quantifies the symptomatic impact of COPD.

Documentation:

CAT score: ___
Under TSJH outpatient criteria, CAT score greater than 10 represents a pulmonary risk marker that should trigger further review of outpatient surgical suitability.

BODE Index

The BODE index incorporates body mass index, airflow obstruction, dyspnea and exercise capacity.

BODE:

B = Body mass index
O = Airflow obstruction
D = Dyspnea
E = Exercise capacity
Under TSJH criteria, BODE score ≥5 represents significant pulmonary risk and may require a higher level of care rather than routine outpatient surgery.

Arozullah Respiratory Failure Risk Index

The Arozullah index can assist with estimation of postoperative respiratory failure risk using patient and procedural characteristics.

Documentation:

Arozullah score: ___
Under TSJH outpatient criteria, Arozullah score ≥4 should prompt review for increased pulmonary risk and outpatient surgical suitability.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

OSA should be considered together with pulmonary disease because sedatives, opioids and anesthesia can worsen postoperative upper-airway obstruction and hypoventilation.

Documentation:

STOP-BANG: ___
Patients using PAP therapy should bring and use PAP whenever asleep postoperatively unless contraindicated.

OSA Intolerant of PAP

For patients with known OSA who are unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy, document PAP intolerance and plan postoperative continuous pulse oximetry. NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen may be used when clinically warranted.

Opioid and sedative exposure should be minimized when feasible.

Pulmonary Hypertension

Pulmonary hypertension requires assessment of etiology, severity, right ventricular function, functional status and current therapy.

TSJH higher-level-of-care red flags include WHO Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension, RVSP greater than 65 mmHg, severe tricuspid regurgitation, moderate-to-severe right ventricular dysfunction or advanced pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

Smoking

Current smoking increases pulmonary and wound complications.

Encourage cessation as early as possible before surgery. Even when the available interval is short, stopping smoking remains beneficial and should not be discouraged because an ideal cessation window is unavailable.

Postoperative Pulmonary Plan

High-risk pulmonary patients benefit from a clearly documented postoperative respiratory plan.

Possible postoperative plan:

PAP anytime asleep when applicable.
RT assistance with PAP/NIPPV as needed.
Continuous pulse oximetry when indicated.
Supplemental oxygen if clinically warranted.
Xopenex or other bronchodilator as indicated.
EZPAP / pulmonary-expansion therapy.
Incentive spirometry.
Early mobilization.
Minimize excessive opioid and sedative exposure.

When Pulmonology Consultation May Be Appropriate

Consider pulmonology evaluation for:
  • Unexplained significant dyspnea
  • FEV1 below institutional threshold
  • New or increasing oxygen requirement
  • Chronic hypercapnia
  • Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis
  • Significant pulmonary hypertension
  • Frequent COPD exacerbations
  • Poorly controlled severe asthma
  • Uncertain pulmonary diagnosis
  • Concern regarding appropriate surgical setting

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:
  • Active COPD exacerbation
  • Active bronchospasm
  • Poorly controlled severe asthma
  • Pneumonia or significant lower respiratory infection
  • New unexplained hypoxemia
  • Acute respiratory failure
  • Significant respiratory deterioration from baseline
  • Unresolved pulmonary condition requiring higher-level-of-care planning

Concise Physician Interpretation Examples

Obstructive Pattern

PFTs demonstrate obstructive ventilatory physiology with FEV1 ___% predicted and reduced FEV1/FVC ratio, consistent with known obstructive lung disease. Clinical significance should be interpreted with current symptoms, oxygen requirement and exacerbation history.

Possible Restriction

Spirometry demonstrates reduced FVC with preserved FEV1/FVC ratio, suggesting possible restrictive physiology. Spirometry alone does not confirm restriction; lung-volume measurement showing reduced TLC is required for confirmation.

Stable COPD

COPD is clinically stable without current exacerbation, active wheezing, new oxygen requirement or lower respiratory infection. Continue maintenance inhaler therapy and rescue bronchodilator as needed. Recommend postoperative pulmonary-expansion measures and early mobilization.

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is pulmonary disease clinically stable? If yes, continue optimization and assess procedural risk.
Are PFTs needed? Order only when results will clarify diagnosis, severity or management.
Is FEV1 <50% predicted? Apply TSJH higher-risk pathway and review surgical setting.
Does the patient require continuous oxygen? Evaluate for higher level of care.
Is COPD or asthma actively exacerbated? Optimize before elective surgery.
Is pulmonary fibrosis or chronic hypercapnia present? Higher-level-of-care evaluation is appropriate.
Does OSA require postoperative precautions? Establish PAP, oximetry and opioid-sparing plan.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not order routine PFTs for every elective surgery patient.
  • Reduced FEV1/FVC suggests obstruction.
  • Restriction requires confirmation with reduced TLC.
  • DLCO provides information about pulmonary gas transfer.
  • FEV1 should not be interpreted in isolation.
  • FEV1 <50% predicted is a TSJH higher-risk marker.
  • Continuous home oxygen requires higher-level-of-care review.
  • BODE ≥5, CAT >10 and Arozullah ≥4 are important TSJH pulmonary risk markers.
  • Active COPD exacerbation or bronchospasm should be optimized before elective surgery.
  • Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure and IPF are not routine outpatient conditions.
  • OSA requires a specific postoperative respiratory plan.
  • Pulmonary hypertension severity and RV function can change the appropriate surgical setting.
