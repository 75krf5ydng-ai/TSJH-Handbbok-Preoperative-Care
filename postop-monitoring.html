Postoperative Monitoring, Higher-Level-of-Care & Disposition

Cardiac surveillance, respiratory monitoring, mobility, discharge readiness and escalation planning

Home Evaluation Cardiac Pulmonary Screening Tools Communication

Purpose

Postoperative planning should begin during the preoperative evaluation. Patients with significant cardiac, pulmonary, frailty, mobility or medication risk may require enhanced monitoring, overnight observation or a higher level of care.

The appropriate postoperative pathway should be based on the patient's baseline risk and planned procedure rather than waiting for a complication to occur.

Postoperative Monitoring Domains

Domain Key Issues
Cardiac Ischemia, arrhythmia, heart failure, BNP/NT-proBNP surveillance pathway.
Respiratory OSA, hypoxemia, COPD, PAP/NIPPV use, opioid-related respiratory depression.
Hemodynamic Hypotension, hypertension, orthostasis, bleeding and fluid balance.
Neurologic Delirium, focal deficits, excessive sedation, seizure risk.
Renal Urine output, AKI risk, electrolytes and volume status.
Mobility Fall risk, walker use, PT clearance and disposition.
GI Nausea, vomiting, constipation and postoperative ileus.
Pain Adequate analgesia while minimizing excessive sedation and respiratory depression.

BNP Screening SmartPhrase

BNP screening. Obtain BNP because of age greater than 65 years and RCRI score of 1. If BNP is 92 pg/mL or greater, implement the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.

Postoperative EKG

A postoperative EKG should be obtained when required by protocol or when new cardiac symptoms or clinically important rhythm changes occur.

Indications May Include

  • BNP/NT-proBNP surveillance pathway
  • Chest pain
  • New dyspnea
  • Palpitations or arrhythmia
  • Unexplained hypotension
  • Significant troponin elevation
  • New heart-failure concern

Postoperative Troponin Interpretation

Postoperative troponin elevation should not be dismissed solely because the patient does not report chest pain. Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery may be clinically silent.

Interpret changes with symptoms, EKG findings, hemodynamics, renal function and the clinical context. Clinically important elevation should prompt appropriate medical evaluation.

OSA Postoperative Monitoring

Patients with obstructive sleep apnea are at increased risk for airway obstruction and respiratory depression after anesthesia, opioids and sedating medications.

PAP should be used anytime asleep postoperatively when prescribed and tolerated, unless a specific contraindication exists.
OSA Postoperative Plan

PAP anytime asleep.
RT assist as needed.
Continuous pulse oximetry when indicated.
Minimize excessive opioid and sedative exposure.
Supplemental oxygen and/or NIPPV when clinically warranted.

OSA Intolerant of PAP

For known OSA with PAP intolerance, document that the patient is not using PAP and plan postoperative continuous pulse oximetry. NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen should be available when clinically warranted.
Supplemental oxygen may improve oxygen saturation without fully correcting hypoventilation or airway obstruction. Clinical monitoring remains important.

Continuous Pulse Oximetry

Consider for

  • Known significant OSA
  • PAP-intolerant OSA
  • High STOP-BANG score with additional risk factors
  • Chronic pulmonary disease with respiratory risk
  • Postoperative oxygen requirement
  • High opioid exposure
  • Frailty with sedative sensitivity
  • Prior respiratory depression

COPD / Asthma Monitoring

Patients with chronic obstructive disease should continue maintenance therapy and be observed for bronchospasm, increased oxygen need and respiratory deterioration.

Postoperative wheezing, increased work of breathing, new hypoxemia or inability to maintain baseline respiratory status requires prompt reassessment.

Heart Failure Higher-Level-of-Care Considerations

EF below 35% generally warrants higher-level-of-care review under TSJH criteria.
For EF ≥35%, evaluate current symptoms, recent admissions, functional status, GDMT, ischemic findings and cardiology follow-up when determining appropriate venue and postoperative monitoring.

Hemodynamic Monitoring

Postoperative blood pressure should be interpreted relative to the patient's baseline and the clinical setting.

Evaluate Significant Hypotension For

  • Bleeding
  • Volume depletion
  • Medication effect
  • Cardiac ischemia
  • Arrhythmia
  • Sepsis
  • Adrenal insufficiency when relevant

Orthostatic Symptoms

Dizziness or near-syncope during early mobilization is common after surgery but should not automatically be dismissed.

Assess blood pressure, heart rate, volume status, hemoglobin, medication effects, pain, nausea and other contributing factors.
Unexplained persistent orthostasis, syncope or hemodynamic instability requires further evaluation before discharge.

Postoperative Renal Monitoring

Higher-Risk Patients May Require

  • Repeat BMP
  • Creatinine monitoring
  • Potassium monitoring
  • Urine-output assessment
  • Fluid balance review
  • Renal medication adjustment
New oliguria, significant creatinine rise or major electrolyte disturbance should be evaluated before discharge.

Postoperative Anemia

Postoperative hemoglobin should be interpreted in the context of expected blood loss, symptoms, hemodynamics and cardiovascular disease.

A low hemoglobin value alone does not mandate transfusion in a stable patient. Assess symptoms, active bleeding and the overall clinical picture.

Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting

Persistent nausea or vomiting can interfere with hydration, oral medications, mobility and discharge readiness.

Evaluate For

  • Anesthetic effect
  • Opioids
  • Hypotension
  • Ileus
  • Medication adverse effects
  • Electrolyte abnormalities

Ileus / Constipation Risk

Spine and orthopedic patients frequently receive opioids and muscle relaxants that increase constipation risk.

Possible Postoperative Bowel Plan

Scheduled bowel regimen as appropriate.
Encourage hydration and mobility.
Magnesium citrate x1 when clinically indicated.
Reglan IVP may be considered for nausea/ileus risk when clinically appropriate.
Persistent abdominal distention, vomiting, inability to tolerate intake or suspected ileus should be evaluated before discharge.

Pain Management

Adequate pain control is necessary for mobility and pulmonary recovery, but excess sedation increases fall and respiratory risk.

Use multimodal analgesia when appropriate and minimize unnecessary sedative burden, especially in older adults, frail patients and those with OSA.

Monitor

  • Pain score and function
  • Sedation
  • Respiratory rate
  • Oxygen saturation
  • Nausea
  • Constipation
  • Ability to participate in PT

Muscle Relaxants

Cyclobenzaprine can contribute to anticholinergic burden. Methocarbamol may be a more appropriate choice in selected older or delirium-prone patients when a muscle relaxant is clinically required.

Postoperative Delirium

Acute confusion after surgery should be evaluated rather than automatically attributed to anesthesia or age.

Common Contributors

  • Medication effect
  • Hypoxemia
  • Infection
  • Urinary retention
  • Constipation
  • Dehydration
  • Electrolyte abnormality
  • Pain
  • Sleep disruption

Delirium Prevention

Orientation

Hydration
Adequate but nonexcessive pain control

Urinary Retention

Postoperative urinary retention may delay discharge and increase discomfort or delirium risk.

Assess voiding history, bladder volume when indicated, medications, anesthesia type and procedure-specific risk.

Fall Risk

Common Contributors

  • Orthostasis
  • Opioids
  • Muscle relaxants
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Neuropathy
  • Frailty
  • Residual anesthesia
  • Lower extremity weakness

When Overnight Observation May Be Appropriate

  • Higher OSA/respiratory risk
  • Significant cardiac surveillance need
  • Frailty
  • Inadequate mobility
  • Poor pain control
  • Persistent nausea
  • Orthostatic symptoms
  • Medical comorbidity requiring laboratory reassessment
  • Inadequate home support

Outpatient vs Inpatient Pathway

Factor Outpatient More Appropriate Inpatient / Higher-Level Consideration
Cardiac status Stable, low residual risk Surveillance or instability concerns
Pulmonary status Baseline without significant oxygen need Continuous oxygen, hypercapnia or major pulmonary risk
Frailty Independent or mildly vulnerable Moderate/severe frailty with recovery concerns
Mobility Expected rapid safe ambulation Significant assistance or rehab needs
Procedure Limited duration/blood loss Complex or prolonged surgery
Home support Reliable caregiver/transport Unsafe or inadequate support

Postoperative DVT Prophylaxis

DVT prophylaxis is procedure-specific and should follow the surgeon's pathway.

Document the prescribed pharmacologic and mechanical prophylaxis, mobility plan and timing of chronic anticoagulant/antiplatelet resumption when relevant.

Postoperative Antithrombotic Resumption

The timing of restarting chronic anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy should be based on hemostasis, procedural bleeding risk, thrombotic indication and the surgeon's/anesthesia or specialist's plan.

For patients with prior TIA or stroke on clopidogrel, postoperative resumption should follow the surgeon-directed plan when surgical bleeding risk is relevant.

Higher-Level-of-Care Escalation

Escalate when the patient's postoperative needs exceed the monitoring or treatment capability of the planned setting, including:

Persistent respiratory instability
Need for advanced ventilatory support
Hemodynamic instability
Active cardiac ischemia
Serious arrhythmia
Significant neurologic change
Major bleeding
Progressive renal failure
Sepsis
Other need for continuous or advanced monitoring

Key Takeaways

  • Plan postoperative monitoring before surgery for higher-risk patients.
  • Apply BNP/NT-proBNP surveillance thresholds when protocol criteria are met.
  • OSA patients require deliberate postoperative respiratory planning.
  • PAP-intolerant OSA should prompt continuous oximetry and escalation options.
  • Use pulmonary higher-level-of-care criteria before selecting an outpatient pathway.
  • Monitor frail patients for delirium, falls and discharge needs.
  • Safe mobility is a discharge requirement, not an optional extra.
  • Persistent orthostasis, hypoxemia, vomiting or urinary retention may delay discharge.
  • Transfusion decisions should consider symptoms and clinical context rather than hemoglobin alone.
  • Restart chronic antithrombotic therapy according to bleeding and thrombotic risk.
  • Escalate to a higher level of care when monitoring needs exceed facility capability.
