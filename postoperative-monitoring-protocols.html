Postoperative Monitoring & Surveillance Protocols

Cardiac, respiratory, metabolic, renal, neurologic and medical monitoring after elective spine and joint surgery

Purpose

Preoperative optimization should identify patients who require additional postoperative monitoring before they arrive for surgery. The postoperative plan should reflect cardiovascular risk, OSA and pulmonary disease, diabetes, renal function, frailty, medications and procedure-related physiologic stress.

Postoperative surveillance should be targeted to the patient's actual risk. Routine testing in every patient can create unnecessary cost and false-positive results, while failure to recognize higher-risk patients can delay recognition of perioperative complications.

Preoperative Monitoring Decision

Before surgery ask:

Does patient require postoperative cardiac surveillance?

Does patient require continuous pulse oximetry?

Does patient require PAP/NIPPV support?

Does patient require glucose surveillance?

Does patient require renal/electrolyte monitoring?

Does patient require delirium/fall precautions?

Does patient require a higher level of postoperative care?

TSJH BNP / NT-proBNP Cardiac Surveillance

Under the applicable TSJH protocol:

BNP ≥92 pg/mL
or
NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L

→ Postoperative EKG
→ Daily troponins for 48–72 hours

The surveillance plan should be documented during the preoperative evaluation so the postoperative team knows the protocol is required.

BNP Screening SmartPhrase

BNP screening. Obtain BNP because of age greater than 65 years and RCRI score of 1. If BNP is 92 pg/mL or greater, implement the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.

When Postoperative EKG May Be Important

  • BNP/NT-proBNP surveillance pathway triggered
  • New chest discomfort
  • New dyspnea
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • New significant arrhythmia
  • Unexplained tachycardia
  • Clinical concern for myocardial injury

Troponin Surveillance

Perioperative myocardial injury may occur without classic chest pain, especially after major surgery. Protocol-directed troponin surveillance helps identify higher-risk postoperative myocardial injury.
An elevated postoperative troponin requires clinical interpretation. Evaluate trend, symptoms, EKG findings, hemodynamics, renal function and other possible causes of myocardial injury.

Telemetry

Telemetry should be targeted to patients with a clinical indication rather than used routinely for every postoperative patient.

Possible indications include

  • Clinically significant arrhythmia
  • Recent uncontrolled AF/flutter
  • Significant conduction disease
  • Recent ventricular arrhythmia
  • Hemodynamic instability
  • Other cardiovascular indication

PAP Therapy

For patients who use PAP at baseline, recommend PAP anytime asleep unless contraindicated by the surgical/anesthesia team.
Respiratory therapy assistance may help ensure correct device use and improve adherence during the postoperative period.

PAP-Intolerant OSA

Patients with known OSA who cannot tolerate PAP require a deliberate postoperative respiratory plan rather than simply documenting “noncompliant.”
Continuous pulse oximetry when indicated

NIPPV if clinically warranted

Supplemental oxygen if clinically warranted

Minimize unnecessary sedatives

Use multimodal analgesia

Monitor for opioid-related respiratory depression

Continuous Pulse Oximetry

Consider for

  • Higher-risk OSA
  • PAP-intolerant OSA
  • Significant COPD
  • Chronic opioid therapy
  • Concurrent opioids and benzodiazepines
  • Prior postoperative hypoxemia
  • Significant obesity with hypoventilation concern
  • Other respiratory-risk patients

Postoperative Pulmonary Expansion

Incentive spirometry

Early mobilization

Upright positioning

EZPAP when clinically indicated

Bronchodilator therapy when indicated

Xopenex when clinically appropriate

RT support as needed

COPD / Asthma Monitoring

Continue maintenance inhalers and monitor for bronchospasm, hypoxemia, increased work of breathing or postoperative exacerbation.
New oxygen requirement, progressive wheezing, hypercapnia or respiratory distress requires prompt evaluation rather than routine postoperative observation.

Higher-Risk Pulmonary Patients

Additional postoperative planning is especially important with:

FEV1 <50% predicted
BODE ≥5
CAT >10
Arozullah ≥4
24-hour oxygen requirement
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Pulmonary hypertension

Pulmonary Hypertension

Pulmonary hypertension may create perioperative right-ventricular and hemodynamic risk.

Higher-risk findings include:

WHO Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension
RVSP >65 mmHg
Severe TR
Moderate-to-severe RV dysfunction
Advanced pulmonary vasodilator therapy

These patients may require higher-level monitoring or a different surgical setting.

Postoperative Glucose Monitoring

Patients with diabetes or clinically significant perioperative hyperglycemia should have glucose monitored according to the institutional glycemic protocol.

Particular attention

  • Type 1 diabetes
  • Insulin-treated diabetes
  • Insulin pump users
  • Major surgery
  • Perioperative steroids
  • Poor baseline glycemic control
  • Recent hypoglycemia

Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 patients require ongoing basal insulin coverage. Postoperative insulin management should prevent both hypoglycemia and ketosis.

SGLT2 Postoperative Monitoring

Do not restart an SGLT2 inhibitor simply because surgery is finished.

Restart when the patient is clinically stable, adequately hydrated and eating normally, with no concern for perioperative ketosis.

Evaluate for euglycemic DKA when there is

  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Abdominal pain
  • Tachypnea
  • Unexpected metabolic acidosis
  • Ketones
  • Unexplained postoperative illness

Renal Monitoring

Consider postoperative BMP/CMP when there is

  • CKD
  • Preoperative AKI
  • Major fluid shifts
  • Significant blood loss
  • Hyperkalemia risk
  • Diuretic/RAAS medication issues
  • Contrast exposure
  • Other renal-risk condition

AKI Prevention

Maintain appropriate volume status

Avoid unnecessary nephrotoxins

Avoid NSAIDs when clinically inappropriate

Review renally cleared medications

Monitor urine output when clinically indicated

Repeat renal function when risk warrants

Anemia / Blood Loss Monitoring

Postoperative CBC monitoring should be based on procedure, expected blood loss, preoperative hemoglobin, symptoms and intraoperative events.

More likely to require CBC

  • TKA/THA with clinically relevant blood loss
  • Bilateral arthroplasty
  • Multilevel spine fusion
  • Revision spine surgery
  • Significant intraoperative blood loss
  • Preoperative anemia
  • Symptoms suggesting postoperative anemia

Postoperative Bleeding Surveillance

Evaluate unexpected:

Hemodynamic instability
Rapid hemoglobin decline
Excessive drain output
Expanding hematoma
Neurologic change after spine/neuraxial procedure
Significant wound bleeding
Other evidence of active blood loss

VTE Surveillance

Routine diagnostic imaging is not required in asymptomatic patients solely because they underwent orthopedic surgery.

Evaluate for possible VTE with:

New unilateral leg swelling
Calf/thigh pain with concerning features
Unexplained hypoxemia
New tachycardia
Pleuritic chest pain
Acute dyspnea
Syncope
Other clinically compatible symptoms

DVT Prophylaxis

DVT prophylaxis is guided by the surgeon and procedure-specific pathway.

The medical team should identify conflicts involving chronic anticoagulant/ antiplatelet therapy, renal function, active bleeding or other relevant factors.

Urinary Retention

Urinary retention may occur after anesthesia, opioids and spine/orthopedic surgery.

Assess

  • Bladder symptoms
  • Bladder scan when indicated
  • Opioid burden
  • Baseline BPH/urinary retention

Constipation Prevention

Orthopedic and spine patients commonly receive opioids and have reduced mobility, placing them at high risk for constipation.

Scheduled bowel regimen

Hydration as appropriate

Early mobility

Minimize unnecessary anticholinergic medications

Escalate treatment when constipation persists

Frailty / Delirium Surveillance

Frailty, advanced age, polypharmacy, opioids, benzodiazepines, sleep disruption and acute illness increase postoperative delirium risk.

Prevention strategies

  • Orientation
  • Day/night cues
  • Early mobility
  • Glasses/hearing aids
  • Hydration
  • Adequate pain control without excessive sedation
  • Avoid unnecessary anticholinergic drugs
  • Avoid unnecessary benzodiazepines

Medication-Related Delirium Risk

Cyclobenzaprine has greater anticholinergic burden. Methocarbamol may be preferable in selected older or delirium-prone patients when a muscle relaxant is clinically required.

Fall Prevention

Baseline assistive device available

Orthostatic symptoms assessed

Sedating medications reviewed

PT/OT mobilization as appropriate

Call assistance reinforced

Environment free of hazards

Bradycardia

Do not treat a vital-sign abnormality in isolation without considering pain, volume status, bleeding, infection, medications and cardiopulmonary disease.

Persistent Postoperative Tachycardia

Consider

  • Pain
  • Anxiety
  • Hypovolemia
  • Anemia/blood loss
  • Hypoxemia
  • Fever/infection
  • Arrhythmia
  • PE
  • Withdrawal
  • Other causes

Postoperative Hypotension

Consider

  • Hypovolemia
  • Blood loss
  • Medication effect
  • Anesthesia effect
  • Cardiac dysfunction
  • Sepsis
  • Other causes
Persistent symptomatic hypotension requires evaluation rather than simply holding every chronic antihypertensive indefinitely.

Postoperative Fever

Early low-grade postoperative fever can occur from inflammatory responses and does not automatically represent bacterial infection.
Evaluate persistent, high, symptomatic or clinically concerning fever according to symptoms, timing, examination and procedure.

Postoperative Infection Evaluation

Avoid reflexive broad testing without a clinical indication.

Evaluate according to symptoms

  • Respiratory symptoms → pulmonary evaluation
  • Urinary symptoms → urinary evaluation
  • Wound findings → surgical-site evaluation
  • Systemic instability → broader infection/sepsis assessment

Urine Testing After Surgery

Do not diagnose UTI solely because an older adult develops nonspecific weakness or confusion and urine testing shows pyuria/bacteriuria.

Assess urinary symptoms and alternative causes of postoperative delirium or functional decline.

Medication Resumption

Medication General Postoperative Principle
Beta blocker Resume/continue unless contraindicated by hemodynamics.
Statin Continue/resume.
ACE/ARB/ARNI Resume when BP, renal function and volume status are appropriate.
Diuretic Resume according to volume status and renal function.
Anticoagulant Resume when adequate hemostasis and surgeon-directed timing permit.
Antiplatelet Restart according to surgeon/cardiology plan.
SGLT2 inhibitor Restart only when clinically stable and eating/hydrated.
GLP-1 Resume according to clinical status and prescribing schedule.

Key Takeaways

  • Plan postoperative monitoring during the preoperative evaluation.
  • BNP ≥92 or NT-proBNP ≥300 triggers the applicable TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance pathway.
  • Higher-risk OSA may require continuous pulse oximetry.
  • PAP should be used anytime asleep when prescribed and tolerated.
  • PAP-intolerant OSA requires an alternative respiratory monitoring plan.
  • Type 1 diabetes requires continued basal insulin coverage.
  • Do not restart SGLT2 therapy until the patient is stable and eating/hydrated.
  • Postoperative labs should be targeted to clinical and procedural risk.
  • Persistent tachycardia warrants evaluation for pain, blood loss, hypoxemia, PE and other causes.
  • New neurologic deficits after spine surgery require prompt escalation.
  • Frailty and medication burden increase delirium and fall risk.
  • Routine urine testing should not substitute for evaluation of actual urinary symptoms.
  • Medication restart plans should be established before discharge.
