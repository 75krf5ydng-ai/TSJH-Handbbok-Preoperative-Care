Preoperative Red Flags, Escalation & “Do Not Miss” Findings

Rapid recognition of conditions that may require additional evaluation, surgical delay or higher-level-of-care planning

Home Evaluation Cardiac Pulmonary Readiness Resources

Purpose

Most preoperative abnormalities do not require cancellation. The challenge is recognizing the smaller group of findings that represent active medical instability, require additional evaluation or exceed the capabilities of the planned surgical setting.

This chapter is designed as a rapid provider safety check. A red flag does not always mean the procedure must be cancelled, but it does mean the issue should be deliberately addressed before final optimization status is assigned.

Three Levels of Preoperative Concern

Level Meaning Typical Action
Immediate Possible acute medical emergency or active unstable condition. Stop routine elective pathway and obtain urgent clinical evaluation.
Pending / Escalate Potentially significant abnormality requiring clarification or optimization. Obtain required testing, records, specialist or anesthesia review before final status.
Monitor / Proceed Stable chronic abnormality with adequate evaluation and perioperative plan. Document and proceed based on overall risk.

Immediate Cardiac Red Flags

Stop the routine elective pathway for:

Active chest pain concerning for ischemia
Suspected acute coronary syndrome
New ST elevation concerning for acute MI
Decompensated heart failure
Pulmonary edema
Unstable ventricular arrhythmia
Sustained symptomatic SVT with instability
Symptomatic severe bradycardia
New unstable complete heart block
Hemodynamic instability from an arrhythmia

Cardiac Findings Requiring Further Review

Finding Suggested Action
New LBBB Compare prior tracing and apply cardiac evaluation pathway.
Mobitz II Cardiology/EP evaluation.
Complete heart block Urgent cardiology/EP evaluation.
New AF/flutter Assess rate, symptoms, anticoagulation and underlying trigger.
AF with RVR Control rate and evaluate before elective surgery.
Unexplained syncope Determine cardiac/neurologic cause before elective anesthesia when clinically significant.
New ischemic EKG change Additional cardiovascular evaluation.
Marked QTc prolongation Review medications, potassium, magnesium and reversible causes.

Heart Failure Red Flags

New or worsening orthopnea
New PND
Increasing edema
Rapid unexplained weight gain
Rest dyspnea
Pulmonary congestion
Recent CHF hospitalization
New oxygen requirement related to congestion
Evidence of cardiogenic instability
Stable compensated heart failure is different from active decompensated heart failure. The latter should be treated before routine elective surgery.

HFrEF / Ischemic Cardiomyopathy

Under the TSJH pathway, EF <35% is a higher-level-of-care concern.
For EF ≥35%, review current symptoms, NYHA status, cardiology follow-up, GDMT, functional capacity, recent CHF admissions and EKG findings according to the applicable pathway.

Aortic Stenosis Red Flags

Concerning features include:

Exertional syncope/presyncope
Angina
Heart-failure symptoms
Known severe AS
Rapid symptom progression
Severe AS may include:

AVA <1.0 cm²
Mean gradient >40 mmHg
Vmax >4.0 m/s
Severe aortic stenosis is not appropriate for routine TSJH elective surgical management under the established facility pathway.

Mitral Stenosis Red Flags

Severe MS may include:

MVA ≤1.5 cm²
Mean gradient ≥10 mmHg
PASP >50 mmHg
Moderate-to-severe or symptomatic mitral stenosis requires appropriate cardiovascular assessment before elective surgery.

Pulmonary Hypertension Red Flags

Higher-level-of-care concerns include:

WHO Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension
RVSP >65 mmHg
Severe tricuspid regurgitation
Moderate-to-severe RV dysfunction
Advanced pulmonary vasodilator therapy
Syncope related to pulmonary hypertension
Right-heart failure

OSA Red Flags

Additional concern when OSA is combined with:

High opioid requirements
Benzodiazepines
Multiple sedating medications
Known hypoventilation
Severe obesity
PAP intolerance
Prior postoperative respiratory event
Daytime hypoxemia/hypercapnia
For PAP-intolerant OSA, establish postoperative continuous pulse oximetry when indicated and have NIPPV and/or supplemental oxygen available when clinically warranted.

Neurologic Emergency Red Flags

New unilateral weakness
New facial droop
Acute speech difficulty
New aphasia
Acute visual loss
New severe focal sensory deficit
New unexplained ataxia
Acute confusion with focal findings
Suspected acute stroke/TIA

These findings require urgent neurologic evaluation rather than routine preoperative assessment.

Recent Stroke / TIA

Recent cerebrovascular events require timing review before elective surgery. Under the local anesthesia pathway, approximately one year may be preferred after stroke when feasible, with shorter intervals such as approximately 8–9 months requiring individualized discussion.

Renal Red Flags

Progressive AKI
Significant hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia

Unexpected potassium elevation should be confirmed when hemolysis or specimen error is possible.

True significant hyperkalemia—especially with EKG changes, worsening kidney function or progressive elevation—requires treatment before routine elective surgery.

DKA
Euglycemic DKA

Severe symptomatic hypoglycemia
Severe dehydration
Significant metabolic acidosis

SGLT2 Inhibitor Red Flag

A patient who recently used an SGLT2 inhibitor and presents with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, malaise, tachypnea or unexplained acidosis requires evaluation for euglycemic DKA even when glucose is not markedly elevated.

GLP-1 Aspiration Red Flags

Active vomiting
Significant nausea
Marked bloating
Severe early satiety
Known gastroparesis
Recent dose escalation with significant GI symptoms
Other concern for delayed gastric emptying

Coordinate concerning findings with anesthesia before elective sedation or general anesthesia.

Thyroid storm
Overt uncontrolled thyrotoxicosis

Hematology Red Flags

Active clinically significant bleeding
Rapidly falling hemoglobin
Severe symptomatic anemia

Possible active hemolysis
Pancytopenia
Severe neutropenia before implant surgery

High-Blood-Loss Surgery + Anemia

A hemoglobin value that may be acceptable for a minor hand procedure may deserve additional optimization before a multilevel revision spine fusion or other operation with substantial expected blood loss.
Consider the hemoglobin trend, etiology, symptoms, cardiovascular reserve and whether the patient accepts transfusion.

Anticoagulant hold not completed
Unknown last dose
Unexpectedly elevated INR

Recent thromboembolism

Active bleeding while anticoagulated

Antiplatelet Red Flags

Recent PCI/stent with uncertain antiplatelet interruption
Dual antiplatelet therapy with no plan
Clopidogrel not held before high-bleeding-consequence procedure when hold is required
Aspirin plan uncertain before spine/SCS/intrathecal procedure
History of recent stroke/TIA with unclear antiplatelet strategy

Recent ICD shock
Possible device malfunction
Battery near end of life in a pacing-dependent patient
Lead malfunction
Unknown pacing dependence with anticipated EMI
ICD with no tachytherapy plan for significant EMI
Prone procedure with an inaccessible magnet strategy
Device reprogramming with no postoperative restoration plan

Infection Red Flags

Sepsis
Bacteremia
Active pneumonia
Pyelonephritis
Active infected wound near operative site
Significant cellulitis
Dialysis-access infection
Uncontrolled prosthetic/joint infection concern
Active systemic infection before implant surgery
An isolated abnormal urinalysis or asymptomatic bacteriuria is not equivalent to active systemic infection.

Urinary Findings — What Is NOT Automatically a Red Flag

Pyuria without symptoms
Positive leukocyte esterase without symptoms
Positive nitrite without symptoms
Asymptomatic bacteriuria
Mixed-flora contaminated specimen

Treat the patient and clinical syndrome—not the urine result in isolation.

Blood Pressure Red Flags

An isolated elevated reading should usually be repeated under appropriate conditions.

Persistent severe hypertension accompanied by chest pain, neurologic symptoms, acute heart-failure symptoms or other evidence of end-organ injury requires urgent evaluation.
Stable asymptomatic chronic hypertension should be distinguished from a hypertensive emergency. Do not treat one isolated anxious preoperative reading as an emergency without clinical context.

Vital-Sign Red Flags

Finding Action
Persistent unexplained tachycardia Assess pain, anxiety, dehydration, infection, anemia, PE, arrhythmia and other causes.
Significant unexplained bradycardia Review medications and conduction disease; evaluate symptoms.
New hypoxemia Determine pulmonary/cardiac cause before proceeding.
Fever Assess infectious source and procedure implications.
Symptomatic hypotension Evaluate volume status, medications, cardiac disease, infection and other causes.

Recent Hospitalization Red Flag

A recent ER visit or hospitalization does not automatically cancel surgery, but the reason must be understood before final optimization.

Determine

  • Why was the patient evaluated/admitted?
  • Has the condition resolved?
  • Were new medications started?
  • Were important tests performed?
  • Was follow-up recommended?
  • Has baseline function returned?

Higher-Level-of-Care Triggers

Some patients may be medically stable but inappropriate for the planned outpatient setting.

Examples include:

EF <35% under TSJH pathway
Severe pulmonary hypertension criteria
24-hour oxygen dependence
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Complex dialysis requirements
Severe frailty
Major anticipated blood loss with limited rescue options
Need for ICU-level postoperative monitoring
Other risk exceeding facility capability

Stable nonspecific EKG changes
Occasional PACs/PVCs
Mild stable anemia
Stable CKD
Mild chronic transaminase elevation
Stable COPD/asthma
Stable OSA with an appropriate respiratory plan
Asymptomatic bacteriuria
Elevated A1c without acute metabolic instability
Advanced age alone
Walker use
Stable remote stroke deficits
Stable chronic opioid therapy
Stable treated hypothyroidism

Key Takeaways

  • Not every abnormality is a cancellation.
  • Always distinguish stable chronic disease from acute instability.
  • Active cardiac disease should be recognized before routine elective surgery.
  • New hypoxemia or respiratory decompensation requires evaluation.
  • Recent stroke/TIA timing matters.
  • DKA, HHS and severe metabolic instability require treatment before elective surgery.
  • Progressive AKI, major electrolyte abnormalities and uncontrolled volume disturbance matter.
  • Active systemic infection is different from asymptomatic bacteriuria.
  • Procedure-specific bleeding consequence matters as much as the laboratory number.
  • Recent ICD shocks or unresolved device plans should not reach the day of surgery unresolved.
  • Some medically stable patients still require a higher level of care.
  • Every unresolved red flag must have a named owner and follow-up plan.
  • Do not assign Optimized while a clinically meaningful red flag remains unresolved.
