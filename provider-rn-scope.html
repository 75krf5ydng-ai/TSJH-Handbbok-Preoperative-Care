Preoperative Provider & RN Scope

Optimization responsibilities, standing protocols, escalation pathways and multidisciplinary collaboration

Home Evaluation Communication Cancellation Prevention Resources

Purpose

Efficient preoperative optimization requires clear separation between data collection, protocol-driven nursing functions, medical decision-making and specialty consultation.

The preoperative provider should manage medical issues that can reasonably be evaluated and optimized within their scope rather than automatically referring every abnormality to a PCP or specialist.
Actual scope of practice, standing orders, credentialing, collaborative requirements and institutional policies always supersede this educational workflow.

Three-Level Workflow

Level Primary Function Examples
RN / Protocol Collect, verify, screen, summarize and initiate approved standing orders. Vitals, medication reconciliation, screening tools, record retrieval, protocol labs.
Preoperative Provider Interpret, diagnose, risk stratify, optimize and determine whether escalation is required. Lab interpretation, medication plan, risk assessment, optimization status.
Specialist / Anesthesia Manage disease or procedural questions requiring specialty expertise or higher-level decision-making. Unstable CAD, severe valve disease, complex CIED management, severe pulmonary disease.

RN Screening Tools

When incorporated into facility workflow and policy, nursing staff may collect standardized screening information for provider interpretation.

Tool Purpose
STOP-BANG OSA screening
DASI Self-reported functional capacity
CFS Frailty assessment
AWOL-S Postoperative delirium-risk screening
CAT COPD symptom burden
The RN may collect or calculate approved standardized screening scores, while the provider incorporates those findings into the medical risk assessment and perioperative plan.

RN Medical Record Summarization

An RN can support efficient preoperative review by identifying and organizing relevant records according to institutional policy.

Examples

  • Most recent cardiology visit
  • Most recent echocardiogram
  • Stress testing
  • Heart catheterization
  • Pacemaker/ICD interrogation
  • Pulmonary visit
  • PFT
  • Sleep study
  • Neurology records
  • Hospital discharge summaries
Record summarization should preserve the original clinician's findings and should not substitute for provider interpretation of clinically significant results.

RN Standing Orders

Standing orders can improve workflow when they are formally approved and define which patients qualify for testing.

Potential Protocol-Driven Orders

  • CBC
  • BMP/CMP
  • A1c
  • PT/INR
  • PTT
  • UA when indicated by institutional protocol
  • EKG when protocol criteria are met
  • MRSA/MSSA screening when required
  • Pregnancy testing when indicated
Nursing standing orders must be authorized by the facility and should not be expanded beyond the approved protocol.

Conditions the Preoperative Provider Can Often Address Directly

Issue Potential Provider Action
Stable hypertension Review trend, medication adherence and perioperative medication plan.
Mild anemia Classify, investigate when appropriate and initiate reasonable optimization.
Prediabetes / stable diabetes Review A1c/glucose and create perioperative medication instructions.
Stable CKD Review renal function, medications, electrolytes and NSAID risk.
Stable COPD/asthma Assess symptoms, inhaler use, recent exacerbations and postoperative pulmonary plan.
OSA Assess PAP adherence/intolerance and establish postoperative monitoring recommendations.
Stable hypothyroidism Review treatment and available thyroid studies when clinically relevant.
Medication abnormalities Reconcile and address perioperative holds/continuation within approved practice.
Constipation risk Establish postoperative prevention plan.
Frailty Assess CFS and incorporate into mobility/disposition planning.

Abnormal Labs the Provider Can Often Evaluate

  • Mild anemia
  • Mild leukocytosis without infection symptoms
  • Mild thrombocytosis
  • Mild glucose elevation
  • Prediabetic A1c
  • Stable CKD
  • Mild electrolyte abnormality
  • Isolated mild liver-enzyme abnormality
  • Abnormal UA without urinary symptoms
An abnormal result does not automatically require PCP or specialist referral. The provider should first determine its severity, chronicity, clinical context and perioperative significance.

When PCP Involvement Is Helpful

PCP referral is most useful when the issue requires longitudinal disease management rather than simply perioperative risk assessment.

Examples

  • Newly diagnosed chronic disease requiring ongoing treatment
  • Poorly controlled hypertension requiring longitudinal titration
  • New diabetes requiring long-term management
  • Unexplained constitutional symptoms
  • Complex chronic medication management outside perioperative scope
  • Need for longer-term diagnostic workup unrelated to immediate surgery
The PCP should not be used merely as a required signature when the preoperative provider can appropriately evaluate and manage the perioperative issue.

When Cardiology Is Appropriate

Consider cardiology involvement for:

New or unstable ischemic symptoms
Decompensated heart failure
New significant arrhythmia
Unexplained syncope with cardiac concern
Moderate-to-severe symptomatic valve disease
Severe pulmonary hypertension with cardiac implications
Recent PCI/stent with uncertain antiplatelet management
Complex cardiomyopathy
New LBBB requiring evaluation under facility pathway
Other significant unstable cardiovascular disease

When Cardiology Is Usually Not Necessary Solely For

Stable hypertension
Remote stable CAD without new symptoms when adequate evaluation exists
Stable chronic RBBB
Stable nonspecific ST/T-wave abnormality
Controlled atrial fibrillation with an established plan
Remote normal cardiac testing without clinical change
Cardiovascular risk factors alone

When Pulmonology Is Appropriate

Consider pulmonary evaluation for:

Unexplained significant dyspnea
Recent severe COPD exacerbation
New oxygen requirement
FEV1 <50% predicted when relevant
BODE ≥5
CAT >10 under facility pathway
Chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis
Severe uncontrolled asthma
Other significant pulmonary instability

When Hematology Is Appropriate

  • Severe or unexplained anemia
  • Significant thrombocytopenia
  • Unexplained coagulopathy
  • Complex thrombophilia
  • Recurrent VTE
  • Complex anticoagulation/bridging problem
  • Major surgery in a patient refusing blood products when advanced planning is required

When Nephrology Is Appropriate

  • Progressive AKI
  • Severe CKD with instability
  • Persistent significant hyperkalemia
  • Complex volume-management problem
  • Dialysis coordination issue
  • Unexplained rapid renal deterioration
Stable CKD with acceptable electrolytes does not automatically require nephrology evaluation before elective surgery.

When Neurology Is Appropriate

  • Recent stroke/TIA requiring timing assessment
  • New focal neurologic symptoms
  • Uncontrolled seizure disorder
  • New unexplained neurologic deficit
  • Complex neurologic disease affecting anesthesia or positioning
Remote stable stroke with established residual deficits does not automatically require a new neurology visit before every elective procedure.

When Endocrinology Is Appropriate

  • Type 1 diabetes with complex perioperative insulin management
  • Recurrent DKA/HHS
  • Severe uncontrolled hyperglycemia
  • Thyrotoxicosis
  • Complex adrenal insufficiency
  • Uncertain perioperative steroid-replacement plan

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

Escalate to anesthesia when the question is primarily anesthetic, airway, procedural or facility-level rather than chronic disease management.

Examples:

Prior difficult airway
Severe OSA/respiratory concern
Severe pulmonary disease
Complex pacemaker/ICD plan
Neuraxial anticoagulant timing
Significant aspiration concern
Complex GLP-1 issue
Severe frailty
Prior major anesthesia complication
Question regarding appropriateness of surgical venue
Need for postoperative higher level of care

Provider vs Anesthesia Decision

Question Primary Owner
Does the patient have active medical instability? Preoperative provider
Does the patient need further medical testing? Preoperative provider
Is the airway concerning? Anesthesia
Can neuraxial anesthesia be safely performed after anticoagulant interruption? Anesthesia / applicable protocol
Is the facility appropriate for this anesthesia risk? Anesthesia + facility leadership pathway
Does the patient require postoperative cardiac surveillance? Provider using applicable protocol, coordinated with anesthesia as needed

Provider vs Surgeon Decision

Issue Primary Responsibility
Medical optimization Preoperative provider
Procedure-specific bleeding consequence Surgeon + anesthesia/protocol
DVT prophylaxis Surgeon
Postoperative analgesia Surgeon / pain pathway
When to resume antiplatelet therapy after surgery Surgeon with appropriate medical/cardiology input
Postoperative activity restrictions Surgeon
Medical monitoring Preoperative/hospitalist team + anesthesia as appropriate

Do Not Automatically Ask for “Clearance”

Avoid sending a patient to a specialist solely to obtain the words “cleared for surgery.”
Instead, identify the specific unresolved medical question and ask the appropriate specialist to address that question.

Example

Patient has known cardiomyopathy with EF 40% and is scheduled for elective lumbar fusion. No CHF admission in the past two years and no current decompensated heart-failure symptoms. Please provide current assessment of cardiovascular stability and any specific perioperative recommendations for planned noncardiac surgery.

Escalation Decision Framework

Abnormal finding identified
↓
Is patient acutely unstable?

YES → urgent evaluation / stop elective pathway

NO → Can preoperative provider evaluate and optimize within scope?

YES → manage, document and reassess

NO → Is issue primarily anesthetic/procedural?

YES → anesthesia/surgeon review

NO → identify the correct specialist and ask a specific clinical question

RN Escalation Triggers

Prompt provider notification should occur for findings such as:

Chest pain
New dyspnea
Syncope
New focal neurologic symptoms
Markedly abnormal vital signs
Severe glucose abnormality
Significant potassium abnormality
Unexpected severe anemia
Significant renal deterioration
New fever or infection symptoms
Recent hospitalization
Recent ICD shock
Anticoagulant/antiplatelet discrepancy
Medication hold not followed

RN Should Not Independently Determine

  • Whether an abnormal EKG is medically acceptable for surgery
  • Whether significant anemia is optimized
  • Whether cardiac testing is clinically indicated
  • Whether anticoagulation bridging is required unless following an explicit authorized protocol
  • Whether active disease is stable enough for elective surgery
  • Whether specialist consultation is medically unnecessary when provider review is required
  • Final preoperative optimization status

Provider Optimization Examples

Mild Anemia

Mild anemia with Hgb ___, compared with prior ___ on ___. No evidence of active bleeding. Iron studies ___ when indicated. Expected surgical blood loss reviewed. Current perioperative plan: ___.

Stable CKD

CKD with creatinine ___ / eGFR ___. Renal function is stable compared with prior baseline. Avoid nephrotoxins/NSAIDs as clinically appropriate and maintain perioperative volume management. No nephrology consultation required solely for stable chronic renal disease at this time.

OSA

Known OSA. STOP-BANG ___. Patient is compliant with PAP / intolerant of PAP. Recommend PAP anytime asleep when used at baseline, respiratory therapy assistance as needed and postoperative continuous pulse oximetry when indicated.

Do Not Duplicate Specialist Management

The preoperative provider should optimize perioperative risk but should not unnecessarily replace an established specialist's management of complex active disease.

Examples include changing advanced pulmonary-hypertension therapy, altering antiarrhythmic therapy without indication, independently programming a CIED or changing a complex immunosuppressive regimen without the appropriate treatment plan.

Communication Back to the Surgeon

The surgeon should receive a concise statement describing any issue that could change surgical timing, bleeding management, postoperative monitoring or disposition.

Final Optimization Status

Status Use When
Optimized Required evaluation is complete and no unresolved medical issue currently prevents proceeding.
Pending A required result, recommendation or management decision remains unresolved.
Not optimized A current medical issue requires treatment, postponement, different venue or additional intervention before elective surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • RN workflow should focus on accurate collection, screening, record retrieval and approved protocols.
  • RNs can summarize records but should not replace provider interpretation.
  • Standing orders must be formally approved by the institution.
  • The preoperative provider owns medical interpretation and risk stratification.
  • Many stable chronic conditions can be managed directly by the preoperative provider.
  • Do not automatically refer every abnormal laboratory result to the PCP.
  • Use specialists for specific unresolved questions or unstable disease.
  • Use anesthesia for anesthesia-specific, airway, procedural and venue questions.
  • Avoid generic “medical clearance” and “cardiac clearance” requests.
  • Ask consultants a specific perioperative question.
  • Every pending item must have a responsible owner and final reviewer.
  • The provider determines the final preoperative optimization status.
