CKD, AKI, Dialysis & Preoperative Renal Optimization

Renal staging, electrolyte risk, volume status, dialysis timing and perioperative medication implications

Purpose

Renal disease affects fluid balance, electrolyte stability, medication clearance, anemia, cardiovascular risk and postoperative recovery. Preoperative evaluation should distinguish stable chronic kidney disease from acute kidney injury or unstable renal dysfunction.

The most important questions are whether renal function is stable, whether potassium and volume status are acceptable, whether medications require adjustment and whether the planned surgical setting can safely manage the patient's renal needs.

Compare With Baseline

A creatinine value is much more useful when compared with previous results.

A creatinine that is “within normal range” may still represent AKI if it has risen significantly from the patient's usual baseline.
Stable chronic elevation may be acceptable when the patient is clinically well, electrolytes are acceptable and no evidence of acute deterioration is present.

Acute Kidney Injury

AKI represents an acute decline in kidney function and should be distinguished from stable CKD.

Potential Causes

  • Dehydration
  • Recent illness
  • Sepsis
  • NSAIDs
  • ACE inhibitor / ARB effects in susceptible patients
  • Diuretics / overdiuresis
  • Urinary obstruction
  • Contrast exposure
  • Intrinsic renal disease
  • Heart failure / poor renal perfusion
Progressive or unexplained AKI requires evaluation before routine elective surgery.

Potassium

Renal dysfunction is a common cause of hyperkalemia.

Significant hyperkalemia requires confirmation and treatment before elective surgery, particularly when progressive, associated with EKG changes or occurring in advanced kidney disease.

Review Potential Contributors

  • ACE inhibitor
  • ARB
  • Spironolactone/eplerenone
  • Potassium supplements
  • NSAIDs
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • AKI
  • Dialysis adequacy

Pseudohyperkalemia

Unexpected potassium elevation in a clinically well patient should be reviewed for hemolysis or specimen error before assuming true hyperkalemia.

Repeat testing may be appropriate when the result is inconsistent with the clinical picture.

Hypokalemia

Patients with CKD can also develop hypokalemia, particularly with diuretics, GI losses or poor intake.

Clinically important hypokalemia should be corrected before elective surgery, especially when arrhythmia risk or QT prolongation is present.

Bicarbonate / CO2

Low bicarbonate may indicate metabolic acidosis associated with advanced CKD, AKI, diarrhea, DKA or another process.

Significant unexplained metabolic acidosis requires further evaluation before elective surgery.

RCRI and Renal Disease

RCRI renal criterion:

Creatinine >2.0 mg/dL = 1 RCRI point
This criterion is for cardiac-risk scoring. It should not be used as the only definition of clinically important renal disease.

CKD and BNP Interpretation

Natriuretic peptide concentrations can be elevated in patients with renal dysfunction.

Apply institutional BNP/NT-proBNP postoperative surveillance thresholds as required, while interpreting the value in the context of renal function and the overall cardiovascular picture.

CKD and Anemia

CKD commonly contributes to normocytic anemia through reduced erythropoietin production and altered iron metabolism.

Do not automatically attribute anemia to CKD. Iron deficiency, GI blood loss, B12 deficiency and other causes may coexist.

See the dedicated anemia chapter for additional workup.

Renal Function and Anticoagulants

Renal function affects clearance of several anticoagulant medications.

Medication Renal Consideration
Dabigatran Renal clearance is especially important; interruption interval may need to be longer with impaired function.
Apixaban Review renal function, indication and procedure-specific guidance.
Rivaroxaban Renal function contributes to medication clearance and dosing.
Enoxaparin Accumulation may occur with significant renal impairment.
Do not apply a standard anticoagulant hold interval without considering renal function when the medication has meaningful renal clearance.

NSAIDs

NSAIDs may worsen renal perfusion, fluid retention and blood pressure in susceptible patients.

Avoid unnecessary NSAID exposure in patients with AKI, advanced CKD, significant volume depletion or other high-risk renal conditions.

ACE Inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitor/ARB therapy can affect potassium, blood pressure and renal hemodynamics.

Review the indication, renal trend, potassium and anesthesia medication instructions. Management may differ when therapy is used for hypertension versus heart-failure GDMT.

Diuretics

Diuretics may contribute to hypovolemia, electrolyte abnormalities or AKI, but they may also be necessary to maintain euvolemia in heart failure.

Assess the indication and volume status rather than applying the same perioperative instruction to every diuretic patient.

Metformin and Renal Function

Renal function should be reviewed in patients taking metformin, particularly when AKI, severe CKD or acute illness is present.

Follow facility medication guidance for perioperative metformin management and address acute renal deterioration rather than focusing only on the medication.

SGLT2 Inhibitors and CKD

Many patients with CKD or heart failure take SGLT2 inhibitors.

Regardless of whether the drug is being used for diabetes, CKD or heart failure, perioperative SGLT2 interruption is important because of euglycemic DKA risk.

Contrast Exposure

Recent or planned iodinated contrast may be relevant in patients with impaired renal function.

Review baseline renal function, recent AKI, volume status and whether there has been a clinically important creatinine change after contrast exposure.
There is no universal mandatory surgical waiting period after uncomplicated contrast exposure. Delay is driven by clinically meaningful renal deterioration or another complication, not the fact that contrast was administered.

Urinary Obstruction

Obstruction may cause AKI and can be associated with infection.

Suspected acute obstruction with rising creatinine, significant hydronephrosis, infection or severe symptoms requires evaluation before routine elective nonurologic surgery.

Timing of Hemodialysis

The ideal dialysis schedule depends on volume status, potassium and the timing of surgery.

Elective surgery is often planned after recent adequate dialysis so the patient is near euvolemia and significant electrolyte abnormalities have been corrected.
Do not rely on laboratory values from several weeks earlier to establish current potassium or volume status in a hemodialysis patient.

Same-Day Dialysis Considerations

If hemodialysis occurs on the same day as surgery, timing and anticoagulation used during dialysis may affect the plan.

Same-day dialysis requires coordination with nephrology, anesthesia and the surgical team. Review intradialytic heparin exposure, hemodynamics, potassium and post-dialysis volume status.

Dialysis Access

Document

  • AV fistula
  • AV graft
  • Tunneled catheter
  • Other access
Avoid unnecessary blood-pressure cuffs, IV placement and venipuncture in a functional fistula/graft extremity according to facility policy.

Peritoneal Dialysis

Patients receiving peritoneal dialysis require individualized planning based on procedure type, abdominal involvement, fluid status and infection risk.

Coordinate with nephrology when the surgery may affect the peritoneal cavity or when dialysis management during hospitalization requires modification.

Dialysis and Anticoagulation

Dialysis anticoagulation may contribute to perioperative bleeding considerations depending on timing and agent used.

When surgery occurs near the dialysis session, document whether heparin or another anticoagulant was used during dialysis and whether this affects the planned procedure.

Dialysis and Anemia

Anemia is common in advanced CKD and dialysis.

Review

  • Hemoglobin trend
  • Iron status when available
  • Erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy
  • Recent transfusion
  • Expected surgical blood loss

Dialysis and Cardiac Risk

Patients requiring dialysis often have substantial cardiovascular comorbidity.

Apply the usual cardiovascular pathway rather than assuming all risk is explained by kidney disease. Review CAD, heart failure, valvular disease, arrhythmia and functional capacity.

Dialysis and Infection Risk

Dialysis access infections, catheter infections and systemic infection can affect surgical timing.

Suspected dialysis-access infection, bacteremia or sepsis should be treated and resolved before routine elective surgery.

Postoperative AKI Prevention

Consider

  • Avoiding unnecessary nephrotoxic exposure
  • Maintaining appropriate perfusion and volume status
  • Monitoring renal function in high-risk patients
  • Adjusting renally cleared medications
  • Prompt treatment of hypotension
  • Monitoring urine output when clinically indicated

When a Higher Level of Care May Be Needed

Renal disease alone does not automatically determine venue, but advanced or unstable disease can increase monitoring requirements.

Examples

  • Dialysis requiring inpatient coordination
  • Unstable volume status
  • Persistent electrolyte abnormality
  • Progressive AKI
  • Severe associated heart failure
  • Need for close postoperative renal monitoring

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Progressive or unexplained AKI
Severe hyperkalemia
Significant symptomatic electrolyte abnormality
Severe metabolic acidosis
Major dehydration
Clinically significant volume overload
Dialysis patient without an adequate perioperative dialysis plan
Active dialysis-access infection or bacteremia
Other acute renal instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When clinically stable, these do not automatically require postponement:

Stable CKD stage 3
Stable CKD stage 4 with appropriate planning
Chronic stable creatinine elevation
Stable renal anemia
Remote uncomplicated contrast exposure
Dialysis dependence with acceptable current potassium/volume status and a coordinated plan

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is renal dysfunction chronic or acute? Compare with previous creatinine/eGFR.
Is potassium acceptable? Confirm unexpected abnormalities and treat significant derangements.
Is the patient euvolemic? Correct severe dehydration or overload before elective surgery.
Are medications affected by renal function? Review anticoagulants, NSAIDs, diabetes medications and other renally cleared drugs.
Is the patient on dialysis? Establish dialysis timing, access, potassium and volume plan.
Is nephrology needed? Escalate for unstable or complex renal disease.
Does the planned setting support renal needs? Consider higher level of care when monitoring or dialysis requirements exceed capability.

Key Takeaways

  • Compare creatinine with the patient's previous baseline.
  • Stable CKD is different from acute kidney injury.
  • Assess potassium and volume status, not creatinine alone.
  • Confirm unexpected hyperkalemia when specimen hemolysis is possible.
  • Renal function affects anticoagulant clearance and medication planning.
  • NSAIDs may worsen renal function in susceptible patients.
  • Dialysis patients need current electrolyte and volume assessment near surgery.
  • Coordinate dialysis timing rather than relying on old laboratory values.
  • Protect functional dialysis access.
  • Same-day dialysis requires specific coordination, including review of dialysis anticoagulation.
  • Progressive AKI, severe hyperkalemia, acidosis or major volume disturbance may require postponement.
  • Stable chronic renal disease does not automatically require specialist “clearance.”
