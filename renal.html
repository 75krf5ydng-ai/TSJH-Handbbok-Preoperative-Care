Preoperative Renal, Electrolyte & Metabolic Evaluation

Kidney function, electrolyte abnormalities and metabolic optimization before elective surgery

Home Evaluation Cardiac Pulmonary

Purpose

Renal and metabolic abnormalities can increase the risk of perioperative hypotension, arrhythmia, acute kidney injury, medication toxicity, fluid imbalance, delayed recovery and postoperative complications.

The preoperative goal is to identify significant abnormalities, determine whether they are acute or chronic, correct reversible causes when appropriate, adjust medications, and establish a perioperative monitoring plan.

Initial Renal Assessment

Review kidney function in the context of the patient's baseline rather than relying on a single isolated laboratory result.

Review Clinical Importance
Creatinine Compare with prior values to determine whether renal dysfunction is chronic, stable, or potentially acute.
eGFR Helps stage chronic kidney disease and guides medication and fluid considerations.
BUN Interpret together with creatinine, volume status, medications, and clinical history.
Electrolytes Review sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate for clinically important abnormalities.
Prior renal disease CKD, dialysis, nephrectomy, renal transplant, recurrent AKI, stones or other significant renal history.
Nephrotoxic medications NSAIDs, selected antibiotics, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and other medications may require individualized perioperative management.

Chronic Kidney Disease

Chronic kidney disease is associated with increased perioperative risk, particularly as renal function declines or when accompanied by anemia, electrolyte abnormalities, cardiovascular disease or volume instability.

CKD Stage eGFR
G1 ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m² with evidence of kidney disease
G2 60–89
G3a 45–59
G3b 30–44
G4 15–29
G5 <15 or kidney failure
Mild, stable CKD does not automatically require postponement of elective surgery. The key issues are stability, volume status, electrolyte control, medication dosing and the expected physiologic stress of surgery.

Acute Kidney Injury

A new increase in creatinine should be evaluated before elective surgery, particularly when the cause is unclear or renal function continues to worsen.

Consider postponing elective surgery when there is significant unresolved acute kidney injury, progressive creatinine elevation, severe electrolyte disturbance, oliguria, volume depletion, volume overload or another untreated cause of acute renal dysfunction.

Common Reversible Causes

  • Dehydration or poor oral intake
  • Diuretic-related volume depletion
  • NSAID exposure
  • Recent contrast exposure
  • Hypotension
  • Urinary obstruction
  • Infection or sepsis
  • Medication toxicity

Creatinine and the RCRI

Preoperative serum creatinine greater than 2.0 mg/dL is one of the six traditional Revised Cardiac Risk Index variables.

RCRI renal criterion:

Preoperative creatinine >2.0 mg/dL = 1 RCRI point
When the renal criterion increases the patient's RCRI score, reassess whether institutional BNP or NT-proBNP screening criteria are also met.

Potassium

Potassium abnormalities are particularly important because severe hyperkalemia or hypokalemia can increase arrhythmia and neuromuscular risk.

Hyperkalemia

Confirm unexpected potassium elevation when hemolysis or specimen error is possible. Review renal function and medications that may contribute.

Significant or unexplained hyperkalemia should be corrected and evaluated before elective surgery, especially when accompanied by EKG abnormalities, progressive renal dysfunction or symptoms.

Hypokalemia

Determine the cause and severity. Common contributors include diuretic use, gastrointestinal loss and inadequate intake.

Mild chronic potassium abnormalities may be managed differently from acute or severe abnormalities. Consider the patient's cardiac history, EKG, medications, planned procedure and anesthesia requirements.

Sodium

Sodium abnormalities should be interpreted according to severity, duration, symptoms and underlying cause.

Hyponatremia

Evaluate volume status, medication effects, heart failure, renal disease, endocrine causes and other potential contributors.

Severe, symptomatic, rapidly developing or unexplained hyponatremia warrants correction and further evaluation before elective surgery.

Hypernatremia

Hypernatremia commonly reflects free-water deficit and may indicate significant dehydration or impaired water regulation.

Significant hypernatremia should prompt assessment of hydration status, renal function and underlying cause before proceeding with elective surgery.

Bicarbonate and Acid-Base Status

Abnormal serum bicarbonate may indicate metabolic acidosis, metabolic alkalosis or compensation for chronic respiratory disease.

Finding Consider
Low bicarbonate Renal dysfunction, diarrhea, ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, medication effect or chronic respiratory alkalosis.
High bicarbonate Diuretic use, vomiting, volume contraction or compensation for chronic hypercapnia.
A markedly abnormal bicarbonate level should not be dismissed as an isolated laboratory finding. Determine whether clinically important acid-base disease is present.

Calcium and Magnesium

Calcium and magnesium abnormalities may affect cardiac conduction, neuromuscular function and perioperative medication response.

Significant abnormalities should be corrected when feasible and their underlying cause evaluated, particularly when the patient has symptoms, arrhythmia risk or other major metabolic abnormalities.

Volume Status

Both hypovolemia and volume overload increase perioperative risk.

Possible Hypovolemia Possible Volume Overload
Orthostasis, poor intake, dry mucous membranes, elevated BUN relative to baseline, tachycardia, low blood pressure. Peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion, increasing oxygen need, rapid weight gain, orthopnea or decompensated heart failure.
Clinically significant dehydration or volume overload should be corrected before elective surgery when possible.

Dialysis Patients

Patients receiving hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis require individualized perioperative planning.

Important Preoperative Questions

  • What is the patient's dialysis schedule?
  • When was the most recent dialysis session?
  • What is the patient's usual dry weight?
  • Is volume status clinically acceptable?
  • Is potassium acceptable?
  • Is the dialysis access functioning?
  • Are nephrology recommendations available when needed?
Dialysis is commonly coordinated close to the operative date so the patient enters surgery with acceptable volume and electrolyte status. Timing should be individualized with the nephrology and anesthesia teams.

Medication Considerations

Medication/Class Preoperative Consideration
NSAIDs May worsen renal perfusion in susceptible patients and may also affect bleeding risk depending on the agent and procedure.
Diuretics Review for dehydration, hypokalemia, hyponatremia and volume status. Day-of-surgery management should follow the surgical and anesthesia medication protocol.
ACE inhibitors / ARBs Review indication, blood pressure and institutional day-of-surgery medication guidance.
Renally cleared medications Dose adjustment may be required with reduced renal function.
Nephrotoxic medications Minimize avoidable nephrotoxins in patients with CKD or increased AKI risk.

Diabetes and Renal Risk

Diabetes is a major contributor to chronic kidney disease and may increase the risk of postoperative kidney injury.

Review glucose control, A1c when clinically appropriate, renal function, hydration status and diabetes medications as part of the overall preoperative assessment.

Diabetes medication management, including insulin, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, should follow current institutional and anesthesia perioperative medication guidance.

Urinalysis and Renal Findings

Proteinuria, hematuria, glucosuria and other abnormalities may reflect renal or systemic disease but should be interpreted in the clinical context.

An abnormal urinalysis does not automatically mean that infection is present or that surgery must be delayed. Urinary infection and asymptomatic bacteriuria are addressed separately in the urinary screening chapter of this handbook.

When Nephrology Consultation May Be Appropriate

Consider nephrology consultation when renal disease is severe, unstable, unexplained or requires specialist management.

Examples include:
  • Progressive or unexplained acute kidney injury
  • Advanced CKD with significant perioperative concerns
  • Dialysis-management questions
  • Refractory hyperkalemia
  • Severe acid-base abnormalities
  • Significant unexplained proteinuria or hematuria
  • Complex fluid-management concerns

Laboratory Interpretation: Practical Approach

Finding Preoperative Response
Mild stable creatinine elevation Compare with baseline, identify CKD stage, review medications and plan renal-protective care.
New creatinine increase Evaluate for AKI and reversible causes before elective surgery.
Elevated BUN with stable creatinine Assess hydration, intake, medications and clinical volume status.
Mild isolated electrolyte abnormality Reassess severity, chronicity, cause and perioperative significance.
Severe electrolyte abnormality Correct and evaluate before elective surgery.
Unexpected abnormal result Repeat testing when specimen error or laboratory artifact is plausible.

Postoperative Acute Kidney Injury Prevention

Patients at increased renal risk benefit from attention to hemodynamics, volume status and medication exposure throughout the perioperative period.

  • Avoid prolonged hypotension.
  • Maintain appropriate intravascular volume.
  • Avoid unnecessary nephrotoxins.
  • Renally dose medications when indicated.
  • Monitor urine output when clinically appropriate.
  • Repeat renal function testing in higher-risk patients.
  • Correct important electrolyte abnormalities.

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement when clinically significant unresolved abnormalities are present, including:
  • Progressive acute kidney injury
  • Severe hyperkalemia or hypokalemia
  • Severe symptomatic sodium abnormality
  • Clinically significant metabolic acidosis
  • Severe dehydration
  • Uncontrolled volume overload
  • Dialysis patient with unacceptable electrolyte or volume status
  • Unexplained renal deterioration requiring further evaluation

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is renal function stable compared with baseline? If yes, document CKD status and proceed with appropriate precautions.
Is there evidence of acute kidney injury? Identify and treat reversible causes before elective surgery.
Are electrolytes clinically acceptable? Correct significant abnormalities and repeat testing when appropriate.
Is the patient euvolemic? Correct significant dehydration or overload before surgery.
Does renal dysfunction affect medication management? Adjust renally cleared or nephrotoxic medications when appropriate.
Does the patient require dialysis coordination? Coordinate with nephrology, anesthesia and the surgical team.
Is specialist evaluation necessary? Refer when renal instability or complexity exceeds routine preoperative management.

Key Takeaways

  • Compare renal laboratory results with the patient's baseline.
  • Distinguish stable CKD from acute kidney injury.
  • Creatinine greater than 2.0 mg/dL contributes one RCRI point.
  • Correct clinically significant potassium and sodium abnormalities.
  • Evaluate major bicarbonate abnormalities rather than ignoring them.
  • Assess both dehydration and volume overload.
  • Coordinate dialysis patients carefully around surgery.
  • Review nephrotoxic and renally cleared medications.
  • Use nephrology consultation when there is a specific unresolved renal concern.
