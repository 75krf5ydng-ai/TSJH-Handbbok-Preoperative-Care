Purpose

Patients with rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, spondyloarthropathies and other autoimmune diseases may receive medications that affect immune function, wound healing, infection risk and adrenal reserve.

The goal is to balance postoperative infection risk against the risk of a severe disease flare from unnecessary medication interruption.

General Perioperative Principle

Do not create one universal hold rule for all rheumatologic medications. Management depends on the specific medication, dosing interval, disease severity, surgery type and infection risk.
For biologics, JAK inhibitors and complex immunosuppressive therapy, use the current rheumatology/surgeon institutional pathway or obtain the treating rheumatologist's recommendation.

Conventional DMARDs

Several conventional DMARDs are often continued through surgery in stable patients, depending on the current guideline and clinical circumstances.

Medication General Consideration
Methotrexate Often continued in stable patients, but review renal function, cytopenias and disease-specific guidance.
Hydroxychloroquine Often continued perioperatively.
Sulfasalazine Often continued depending on current protocol.
Leflunomide Often continued under selected guidelines, but individualized review may be appropriate.
Avoid stopping chronic conventional DMARD therapy simply because the medication is labeled an “immune medication.” Determine the actual guideline for the drug.

Methotrexate

Review

  • Indication
  • Weekly dose
  • Renal function
  • WBC count
  • Platelet count
  • Liver function
  • Recent infection
Acute kidney injury may increase methotrexate toxicity risk. Significant cytopenia, active infection or other toxicity should be addressed before elective surgery.

Hydroxychloroquine

Hydroxychloroquine is commonly continued through the perioperative period.

Review other QT-prolonging medications when the patient has significant QT prolongation or arrhythmia concerns, but routine discontinuation is generally not required solely because surgery is planned.

Biologic Agents

Biologic medications can increase infection susceptibility and often require procedure-specific interruption around major surgery.

Do not use an arbitrary number of days for all biologics. Many regimens are managed according to the medication's dosing cycle rather than a single fixed hold interval.

Common Biologic Classes

Class Examples

IL-17 / IL-12/23 / IL-23 agents Selected agents used for psoriatic disease and spondyloarthropathy.

Biologic Timing

Perioperative plans frequently time surgery near the end of a medication dosing cycle and omit the next scheduled dose when appropriate.

Document the exact biologic, dosing interval and last dose before deciding whether the patient has met the required preoperative medication interval.

Restarting Biologic Therapy

Postoperative biologic resumption should take into account wound healing and evidence of infection.

Do not routinely restart immunosuppressive biologic therapy in the presence of active infection, significant wound drainage or poor wound healing without appropriate specialist/surgeon review.
The surgeon and treating rheumatologist should guide resumption for major implant surgery when infection consequences are high.

JAK Inhibitors

JAK inhibitors are targeted synthetic DMARDs with important infection and thrombotic considerations.

Examples

  • Tofacitinib
  • Upadacitinib
  • Baricitinib
These medications commonly require perioperative interruption under current guidelines. Verify the specific hold recommendation rather than treating them as conventional DMARDs.

JAK Inhibitors and VTE Risk

Selected JAK inhibitors carry warnings related to venous thromboembolism and other cardiovascular events in certain populations.

Review previous DVT/PE, cardiovascular risk, mobility and procedure-specific VTE prophylaxis rather than focusing only on infection risk.

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

SLE perioperative risk depends heavily on organ involvement and disease activity.

Review

  • Renal involvement / lupus nephritis
  • Cardiac involvement
  • Pulmonary involvement
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Cytopenias
  • Current immunosuppression
  • Recent flare
Active severe organ-threatening SLE may require rheumatology and specialty optimization before elective surgery.

Antiphospholipid Syndrome

APS may confer significant thrombotic risk and often involves chronic anticoagulation.

Do not treat APS as a routine rheumatology medication issue. Apply the VTE/ anticoagulation pathway and obtain hematology or anticoagulation guidance for complex perioperative interruption decisions.

Rheumatoid Arthritis and Cervical Spine

Longstanding rheumatoid arthritis may involve the cervical spine.

Patients with symptoms or known significant cervical instability require anesthesia/specialist review because airway positioning may create neurologic risk.

Ask About

  • Neck pain
  • Neurologic symptoms
  • Known atlantoaxial instability
  • Prior cervical imaging
  • Previous difficult airway

Rheumatoid Arthritis and Airway

RA may affect the cervical spine, temporomandibular joints and cricoarytenoid joints.

Document limited neck motion, difficult mouth opening, hoarseness or known airway involvement and communicate significant findings to anesthesia.

Ankylosing Spondylitis

Advanced axial spondyloarthritis may cause marked spinal rigidity and difficult airway positioning.

Severe cervical immobility or fixed deformity requires anesthesia planning before surgery.

Systemic Sclerosis / Scleroderma

Systemic sclerosis may involve pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, esophageal dysmotility, renal disease and difficult airway anatomy.

Review aspiration risk, pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease and renal status. These features may matter more than the rheumatologic diagnosis itself.

Inflammatory Myopathy

Polymyositis, dermatomyositis and related disorders may cause muscle weakness, dysphagia and respiratory impairment.

Significant bulbar or respiratory muscle weakness requires anesthesia and specialist review before elective surgery.

Vasculitis

Systemic vasculitis may involve kidneys, lungs, nerves and other organs.

Assess current disease activity and organ involvement rather than relying only on the diagnostic label.
Active severe vasculitis with major organ involvement may require optimization before elective surgery.

Chronic Glucocorticoids

Many rheumatology patients take chronic prednisone or related systemic steroids.

Review dose and duration for HPA-axis suppression, hyperglycemia, infection risk and bone-health considerations.

See the dedicated thyroid/adrenal/steroid chapter for stress-dose planning.

Do Not Abruptly Stop Chronic Steroids

Abrupt discontinuation of chronic systemic glucocorticoids may precipitate adrenal insufficiency and a disease flare.

Infection Screening

Immunosuppressed patients may present with less obvious infection symptoms.

Review

  • Fever
  • Respiratory symptoms
  • Urinary symptoms
  • Skin infection
  • Open wounds
  • Recent antibiotics
  • Recent hospitalization
Active clinically significant infection should generally be treated before elective implant surgery.

Implant Surgery

Infection consequences are particularly important when prosthetic joints, spinal hardware, SCS systems or other implanted devices are planned.

For immunosuppressed patients, medication timing and wound status deserve deliberate review before procedures involving implanted hardware.

Wound Healing

Risk may be influenced by glucocorticoids, malnutrition, diabetes, smoking, active inflammation and immunosuppressive therapy.

Review the total wound-healing risk rather than attributing risk to one medication alone.

Cytopenias

Autoimmune disease and immunosuppressive medications may cause anemia, leukopenia or thrombocytopenia.

Significant neutropenia or thrombocytopenia requires evaluation before major elective surgery, especially procedures with high infection or bleeding consequences.

Liver and Renal Monitoring

Several DMARDs and immunosuppressants can affect liver or kidney function.

Review When Appropriate

  • Creatinine/eGFR
  • AST/ALT
  • CBC
  • Medication-specific monitoring requirements

When Rheumatology Input Is Needed

Obtain rheumatology guidance when there is:

Unclear biologic hold interval
Unclear JAK inhibitor plan
Complex immunosuppressive combination therapy
Active autoimmune flare
Major organ-threatening disease
Uncertainty about postoperative restart timing
Concern that medication interruption could cause serious flare
Other disease-specific perioperative uncertainty

When Anesthesia Review Is Needed

Examples

  • RA with significant cervical spine disease
  • Severe ankylosing spondylitis
  • Scleroderma with aspiration risk
  • Interstitial lung disease
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Inflammatory myopathy with respiratory weakness
  • Complex chronic steroid exposure

When Hematology May Be Needed

Consider hematology for:

Severe thrombocytopenia
Severe neutropenia
Complex antiphospholipid syndrome
Recurrent thrombosis
Unexplained significant cytopenias
Complex perioperative anticoagulation

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Active serious infection
Severe uncontrolled autoimmune flare
Major active organ-threatening disease
Severe cytopenia affecting procedural safety
Unresolved biologic/JAK management for high-risk implant surgery
Severe pulmonary or cardiac complication of autoimmune disease
Other acute rheumatologic instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When clinically stable, the following do not automatically require postponement:

Stable rheumatoid arthritis
Stable lupus without active major organ disease
Methotrexate therapy with acceptable labs and an established plan
Hydroxychloroquine use
Stable chronic steroid use with appropriate adrenal planning
Biologic therapy when the perioperative dosing plan is established

Decision Framework

Question Action
What autoimmune disease is present? Identify organ involvement and current disease activity.
Which medications are used? Separate conventional DMARDs, biologics, JAK inhibitors and steroids.
Is the disease stable? Active major flare may require optimization.
Is a biologic used? Review exact dosing interval and current perioperative guidance.
Is a JAK inhibitor used? Confirm required interruption and VTE considerations.
Is chronic steroid therapy present? Assess adrenal suppression and stress-dose needs.
Is active infection present? Treat before elective implant surgery when clinically significant.
Could the disease affect airway, lungs or heart? Escalate to anesthesia or relevant specialist when needed.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not use one hold rule for every rheumatology medication.
  • Distinguish conventional DMARDs from biologics and JAK inhibitors.
  • Several conventional DMARDs may be continued under current perioperative guidance.
  • Biologic timing often follows the medication dosing cycle.
  • JAK inhibitors commonly require temporary perioperative interruption.
  • Postoperative biologic restart should consider wound healing and infection.
  • Do not abruptly discontinue chronic systemic steroids.
  • RA may create cervical spine and airway concerns.
  • Scleroderma may involve pulmonary hypertension, fibrosis and aspiration risk.
  • Inflammatory myopathy may affect swallowing and respiratory strength.
  • Active serious infection or major autoimmune flare may require postponement.
  • Use rheumatology input for complex medication timing rather than guessing.
