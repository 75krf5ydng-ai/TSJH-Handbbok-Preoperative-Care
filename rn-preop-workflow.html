RN Preoperative Workflow, Record Review & Escalation Guide

A standardized nursing workflow for efficient preoperative preparation, record collection, screening and provider escalation

Home Evaluation Medications Infection Red Flags Resources

Purpose

The RN preoperative workflow should identify missing information early, standardize record collection, recognize findings requiring provider review and reduce preventable day-of-surgery delays or cancellations.

Important scope principle: Nursing staff may collect, verify, organize and summarize clinical information and may initiate testing or other actions specifically authorized by approved standing orders, facility policy and applicable nursing scope. Medical diagnosis, independent perioperative risk determination and final medical optimization remain with the appropriately credentialed provider.

Step 3 — Medication Reconciliation

Medication reconciliation should include medication name, dose, frequency, indication when known and the patient's actual use—not merely a copied historical medication list.

Always identify

  • Antiplatelet medications
  • Aspirin
  • Insulin

  • GLP-1/GIP medications
  • Chronic steroids
  • Biologics/JAK inhibitors
  • Chronic opioids

Step 6 — Pulmonary Record Collection

When applicable, obtain:

  • Pulmonology notes
  • PFT results
  • Sleep study
  • PAP settings/compliance information
  • Home oxygen requirements
  • Recent chest imaging
  • Recent COPD/asthma exacerbation records
Escalate 24-hour oxygen use, chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, significant abnormal PFTs or recent pulmonary hospitalization.

BNP Surveillance Trigger

When applicable under the established TSJH protocol, NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L or BNP ≥92 pg/mL triggers the postoperative cardiac-surveillance pathway with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.
The provider determines protocol applicability and documents the final perioperative plan.

Standing Orders

Standing orders must be formally approved by the facility and executed within the RN's authorized scope. This handbook does not independently authorize an RN to order a test, medication or treatment.

When an approved standing-order protocol exists, the RN may initiate the specified action when the patient meets the protocol criteria.

Examples of Potential Standing-Order Categories

What the RN Can Summarize

Examples of useful record summarization:

“Cardiology follow-up 06/10/26 with Dr. ___: stable CAD without angina.”

“Echo 05/02/26: EF 55–60%, mild MR, no severe valvular abnormality.”

“Lexiscan 04/18/26: no inducible ischemia; EF 63%.”

“Device interrogation 07/01/26: normal function, battery longevity ___.”

“Pulmonology 05/15/26: COPD stable on current inhalers; no recent exacerbation.”

What the RN Should Not Independently Conclude

Avoid independent statements such as:

“Patient is cleared for surgery.”
“Cardiac risk is acceptable.”
“Patient does not need cardiology.”
“Abnormal EKG is insignificant.”
“Patient needs a stress test.”
“Patient needs anticoagulation bridging.”
“Positive urine culture requires antibiotics.”
“Patient is optimized.”

Instead, organize the objective findings and route them to the responsible provider for clinical interpretation.

Severe lab abnormality

Emergency Symptoms

If the patient reports symptoms suggesting an acute medical emergency, follow the facility emergency/triage protocol rather than continuing routine preoperative processing.

Communication Standard

When escalating, provide the clinical question—not simply “please review.”

Example:

“Patient scheduled for L4-5 fusion on 08/28/26. History of HFrEF. Echo received today shows EF 32%; prior EF 45%. Patient reports increased exertional dyspnea. Please review for optimization and surgical venue appropriateness.”

Cancellation-Prevention Principles

  • Identify high-risk medications early.
  • Request cardiology and device records early.
  • Do not wait until the day before surgery to discover a recent hospitalization.
  • Track every unresolved item with an owner.
  • Escalate abnormal findings with enough clinical context to support a decision.
  • Confirm anticoagulant and diabetes medication instructions were understood.
  • Verify required testing was actually completed—not merely ordered.
  • Reassess for new symptoms shortly before surgery.
  • Resolve procedure/laterality/date discrepancies immediately.

Key Takeaways

  • The RN workflow should collect, verify, organize and escalate—not independently determine final medical optimization.
  • Medication reconciliation should reflect what the patient actually takes.
  • Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, GLP-1 agents, SGLT2 inhibitors and insulin deserve early attention.
  • Cardiac records are most useful when summarized with dates and interpretations.
  • Screening scores support—but do not replace—clinical judgment.
  • Use standing orders only when formally authorized.
  • An abnormal UA is not automatically a UTI.
  • Recent hospitalizations should always be characterized.
  • CIED information should be obtained before the day of surgery when required.
  • Every unresolved item needs an owner.
  • Specific SBAR communication is more useful than “please review.”
  • Early identification and follow-through are central to reducing avoidable day-of-surgery cancellations.

Provider Final Determination

After nursing preparation and record collection are complete, the credentialed provider performs the medical evaluation, interprets the findings, determines whether additional evaluation is necessary and assigns the final preoperative optimization status.
