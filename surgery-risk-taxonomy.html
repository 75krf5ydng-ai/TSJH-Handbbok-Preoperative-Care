Surgery Types, Expected OR Times & Perioperative Surgical Risk

Common spine, orthopedic, pain, hand, foot/ankle and urologic procedures

Home Master H&P Quick Reference Final Readiness Resources

Purpose

Procedure type matters because physiologic stress, expected blood loss, operative duration, positioning, implant use and postoperative monitoring vary substantially between procedures.

Use surgical risk category together with patient-specific cardiovascular, pulmonary, frailty, renal and hematologic risk rather than using procedure type alone.
Expected OR times below are practical planning estimates only. Actual duration varies by surgeon, complexity, revision status, anatomy, number of levels and intraoperative findings.

Surgical Risk Categories

Category Approximate Cardiac Event Risk General Interpretation
Low <1% Minimal physiologic stress and usually limited blood loss.
Intermediate Approximately 1–5% Most orthopedic, spine and general noncardiac procedures.
High >5% Major vascular or other procedures with substantial physiologic stress.

Important Principle

A procedure can be “intermediate risk” from a cardiovascular standpoint but still carry high bleeding consequence, prolonged operative time or significant pulmonary/positioning risk.

For example, a multilevel spine fusion may still be classified as intermediate cardiac risk while requiring much greater anemia, pulmonary, anticoagulant and postoperative planning than a short arthroscopic procedure.

Procedure Typical Planning Time Risk Key Preoperative Issues

Total Hip Arthroplasty ~1–2 hours Intermediate Anemia, blood loss, VTE risk, functional status, falls, disposition.
Bilateral TKA ~2–4 hours Intermediate with increased physiologic/blood-loss burden Anemia, cardiopulmonary reserve, frailty, transfusion risk, inpatient needs.
Revision TKA ~2–4+ hours Intermediate Higher blood loss, infection history, anemia, longer operative stress.
Revision THA ~2–4+ hours Intermediate Higher blood loss, anemia, frailty, implant complexity, transfusion planning.

Lumbar Fusion Procedures

Procedure Typical Planning Time Risk Key Issues
Single-level TLIF/PLIF ~2–4 hours Intermediate Blood loss, anemia, anticoagulation, prone positioning, postoperative pain.
Multilevel TLIF/PLIF ~4–6+ hours Intermediate with increased physiologic burden Greater anemia/blood loss, fluid shifts, frailty, cardiopulmonary reserve.
Posterolateral fusion ~2–5+ hours Intermediate Depends heavily on levels and revision status.
ALIF ~2–4 hours Intermediate Vascular exposure, blood loss, ileus, thrombotic risk.
XLIF / lateral interbody fusion ~2–4 hours Intermediate Positioning, longer anesthesia, neurologic monitoring.
Revision lumbar fusion ~4–8+ hours Intermediate with potentially high physiologic/blood-loss burden Anemia, transfusion, cardiopulmonary reserve, prolonged prone time.

Complex Spine / Deformity Surgery

Long multilevel deformity correction or extensive revision surgery may remain within an “intermediate” cardiovascular category but should be treated as a major physiologic and blood-loss procedure for optimization purposes.

Pay particular attention to:

  • Preoperative anemia
  • Blood refusal
  • Frailty
  • Cardiopulmonary reserve
  • Anticoagulants/antiplatelets
  • Long prone positioning
  • Pressure injury risk
  • Postoperative respiratory monitoring
  • ICU/HLOC requirements

Spinal Cord Stimulator Procedures

Procedure Typical Planning Time Risk Major Preoperative Issue
SCS trial ~30–90 min Low physiologic risk / high neuraxial bleeding consequence Anticoagulant/antiplatelet timing.
SCS permanent implant ~1–2 hours Low to intermediate Bleeding consequence, infection, device planning.
SCS revision ~1–3 hours Intermediate Scar tissue, infection history, anticoagulation.
Do not equate low cardiovascular procedural risk with low bleeding consequence. Neuraxial hematoma risk makes anticoagulant and antiplatelet timing particularly important.

Pain Management vs Surgeon — SCS/PNS

Role Typical Responsibilities
Pain management physician Patient selection, trials, programming strategy, chronic pain management, some implants/revisions depending on practice.
Surgeon May perform surgical paddle lead implantation, laminectomy-based placement, complex revision or other surgical procedures.
The preoperative risk assessment should be based on the actual planned procedure, not simply the label “SCS.”

Urologic Procedures

Procedure Typical Planning Time Risk
Diagnostic cystoscopy ~15–30 min Low
Ureteroscopy / laser lithotripsy ~45–120 min Low to intermediate
Ureteral stent placement/exchange ~15–45 min Low
TURP ~1–2 hours Intermediate
Unlike routine nonurologic surgery, selected endoscopic urologic procedures with mucosal trauma may require specific urine culture/bacteriuria management.

Procedure Factors That Increase Risk Beyond the Basic Category

  • Revision surgery
  • Multiple operative levels
  • Expected significant blood loss
  • Prolonged anesthesia
  • Prone positioning
  • Beach-chair positioning
  • Major fluid shifts
  • Neuraxial bleeding consequence
  • Implant/hardware placement
  • Anticipated high postoperative opioid requirement
  • Limited postoperative monitoring resources

Expected Blood-Loss Planning

General Blood-Loss Category Examples
Minimal Trigger finger, carpal tunnel, minor arthroscopy, cystoscopy.
Moderate TKA, THA, single-level fusion, shoulder arthroplasty.
Potentially significant Bilateral arthroplasty, multilevel fusion, revision spine, deformity surgery, complex revision arthroplasty.

Procedure-Specific Anemia Significance

A hemoglobin of 10.5 g/dL may have very different implications before a 15-minute trigger-finger release versus a long multilevel revision fusion.

Use expected blood loss to determine how aggressively potentially correctable anemia should be optimized.

Procedure-Specific Anticoagulation Significance

Bleeding consequence matters as much as absolute blood-loss volume.
Spine, neuraxial, SCS and intrathecal procedures require particularly careful anticoagulant/antiplatelet timing even when the overall procedure is not high cardiovascular risk.

Procedure-Specific Pulmonary Significance

Long prone cases, cervical procedures, high postoperative opioid requirements and extended anesthesia may increase respiratory concern in OSA, COPD and frail patients.

Procedure-Specific Frailty Significance

Frailty has greater practical impact when surgery is associated with prolonged mobility limitation, expected inpatient stay, major pain burden or likely need for rehabilitation.

Key Takeaways

  • Low-risk surgery generally carries less than 1% major perioperative cardiac risk.
  • Intermediate-risk surgery generally carries approximately 1–5% risk.
  • Most spine and major orthopedic procedures fall within an intermediate cardiovascular-risk category.
  • Cardiac risk category does not describe blood-loss or neuraxial bleeding consequence.
  • Minor hand and arthroscopic procedures usually have minimal physiologic and blood-loss burden.
  • Total joint replacement deserves attention to anemia, VTE, OSA, diabetes and disposition.
  • Multilevel and revision spine surgery deserves aggressive attention to anemia and cardiopulmonary reserve.
  • SCS/intrathecal procedures can have low physiologic risk but high neuraxial bleeding consequence.
  • Operative duration and revision status materially change risk.
  • Expected blood loss should influence anemia optimization.
  • Use procedure risk together with patient-specific risk to determine final perioperative planning.
