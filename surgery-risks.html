Surgery Taxonomy, OR Duration & Surgical Risk Classification

Practical categorization of common spine, orthopedic and pain procedures

Purpose

Surgical procedure type influences cardiovascular stress, pulmonary risk, blood loss, anesthesia exposure, postoperative pain, thromboembolic risk and disposition planning.

Surgical risk classification should be based on the actual procedure, expected duration, invasiveness, anticipated blood loss and patient-specific risk rather than the surgical specialty alone.

General Surgical Risk Categories

Risk Category Estimated Cardiac Risk General Features
Low <1% Limited physiologic stress, short duration, minimal blood loss and generally rapid recovery.
Intermediate Approximately 1–5% Moderate physiologic stress, longer anesthesia time, greater tissue trauma or expected blood loss.
High >5% Major physiologic stress, substantial blood loss, major vascular shifts or prolonged complex surgery.
Most elective orthopedic and spine procedures fall into the intermediate surgical-risk category, while small hand, superficial and selected pain procedures are often low risk.

Important Note About OR Duration

OR times listed in this chapter are practical estimates only. Actual duration varies by surgeon, number of levels, revision status, complexity, anatomy, implants, positioning, blood loss and intraoperative findings.

OR duration should not be used as a substitute for the surgeon's scheduled operative time when exact timing is available.

SCS: Pain Management vs Surgeon Role

Spinal cord stimulation may involve both pain-management physicians and spine or neurosurgical specialists depending on the procedure and local practice.

Role Common Responsibilities
Pain Management Patient selection, diagnostic evaluation, SCS trial, programming, long-term pain management and many percutaneous implants.
Spine / Neurosurgical Specialist Paddle-lead placement, laminectomy-based implantation, difficult revisions, complex hardware placement or surgical complications.
The preoperative evaluator should identify whether the planned SCS procedure is percutaneous or surgical because bleeding risk, anesthesia requirements and procedural complexity differ.

Spinal Cord Stimulator Bleeding Risk

SCS trials and implants involve the neuraxial space. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet management must follow neuraxial/procedure-specific guidance rather than routine minor-procedure rules.

Aspirin, clopidogrel, DOACs, warfarin and LMWH require deliberate review before SCS procedures because epidural hematoma can cause serious neurologic injury.

Peripheral Nerve Stimulation

PNS is generally less invasive than neuraxial SCS implantation but still requires consideration of bleeding risk, infection risk, device location and sedation requirements.

Determine whether the planned system is temporary or permanent and whether implantation involves deep tissue or a higher-risk anatomic location.

Intrathecal Pump Procedures

Intrathecal drug-delivery systems involve neuraxial access and require careful anticoagulation management.

Antithrombotic interruption must follow neuraxial guidance. Also review the medication being infused because interruption of intrathecal baclofen or other chronic therapy can have serious consequences.

What Makes a Procedure More Risky?

The same named procedure can shift risk categories when complexity increases.

Risk-Increasing Features

  • Revision surgery
  • Multiple operative levels
  • Prolonged anesthesia time
  • Expected major blood loss
  • Prone positioning for prolonged periods
  • Major fluid shifts
  • Combined anterior/posterior approaches
  • Complex instrumentation
  • Severe patient frailty
  • Significant cardiopulmonary disease

Expected Blood Loss Matters

Procedure Type Typical Blood-Loss Concern
Minor hand/pain procedure Usually minimal
Knee arthroscopy Usually minimal
TKA / THA Moderate
Single-level lumbar fusion Moderate
Multilevel or revision spine fusion Moderate to substantial
Complex deformity surgery Potentially substantial
Expected blood loss should influence anemia optimization, type-and-screen planning and perioperative monitoring.

Duration Matters

Longer operations increase exposure to anesthesia, immobility, temperature changes, fluid shifts and hemodynamic stress.

A procedure expected to last 5–8 hours should not be treated as physiologically equivalent to a 30-minute procedure even if both are performed within the same surgical specialty.

Positioning Considerations

Position Potential Concerns
Prone Airway access, pressure injury, venous return, ocular complications and respiratory mechanics.
Beach chair Cerebral perfusion, hypotension and positioning-related risk.
Lateral Pressure injury and ventilation/perfusion changes.
Supine Generally least restrictive but still influenced by obesity and airway disease.

Low-Risk Procedure Examples

Examples commonly considered low risk include:
  • Carpal tunnel release
  • Trigger finger release
  • Minor hand procedures
  • Simple knee arthroscopy
  • Minor foot procedures
  • Diagnostic cystoscopy
  • Most injections and diagnostic pain procedures
  • Selected PNS procedures

Intermediate-Risk Procedure Examples

Examples commonly considered intermediate risk include:
  • Total knee arthroplasty
  • Total hip arthroplasty
  • Total shoulder arthroplasty
  • ACDF
  • Lumbar laminectomy
  • Single-level lumbar fusion
  • SCS permanent implantation
  • Intrathecal pump implantation
  • Selected major foot/ankle procedures
  • Ureteroscopy with lithotripsy

High-Risk or Higher-Complexity Examples

Examples that may approach or enter a high-risk category include:
  • Complex multilevel spinal deformity correction
  • Long-segment revision fusion
  • Combined major anterior/posterior spine procedures
  • Major revision arthroplasty with anticipated substantial blood loss
  • Major vascular surgery
  • Other prolonged procedures with substantial blood loss and hemodynamic stress

Decision Framework

Question Why It Matters
How invasive is the procedure? Determines physiologic stress.
How long will surgery likely take? Longer anesthesia increases cardiopulmonary stress.
How much blood loss is expected? Influences anemia optimization and transfusion planning.
Is this a revision? Revision surgery is often longer and more complex.
Is neuraxial access involved? Changes anticoagulation and antiplatelet requirements.
Will hardware be implanted? Increases consequences of infection and may influence optimization.
What is the patient's physiologic reserve? Surgical risk must be interpreted together with frailty and cardiopulmonary disease.

Key Takeaways

  • Most elective joint replacement and major spine procedures are intermediate risk.
  • Minor hand, arthroscopy and selected pain procedures are often low risk.
  • Complex multilevel and revision spine surgery may approach high risk.
  • Procedure duration and anticipated blood loss modify risk.
  • Revision procedures are generally more physiologically demanding than primary procedures.
  • Neuraxial SCS and intrathecal procedures require special antithrombotic planning.
  • Distinguish percutaneous pain procedures from surgically implanted systems.
  • Use the surgeon's actual anticipated OR time when available.
  • Risk classification should reflect the actual planned operation rather than specialty alone.
