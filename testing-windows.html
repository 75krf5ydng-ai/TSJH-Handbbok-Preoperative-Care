Preoperative Surveillance Testing & Acceptable Testing Windows

Practical guidance for deciding whether prior tests and specialist evaluations remain usable

Home Evaluation Cardiac Testing EKG PFTs Anemia

Purpose

Preoperative testing should be current enough to answer the clinical question, but unnecessary duplication should be avoided. The age of a test alone does not determine whether it remains useful.

The best test window is determined by three questions: Has the patient's condition changed? Is the prior test adequate for the current clinical question? Would repeating the test change perioperative management?

General Rule

Stable chronic disease with unchanged symptoms often does not require repeat testing simply because a prior study has reached an arbitrary age.
New symptoms, disease progression, hospitalization, medication escalation, new abnormal examination findings or a meaningful decline in functional status may invalidate an otherwise recent test.

CBC

CBC timing depends on expected surgical blood loss, known anemia, platelet abnormalities and recent clinical changes.

A stable CBC from the recent preoperative period may be adequate for a clinically unchanged patient. Repeat sooner when there is evidence that the hemoglobin, platelet count or white count may have materially changed.

Reasons to Repeat

  • Recent bleeding
  • Recent transfusion
  • Iron infusion or other active anemia treatment
  • New hematologic symptoms
  • Recent hospitalization
  • Recent infection
  • New chemotherapy or marrow-suppressive therapy

BMP / CMP

Electrolyte and renal testing should reflect the patient's current renal, fluid and medication status.

A BMP that was normal several weeks earlier may no longer be reliable after dehydration, acute illness, medication changes, diuretic escalation or worsening renal disease.

Repeat When Clinically Appropriate

  • Known unstable CKD
  • Recent AKI
  • Recent contrast exposure with renal concern
  • Potassium or sodium abnormality
  • Diuretic dose change
  • ACE inhibitor/ARB medication change with renal concern
  • Poor oral intake or dehydration

Hemoglobin A1c

A1c reflects average glycemic exposure over approximately the preceding 2–3 months.

An A1c obtained within approximately 3 months is usually sufficient for preoperative glycemic assessment unless there has been a major interval change in diabetes management or clinical status.

A current point-of-care or serum glucose may still be necessary because A1c does not identify acute hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia.

EKG

EKG timing should be based on cardiovascular disease, symptoms, procedural risk and institutional requirements.

A recent EKG may remain adequate when the patient has had no new chest pain, syncope, arrhythmia symptoms, heart-failure symptoms or meaningful change in cardiovascular status.
Repeat or obtain a new tracing when there is a new cardiovascular symptom, new arrhythmia concern, recent cardiac event or another indication that the prior tracing may no longer represent the patient's current state.

Echocardiogram

Echocardiography does not have a universal expiration date.

Do not routinely repeat an echocardiogram solely because it is older when ventricular function and symptoms remain clinically stable and the prior study adequately addresses the current question.

Repeat or Reassess When

  • New or worsening dyspnea
  • New heart-failure symptoms
  • New murmur suspicious for significant valve disease
  • Known valve disease with possible progression
  • Suspected new ventricular dysfunction
  • Meaningful change in functional status

Moderate-to-Severe Valvular Disease

Patients with known significant valvular disease require more structured surveillance than patients with normal or trivial findings.

For moderate-to-severe valvular disease, confirm that specialist evaluation and echocardiographic information are sufficiently current to establish present disease severity.

Under TSJH guidance, moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis requires cardiovascular evaluation within approximately 12 months.

Aortic Stenosis Surveillance

The need for repeat echocardiography depends on severity, prior study date, symptoms and evidence of progression.

Moderate-to-severe or symptomatic aortic stenosis requires cardiovascular review before elective surgery. Severe AS is outside routine TSJH elective parameters.

Severe AS includes findings such as valve area less than 1.0 cm², mean gradient greater than 40 mmHg or peak velocity greater than 4.0 m/s.

Stress Testing

Stress tests do not have a universal automatic expiration date.

A previous reassuring stress test may remain useful when the patient is asymptomatic, functional status is stable and no new high-risk cardiac findings have emerged.
Repeat ischemic testing should be considered when symptoms, functional capacity or cardiovascular risk status have meaningfully changed and when the result would alter management.

Coronary Angiography / Heart Catheterization

A prior diagnostic coronary angiogram does not need routine repetition simply because another elective surgery is planned.

Review whether the prior study showed obstructive disease, whether PCI was performed, whether symptoms have changed and whether the patient has had any intervening cardiac events.

Cardiology Evaluation

Specialist evaluations should be recent enough to reflect the current state of the disease being managed.

Clinical Situation Practical Approach
Stable CAD Use the most recent clinically meaningful cardiology assessment when no symptoms or disease progression have occurred.
HFrEF / ischemic cardiomyopathy Under TSJH criteria, cardiology evaluation within approximately 12 months is part of the pathway for selected stable patients with EF ≥35%.
Moderate-to-severe valve disease Confirm recent cardiovascular assessment, commonly within 12 months or sooner if symptoms changed.
New cardiac symptoms Prior stable cardiology evaluation no longer substitutes for reassessment.

Electrophysiology Evaluation

EP evaluation is most relevant for patients with rhythm disease, ablation history, implanted devices or complex anticoagulation related to arrhythmia treatment.

A stable patient does not require a new EP visit solely because surgery is planned unless device status, anticoagulation or rhythm control requires clarification.

Pacemaker / ICD / CRT Interrogation

Device interrogation should be recent enough to confirm device function, battery status and pacing dependence for the planned procedure.

Device Situation Review
Pacemaker Determine recent interrogation, dependence and electrocautery risk.
ICD Confirm device status and perioperative tachytherapy plan when relevant.
CRT Review function as part of the patient's heart-failure/device plan.
If device status cannot be established or the patient has symptoms suggesting device malfunction, obtain device or EP evaluation before elective surgery.

Watchman / Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion

The key timing issue is not simply the age of prior imaging but whether the patient has completed the required post-implant antithrombotic pathway.

Recent Watchman implantation requires review of implantation date, post-procedure imaging, current anticoagulation or antiplatelet regimen and cardiology/EP guidance before therapy is interrupted.

Recent Cardiac Ablation

After atrial fibrillation or other cardiac ablation, determine whether a period of uninterrupted anticoagulation is still required.

Under institutional quick-reference guidance, oral anticoagulation may need to remain uninterrupted for approximately 2–4 weeks before elective interruption is considered.

BNP / NT-proBNP

BNP or NT-proBNP should reflect the current preoperative risk assessment and is not intended as a long-term surveillance test.

Marker TSJH Threshold Action
BNP ≥92 pg/mL Postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours
NT-proBNP ≥300 ng/L Postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours
BNP screening. Obtain BNP because of age greater than 65 years and RCRI score of 1. If BNP is 92 pg/mL or greater, implement the TSJH postoperative cardiac-surveillance protocol with postoperative EKG and daily troponins for 48–72 hours.

Pulmonary Function Tests

PFTs should be repeated based on clinical change rather than on a universal expiration date.

Stable, well-characterized COPD or asthma generally does not require repeat PFTs solely because surgery is planned.

Repeat When

  • Respiratory symptoms have significantly changed
  • Disease severity is uncertain
  • New oxygen requirement has developed
  • Pulmonology requests reassessment
  • Results may change the appropriate surgical setting

Pulmonology Evaluation

Stable pulmonary disease does not require routine repeat specialist visits if sufficient recent information is available and the patient remains at baseline.

New oxygen requirement, exacerbation, chronic hypercapnia, pulmonary fibrosis, significant pulmonary hypertension or worsening dyspnea should prompt updated evaluation.

Sleep Study

A prior diagnostic sleep study does not expire simply because it is old.

For established OSA, the important preoperative issues are current severity when known, PAP use or intolerance, symptoms and postoperative respiratory planning.

Repeat sleep testing may be considered when there has been major weight change, significant clinical progression, treatment failure or another sleep-medicine indication.

PAP Compliance Data

Formal compliance downloads are not required for every surgical patient, but recent device-use information may be useful in selected high-risk OSA patients.

If the patient is intolerant of PAP or has stopped using it, document this clearly and establish postoperative continuous pulse-oximetry and respiratory support planning.

Chest X-Ray

Chest radiography does not have a routine preoperative validity interval because it should not be obtained solely as a screening test in asymptomatic patients.

A chest X-ray should be obtained when there is a clinical indication such as new respiratory symptoms, suspected pneumonia, unexplained hypoxemia or a specific cardiopulmonary question.

Carotid Imaging

Routine repeat carotid ultrasound is not indicated solely because elective surgery is planned.

Reassess when there are new neurologic symptoms, a specialist-directed surveillance plan or another clinically meaningful indication.

Neurology Evaluation

A stable chronic neurologic condition may not require updated neurology evaluation before every elective operation.

Recent stroke/TIA, new focal symptoms, uncontrolled seizures or progressive neurologic disease require updated assessment regardless of how recently the patient was previously seen.

Stroke / TIA Records

The key issue is the date of the cerebrovascular event rather than the age of the neurology note.

Document event date, residual deficits, secondary-prevention therapy, current neurologic stability and perioperative antithrombotic plan.
TSJH anesthesia may prefer approximately one year after stroke when feasible, with a minimum interval commonly considered around 8–9 months after individualized review.

Renal Function

Kidney function testing should be recent enough to reflect current volume status, medication exposure and disease stability.

Repeat renal testing sooner when the patient has recent AKI, dialysis, dehydration, medication changes, contrast exposure, hyperkalemia or another reason to suspect interval deterioration.

Dialysis Patients

Dialysis patients require current electrolyte and volume assessment near the operative date.

A routine laboratory panel from several weeks earlier is not sufficient to establish current potassium and volume status in a dialysis patient.

Iron Studies

Ferritin and transferrin saturation should be recent enough to represent the current anemia state, particularly when treatment has begun.

Repeat iron studies based on the treatment interval and clinical need rather than according to a fixed universal preoperative expiration date.

Urinalysis and Urine Culture

Do not repeat urine testing merely to obtain a newer negative result in an asymptomatic patient undergoing nonurologic surgery.

For nonurologic spine and joint procedures, repeat testing should be driven by symptoms or a procedure-specific requirement, not asymptomatic bacteriuria alone.

Specialist Evaluation Windows: Practical Summary

Specialty When Prior Evaluation May Remain Adequate When Updated Evaluation Is Needed
Cardiology Stable disease, adequate recent testing, no new symptoms. New ischemic symptoms, HF decompensation, significant valve concern, new arrhythmia or disease-specific TSJH requirement.
Electrophysiology Stable device/rhythm status with adequate interrogation information. Recent ablation, device concern, anticoagulation question or rhythm instability.
Pulmonology Stable chronic disease at baseline. New oxygen requirement, exacerbation, fibrosis, hypercapnia, pulmonary hypertension or unclear surgical setting.
Neurology Stable chronic neurologic disease. Recent stroke/TIA, new deficit, seizure instability or progression.
Nephrology Stable CKD without complex perioperative issue. Dialysis, AKI, severe electrolyte abnormality or advanced unstable CKD.
Hematology Stable known disorder with an established plan. New severe anemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, thrombosis or complex anticoagulation issue.

When a “Recent” Test Is No Longer Good Enough

Even a very recent test may need to be reconsidered after:
  • Emergency department visit
  • Hospital admission
  • New chest pain
  • New dyspnea
  • Syncope
  • Heart-failure exacerbation
  • Stroke or TIA
  • New arrhythmia
  • Respiratory exacerbation
  • Acute kidney injury
  • Major bleeding
  • Medication changes likely to alter laboratory status

When an “Old” Test May Still Be Adequate

A prior study may remain useful when:
  • The disease is clinically stable
  • Symptoms are unchanged
  • Functional capacity is unchanged
  • No hospitalization or major event has occurred
  • The prior test directly answers the present question
  • No institutional disease-specific surveillance interval has been exceeded

Concise Physician Summary Example

Prior diagnostic and specialty records reviewed. Available testing remains clinically applicable because the patient has had no meaningful interval change in symptoms, functional capacity or disease status. Repeat testing is not indicated solely based on the age of the prior study. Additional testing will be obtained only if required by disease-specific institutional criteria or if the patient's clinical condition changes.

Decision Framework

Question Action
What clinical question does the prior test answer? Determine whether it is relevant to the current operation.
Has the patient's condition changed? If yes, reassess whether repeat testing is needed.
Does the disease have a TSJH-specific surveillance requirement? Apply the disease-specific interval.
Would a newer test change management? If no, avoid unnecessary repetition.
Was there a recent hospitalization or acute event? Reassess even if previous tests were performed recently.
Is specialist guidance still clinically current? Use it when disease remains stable; update when status changes.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not use arbitrary expiration dates for every test.
  • Clinical stability is more important than calendar age alone.
  • Recent acute illness can make a recent test obsolete.
  • A stable old test may still answer the current perioperative question.
  • Use disease-specific TSJH requirements when they exist.
  • Moderate-to-severe valve disease requires structured surveillance.
  • Stable stress testing does not need automatic repetition.
  • PFTs and sleep studies should be repeated for clinical indications, not simply for surgery.
  • Dialysis and unstable renal patients need laboratory data close enough to surgery to reflect current status.
  • A1c generally reflects approximately 3 months of glycemic control.
  • Do not repeat urine studies merely to demonstrate a negative result in asymptomatic nonurologic patients.
  • Document why prior testing remains adequate or why updated testing is required.
