Thyroid Disease, Adrenal Insufficiency & Chronic Steroid Management

Hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, HPA-axis suppression and perioperative steroid planning

Purpose

Endocrine disease can influence hemodynamic stability, metabolism, thermoregulation, cardiovascular risk, glucose control and the physiologic response to surgical stress.

The preoperative objective is to identify clinically important thyroid dysfunction, recognize patients at risk for adrenal insufficiency, continue essential chronic hormone therapy and determine whether stress-dose steroid coverage is needed.

Hypothyroidism

Hypothyroidism ranges from mild biochemical disease to severe clinical decompensation.

Potential Symptoms

  • Fatigue
  • Cold intolerance
  • Constipation
  • Weight gain
  • Bradycardia
  • Dry skin
  • Slowed cognition
  • Muscle weakness
Mild or moderate stable hypothyroidism generally does not automatically require postponement of elective surgery when the patient is clinically stable.

Severe Hypothyroidism

Elective surgery should be delayed when severe hypothyroidism is associated with clinically important decompensation such as:

Altered mental status
Hypothermia
Marked bradycardia
Significant hypotension
Heart failure
Severe hyponatremia
Hypoventilation
Concern for myxedema coma

Myxedema Coma

Myxedema coma is a medical emergency and is incompatible with elective surgery. Urgent endocrine and critical-care management is required.

TSH Interpretation

TSH should be interpreted together with free T4 when clinically indicated.

Pattern Possible Interpretation
High TSH + low free T4 Overt primary hypothyroidism
High TSH + normal free T4 Subclinical hypothyroidism
Low TSH + high free T4/T3 Overt hyperthyroidism
Low TSH + normal free T4/T3 Possible subclinical hyperthyroidism

Do Not Order Thyroid Tests Routinely

Routine thyroid screening is not required before every elective surgery. Obtain testing when thyroid disease is known, symptoms suggest dysfunction, or there is another clinical indication.

Levothyroxine

Chronic levothyroxine therapy should generally be continued perioperatively.

Because levothyroxine has a long half-life, a brief interruption is usually not clinically important, but routine unnecessary discontinuation should be avoided.

Hyperthyroidism

Hyperthyroidism increases cardiovascular and metabolic stress and may increase risk for tachyarrhythmia, heart failure and thyroid storm.

Potential Symptoms

  • Tachycardia
  • Palpitations
  • Tremor
  • Heat intolerance
  • Weight loss
  • Anxiety
  • Diarrhea
  • Hyperreflexia
  • Atrial fibrillation

Overt Uncontrolled Hyperthyroidism

Elective surgery should generally be postponed in patients with overt, clinically significant uncontrolled hyperthyroidism because of risk for thyroid storm and cardiovascular instability.

Further Review May Include

  • TSH
  • Free T4
  • Total/free T3 when indicated
  • Heart rate/rhythm
  • Beta-blocker use
  • Endocrinology recommendations

Thyroid Storm

Thyroid storm is a medical emergency characterized by severe thyrotoxicosis with systemic decompensation. Elective surgery must not proceed.

Possible Findings

  • High fever
  • Marked tachycardia
  • Arrhythmia
  • Heart failure
  • Agitation/delirium
  • GI symptoms
  • Hemodynamic instability

Beta Blockers in Hyperthyroidism

Beta blockers are commonly used for symptomatic control of tachycardia and adrenergic symptoms.

Chronic beta-blocker therapy should generally be continued perioperatively unless contraindicated.

Antithyroid Medications

Examples

  • Methimazole
  • Propylthiouracil
Patients taking antithyroid medication should generally continue therapy unless their treating clinician provides a specific alternative plan.

Adrenal Insufficiency

Adrenal insufficiency may be primary, secondary or related to chronic exogenous glucocorticoid exposure.

Potential Symptoms

  • Fatigue
  • Weakness
  • Weight loss
  • Hypotension
  • Orthostasis
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Abdominal pain
  • Hyponatremia
  • Hyperkalemia in primary adrenal insufficiency

Adrenal Crisis

Adrenal crisis is a medical emergency. Suspected crisis with hypotension, vomiting, electrolyte abnormalities or shock requires urgent treatment and is incompatible with elective surgery.

Chronic Steroid Exposure

Long-term exogenous glucocorticoids may suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary- adrenal axis.

Review

  • Medication
  • Daily dose
  • Duration
  • Recent taper
  • Intermittent high-dose courses
  • Reason for therapy
  • Clinical evidence of adrenal suppression

Examples of Systemic Glucocorticoids

  • Prednisone
  • Prednisolone
  • Methylprednisolone
  • Dexamethasone
  • Hydrocortisone

Not Every Steroid Patient Needs Large “Stress Doses”

Modern perioperative practice generally favors continuing the patient's usual glucocorticoid therapy and providing supplemental stress dosing when the degree of HPA-axis suppression and surgical stress justify it, rather than giving very large empiric doses to every steroid-exposed patient.
Exact supplementation should follow anesthesia, endocrinology and facility protocol. Avoid both unnecessary supraphysiologic dosing and omission of coverage in a truly adrenal-suppressed patient.

Assess Risk of HPA-Axis Suppression

Risk depends on steroid potency, dose, duration and timing.

Brief low-dose exposure is less likely to produce clinically significant suppression than prolonged daily systemic glucocorticoid therapy.

When exposure history is uncertain or clinically complex, consult anesthesia or endocrinology rather than guessing.

Inhaled Steroids

Most patients using standard inhaled corticosteroid therapy for asthma/COPD do not require perioperative stress-dose steroids solely because they use an inhaler.

Very high-dose or prolonged inhaled steroid exposure can occasionally contribute to systemic adrenal suppression. Review the total steroid burden when there is clinical concern.

Topical and Local Steroids

Topical, intra-articular and other local glucocorticoids usually produce less systemic exposure than chronic oral therapy, but repeated or high-dose administration may still be relevant.

Consider cumulative exposure when the history suggests frequent injections, multiple formulations or symptoms compatible with adrenal suppression.

Recent Steroid Taper

HPA-axis suppression can persist after chronic glucocorticoids have been reduced or stopped.

A patient who recently completed a prolonged steroid course may still be at risk for adrenal insufficiency even if steroids are no longer listed on the current medication list.

Surgical Stress Matters

Surgical Stress Examples
Minor Small superficial procedures, selected hand or minor pain procedures.
Moderate Many arthroplasty, spine decompression or intermediate procedures.
Major Complex multilevel spine surgery, major revision procedures or operations with substantial physiologic stress.
Supplemental glucocorticoid requirements, when indicated, increase with the degree of surgical stress.

Chronic Steroids and Glucose

Glucocorticoids may worsen perioperative hyperglycemia.

In steroid-treated diabetic patients, establish a glucose-monitoring and medication plan in addition to the adrenal-risk assessment.

Chronic Steroids and Infection / Wound Healing

Long-term systemic steroids may increase infection risk and impair tissue or bone healing.

Do not abruptly stop essential steroid therapy to reduce infection risk. Instead, assess the indication, dose and overall surgical risk.

Chronic Steroids and Osteoporosis

Long-term glucocorticoid exposure may contribute to osteoporosis and impaired bone quality.

This may be particularly relevant in spine fusion or other procedures dependent on bone quality and healing. Surgeon-directed bone-health evaluation may be appropriate.

Pheochromocytoma

Known or suspected pheochromocytoma requires specialist management and should not be approached as routine hypertension before elective surgery.

Patients with episodic severe hypertension, headache, diaphoresis and palpitations plus a known adrenal mass or strong clinical suspicion require appropriate endocrine evaluation.

Primary Hyperaldosteronism

Primary aldosteronism may present with resistant hypertension and hypokalemia.

Extensive secondary-hypertension workup does not need to be completed in every stable patient before surgery, but significant hypokalemia or uncontrolled hypertension should be addressed.

Hyponatremia and Endocrine Disease

Hyponatremia can occur with adrenal insufficiency or severe hypothyroidism.

When significant hyponatremia is accompanied by endocrine symptoms or unexplained hypotension, consider an endocrine cause rather than treating the sodium value in isolation.

When Endocrinology Consultation May Be Appropriate

Consider endocrinology for:

Overt uncontrolled hyperthyroidism
Severe hypothyroidism
Known adrenal insufficiency without a stress-dose plan
Uncertain HPA-axis suppression after prolonged steroid use
Recent adrenal crisis
Suspected pheochromocytoma
Complex endocrine disease likely to affect surgical timing
Other difficult endocrine management

When Anesthesia Review Is Appropriate

Examples

  • Known adrenal insufficiency
  • Chronic high-dose systemic steroid therapy
  • Uncertain stress-dose requirement
  • Overt hyperthyroidism
  • Severe hypothyroidism
  • Complex endocrine hemodynamic risk

When to Consider Postponing Elective Surgery

Consider postponement for:

Thyroid storm
Overt uncontrolled hyperthyroidism
Myxedema coma
Severe decompensated hypothyroidism
Adrenal crisis
Unresolved significant adrenal insufficiency without a perioperative plan
Other acute endocrine instability

Findings That Do Not Automatically Require Delay

When clinically stable, these do not automatically require postponement:

Stable treated hypothyroidism
Mild subclinical hypothyroidism
Stable thyroid replacement therapy
Stable chronic steroid use with an appropriate perioperative plan
Known adrenal insufficiency with appropriate replacement and stress-dose planning

Decision Framework

Question Action
Is thyroid disease clinically stable? Continue therapy and avoid unnecessary repeat testing if stable.
Is overt hyperthyroidism present? Optimize before elective surgery.
Are severe hypothyroid features present? Delay and treat decompensation.
Does the patient use chronic systemic steroids? Assess dose, duration and HPA-axis suppression risk.
Is adrenal insufficiency known? Continue baseline replacement and establish stress-dose plan.
Is steroid history uncertain? Escalate to anesthesia/endocrinology when clinically meaningful.
Is there acute endocrine instability? Do not proceed with routine elective surgery.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not routinely screen every surgical patient with TSH.
  • Stable treated hypothyroidism usually does not require surgical delay.
  • Severe decompensated hypothyroidism and myxedema coma require treatment before elective surgery.
  • Overt uncontrolled hyperthyroidism should be optimized before elective surgery.
  • Thyroid storm is a medical emergency.
  • Continue chronic thyroid replacement therapy.
  • Known adrenal insufficiency requires a perioperative glucocorticoid plan.
  • Chronic steroid exposure may suppress the HPA axis.
  • Not every steroid-exposed patient requires large empiric stress doses.
  • Do not abruptly stop chronic essential glucocorticoid therapy.
  • Recent steroid taper may still leave residual adrenal suppression.
  • Stress-dose requirements depend partly on surgical stress.
  • Endocrinology or anesthesia review is appropriate when suppression risk or endocrine stability is unclear.
